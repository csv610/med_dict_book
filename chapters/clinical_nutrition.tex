\chapter{Clinical Nutrition \& Metabolic Medicine}

\medterm{Bariatric Surgery}-- Surgical procedures performed on the gastrointestinal tract to induce significant, sustained weight loss and improve metabolic comorbidities (type 2 diabetes, hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea, dyslipidemia) in patients with severe obesity ($\text{BMI} \ge 40\text{ kg/m}^2$, or $\text{BMI} \ge 35\text{ kg/m}^2$ with obesity-related conditions). Primary procedures: Roux-en-Y Gastric Bypass (RYGB—combined restrictive and malabsorptive), Sleeve Gastrectomy (restrictive removal of ~80\% of the greater curvature), and Adjustable Gastric Banding. Long-term nutritional complications require mandatory lifelong micronutrient monitoring and supplementation (vitamin $\text{B}_{12}$, iron, calcium, vitamin D, folate, thiamine).

\medterm{Beriberi}-- Nutritional deficiency disorder resulting from severe lack of thiamine (vitamin $\text{B}_1$), essential for pyruvate dehydrogenase and $\alpha$-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase in carbohydrate metabolism. Types: Wet Beriberi (cardiovascular manifestation with high-output heart failure, peripheral vasodilation, cardiomegaly, and dependent edema) and Dry Beriberi (neurological manifestation with symmetrical peripheral neuropathy, sensory loss, muscle wasting, and diminished deep tendon reflexes). Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome represents the central nervous system manifestation (ocular abnormalities, ataxia, confusion, and anterograde amnesia), commonly associated with chronic alcoholism. Treated with immediate intravenous thiamine supplementation prior to glucose administration.

\medterm{Body Mass Index (BMI)}-- Anthropometric measure defined as body mass divided by the square of body height ($\text{kg/m}^2$). WHO classification for adults: Underweight ($<18.5$), Normal weight ($18.5\text{--}24.9$), Overweight ($25.0\text{--}29.9$), Class I Obesity ($30.0\text{--}34.9$), Class II Obesity ($35.0\text{--}39.9$), and Class III / Severe Obesity ($\ge 40$). Serves as a population-level indicator of metabolic disease risk, though limited by inability to distinguish visceral fat from muscle mass or bone density.

\medterm{Enteral Nutrition}-- Delivery of nutritionally complete liquid formulas directly into the stomach or small intestine via a tube, indicated for patients with a functional gastrointestinal tract who cannot meet their nutritional requirements orally (e.g., severe dysphagia, mechanical ventilation, head/neck cancer, altered consciousness). Routes: Nasogastric (NG) tube, Nasojejunal (NJ) tube, Percutaneous Endoscopic Gastrostomy (PEG), or Percutaneous Endoscopic Jejunostomy (PEJ). Preferred over parenteral nutrition due to preservation of gut mucosal integrity, prevention of bacterial translocation, lower infection rates, and reduced cost.

\medterm{Essential Fatty Acid Deficiency (EFAD)}-- Rare clinical deficiency resulting from inadequate dietary intake of essential polyunsaturated fatty acids: linoleic acid (omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3). Occurs primarily in patients receiving long-term fat-free parenteral nutrition or severe fat malabsorption syndromes. Clinical features: dry scaly dermatitis, alopecia, impaired wound healing, thrombocytopenia, growth retardation, and fatty liver. Diagnosed by an elevated plasma triene-to-tetraene ratio ($>0.2$). Prevented and treated by administering intravenous lipid emulsions.

\medterm{Kwashiorkor}-- Form of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) characterized by severe protein deficiency despite adequate or borderline caloric intake (classic "edematous malnutrition"). Typically affects young children after weaning onto a low-protein, starchy carbohydrate diet. Key clinical features: generalized pitting edema (starting in feet and legs), moon face, abdominal distension ("pot belly" due to hepatomegaly from fatty liver infiltration), sparse reddish-orange hair, dry flaky-paint skin lesions, lethargy, loss of appetite, and hypoalbuminemia. High mortality if unmanaged; treated with cautious, phased nutritional rehabilitation (F-75/F-100 therapeutic milk).

\medterm{Malnutrition (Undernutrition)}-- Cellular imbalance between the supply of nutrients and energy and the body's demand for them to ensure growth, maintenance, and specific functions. Encompasses protein-energy malnutrition (Kwashiorkor, Marasmus) and micronutrient deficiencies (hidden hunger). Causes: inadequate food intake, poverty, severe acute/chronic illness (cancer cachexia, sepsis, inflammatory bowel disease), and malabsorption. Consequences: impaired immune function, delayed wound healing, muscle wasting, increased hospital length of stay, and elevated mortality.

\medterm{Marasmus}-- Form of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) resulting from a chronic, severe deficiency of both total calories and protein ("non-edematous malnutrition" or severe wasting). Affects infants under 1 year of age. Key clinical features: severe muscle wasting and loss of subcutaneous fat, giving the child an "old man" or wizened facial appearance, prominent ribs, thin loose skin folds ("baggy pants" gluteal appearance), severe stunting, and ravenous hunger. Unlike kwashiorkor, there is no edema, hair color changes, or fatty liver infiltration.

\medterm{Metabolic Syndrome}-- Cluster of interconnected physiological, biochemical, and metabolic abnormalities that significantly increase the risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease. Diagnostic criteria (ATP III / NCEP—$\ge 3$ required): (1) Abdominal obesity (waist circumference $>102\text{ cm}$ in men, $>88\text{ cm}$ in women), (2) Elevated triglycerides ($\ge 150\text{ mg/dL}$), (3) Reduced HDL cholesterol ($<40\text{ mg/dL}$ in men, $<50\text{ mg/dL}$ in women), (4) Elevated blood pressure ($\ge 130/85\text{ mmHg}$ or on antihypertensive therapy), and (5) Elevated fasting glucose ($\ge 100\text{ mg/dL}$ or on antidiabetic therapy). Insulin resistance is the underlying pathophysiological mechanism.

\medterm{Micronutrient Deficiencies}-- Inadequate physiological availability of essential vitamins and trace minerals required in small quantities for enzymatic catalysis, hormone synthesis, cellular metabolism, and immune function. Major global deficiencies: Vitamin A (leading cause of preventable childhood blindness), Iron (microcytic anemia), Iodine (goiter, cretinism), Zinc (impaired growth, immune dysfunction, dermatitis), and Vitamin D (rickets, osteomalacia). Affects over 2 billion people worldwide ("hidden hunger"), addressed through dietary diversification, biofortification, industrial food fortification, and target supplementation.

\medterm{Parenteral Nutrition (Total Parenteral Nutrition / TPN)}-- Intravenous administration of complete nutritional solutions (hypertonic dextrose, amino acids, lipid emulsions, electrolytes, trace elements, and vitamins) directly into the systemic circulation, bypassing the gastrointestinal tract. Indicated when the GI tract is non-functional, inaccessible, or requires complete rest (e.g., short bowel syndrome, severe acute pancreatitis, high-output enterocutaneous fistulas, bowel obstruction). Central venous access (PICC or central line) is required for TPN to avoid thrombophlebitis. Complications: line sepsis, refeeding syndrome, fatty liver/cholestasis, metabolic bone disease, and hyper/hypoglycemia.

\medterm{Pellagra}-- Systemic nutritional deficiency disease resulting from a lack of niacin (vitamin $\text{B}_3$) or its precursor amino acid, tryptophan. Historically prevalent in populations relying heavily on untreated corn/maize diets. Classic triad (the "3 Ds"): (1) Dermatitis (photosensitive, symmetrical, hyperpigmented scaly rash with Casal collar around neck), (2) Diarrhea (stomatitis, glossitis, nausea, enteritis), and (3) Dementia (insomnia, fatigue, confusion, depression, psychosis, progressing to death—the 4th "D"). Treated with oral nicotinamide supplementation.

\medterm{Refeeding Syndrome}-- Potentially fatal metabolic complication occurring when aggressive nutritional reintroduction (oral, enteral, or parenteral) is initiated in severely malnourished, starved, or anorexic patients. Pathophysiology: carbohydrate loading causes insulin release, driving cellular uptake of glucose along with phosphate, potassium, and magnesium. Rapid intracellular shift leads to severe hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, and hypomagnesemia, resulting in cardiac arrhythmias, heart failure, respiratory failure, seizures, delirium, and sudden death. Prevention: "start low and go slow" feeding protocols with immediate thiamine supplementation and close monitoring/repletion of serum electrolytes.

\medterm{Rickets \& Osteomalacia}-- Defective bone mineralization resulting from inadequate active vitamin D, calcium, or phosphate. Rickets occurs in growing children before epiphyseal plate closure, presenting with delayed fontanelle closure, frontal bossing, rachitic rosary (beading of costochondral junctions), Harrison groove, bowing of legs (genu varum), and widened epiphyses. Osteomalacia occurs in adults after epiphyseal closure, presenting with diffuse bone pain, muscle weakness, waddling gait, and pseudofractures (Looser zones) on X-rays. Treated with ergocalciferol / cholecalciferol and calcium supplementation.

\medterm{Scurvy}-- Nutritional deficiency disease caused by severe lack of vitamin C (ascorbic acid), an essential cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase enzymes required for stable collagen triple-helix cross-linking. Defective collagen synthesis causes capillary fragility, impaired wound healing, and bone abnormalities. Clinical features: perifollicular hemorrhages, corkscrew hairs, swollen bleeding gums, loose teeth, petechiae, ecchymoses, hemarthrosis, anemia, fatigue, and poor wound healing. Prevented and rapidly cured with oral vitamin C (ascorbic acid) supplementation.

\medterm{Trace Elements (Zinc, Copper, Selenium, Chromium)}-- Essential inorganic minerals required in microgram to milligram quantities for catalytic enzyme activity and cellular regulation. Zinc: cofactor for $>300$ enzymes (alkaline phosphatase, RNA polymerase); deficiency causes acrodermatitis enteropathica, delayed wound healing, hypogeusia, and growth failure. Copper: cofactor for cytochrome c oxidase, ceruloplasmin, superoxide dismutase; deficiency causes microcytic anemia, neutropenia, and neurodegeneration. Selenium: component of glutathione peroxidase; deficiency causes Keshan disease (cardiomyopathy). Chromium: enhances insulin action; deficiency causes glucose intolerance.

\medterm{Vitamin A Deficiency (Xerophthalmia)}-- Major nutritional cause of preventable blindness in developing countries. Vitamin A (retinol) is essential for rhodopsin synthesis in retinal rod cells and epithelial tissue integrity. Early manifestation: nyctalopia (night blindness). Progressive manifestations (Xerophthalmia spectrum): conjunctival xerosis, Bitot spots (foamy triangular plaques on bulbar conjunctiva), corneal xerosis, and corneal ulceration/keratomalacia leading to irreversible blindness. Systemic effects: squamous metaplasia of respiratory/urinary epithelia, impaired immunity, and increased child mortality from measles and diarrhea.

\medterm{Vitamin B12 Deficiency (Pernicious Anemia)}-- Nutritional or malabsorptive deficiency of cobalamin (vitamin $\text{B}_{12}$), essential for DNA synthesis (methionine synthase) and fatty acid metabolism (methylmalonyl-CoA mutase). Causes: pernicious anemia (autoimmune destruction of parietal cells / intrinsic factor deficiency), strict vegan diet, gastrectomy, terminal ileal resection, or Crohn disease. Clinical manifestations: megaloblastic anemia (hypersegmented neutrophils, elevated MCV), glossitis ("beefy red tongue"), and neurological impairment—Subacute Combined Degeneration (SCD) of the spinal cord (demyelination of posterior and lateral columns causing loss of vibration/proprioception, spastic paraparesis, ataxia).

\medterm{Vitamin K Deficiency (Coagulopathy)}-- Lack of vitamin K (phylloquinone/menaquinone), an essential cofactor for gamma-glutamyl carboxylase, which converts precursor proteins into active clotting factors II, VII, IX, X, and proteins C and S. Causes: broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy (disrupts gut flora synthesis), severe fat malabsorption (biliary obstruction, celiac disease), or malnutrition. Clinical presentation: bleeding diathesis (ecchymoses, epistaxis, mucosal bleeding, hematuria, prolonged PT/INR). Newborns are uniquely vulnerable (Hemorrhagic Disease of the Newborn / Vitamin K Deficiency Bleeding) due to poor placental transfer and sterile gut; prevented by routine IM vitamin K administration at birth.
