\chapter{Forensic Medicine \& Medical Ethics}

\medterm{Adipocere (Grave Wax)}-- Waxy, grayish-white substance formed postmortem in warm, moist, anaerobic environments (water, damp soil) by the hydrolysis and hydrogenation of body fat into free fatty acids; retards putrefaction and preserves anatomical details for months to years.

\medterm{Admissibility}-- The legal standard governing whether evidence may be received and weighed by a court. The Daubert standard requires expert scientific testimony to rest on testable methodology, peer review, a known error rate, and general acceptance, with the judge as gatekeeper; some state jurisdictions retain the older Frye standard. Improperly admitted evidence may constitute reversible error on appeal.

\medterm{Air Embolism}-- Presence of gas in the venous or arterial circulation, often fatal if massive. Causes: iatrogenic (central line insertion, surgery—especially neurosurgery in sitting position, dialysis, pressurized infusion), trauma (penetrating neck/chest wounds, diving), and procedures (abortion with irrigation, insufflation). Entry requires a vessel with pressure lower than atmospheric and a tract. Features: sudden cardiovascular collapse, cyanosis, mill-wheel murmur, or cutaneous marbling. Diagnosis: air in heart on CT or at autopsy (air aspiration test for right heart); findings significant in forensic evaluation of iatrogenic death.

\medterm{Algor Mortis}-- Post-mortem cooling of the body following death until it reaches ambient environmental temperature. Serves as one of the physical estimators of the post-mortem interval (time since death). Under standard conditions ($20^\circ\text{C}$ ambient temperature), the body cools at a rate of approximately $0.75\text{--}1.0^\circ\text{C}$ ($1.5^\circ\text{F}$) per hour during the first 12 hours, and ~ $0.5^\circ\text{C}$ per hour thereafter. Influenced by ambient temperature, body habitus (obesity slows cooling), clothing, humidity, air movement, and immersion in water. Rectal temperature is recorded using a chemical thermometer.

\medterm{Antemortem Injury}-- Injury inflicted upon a body prior to death, characterized microscopically and macroscopically by physiological tissue responses including hemorrhage, blood clotting, inflammatory cell infiltration, tissue edema, and active healing reactions (differentiating it from postmortem artifact).

\medterm{Asphyxia}-- See emergency_medicine.

\medterm{Autopsy}-- A postmortem examination of a body to determine cause and manner of death, performed by a forensic pathologist (medical examiner/coroner) or, for hospital autopsies, a pathologist. Components: external examination (identification, injuries, scars, tattoos), internal examination (organ weights, findings), histology, toxicology, microbiology, and imaging. Forensic autopsies are conducted when death is sudden, unnatural, unexplained, or violent; they document injuries, collect evidence (bullets, swabs), and issue a report addressing cause of death and manner (natural, accident, suicide, homicide, undetermined).

\medterm{Autopsy (Medicolegal)}-- Systematic external examination and surgical dissection of a deceased body performed under legal authority by a forensic pathologist to determine the Cause, Manner, and Mechanism of Death, document injuries, collect evidence, and assist judicial investigations.

\medterm{Autopsy (Post-Mortem Examination)}-- Specialized surgical procedure performed on a deceased body to determine the cause of death, mechanism of death, manner of death (natural, accidental, suicide, homicide, or undetermined), state of health prior to death, and to document injuries or disease processes. Types: Clinical Autopsy (hospital-based, requiring family consent, to study disease processes and audit medical care) and Forensic (Medico-Legal) Autopsy (ordered by a coroner, medical examiner, or magistrate under statutory authority, without requiring family consent, in cases of sudden, violent, suspicious, or unexplained death). Procedure involves external examination, full internal dissection of cranial, thoracic, and abdominal cavities, histopathology, toxicological sampling, and formal report generation.

\medterm{Ballistics}-- The science of the motion and behavior of projectiles (bullets, shot) and their effects on tissue. Forensic ballistics includes internal (within the firearm), external (in flight), and terminal (on target) ballistics. Firearm identification examines rifling marks (grooves/lands), firing pin impressions, and extractor/ejector marks to match bullets and cartridge cases to a specific weapon. Wound assessment (entry vs. exit, range—contact, close, intermediate, distant—and bullet path) informs reconstruction. Uses: gunshot wound interpretation, range estimation (powder stippling, soot), and weapon identification.

\medterm{Battered Child Syndrome}-- A pattern of repeated, non-accidental physical injury inflicted on a child by a caregiver, formally described by Kempe in 1962. Features include fractures of differing ages, subdural and retinal hemorrhages, inconsistent histories, and delayed presentation; mandatory reporting is required in most jurisdictions. Forensic documentation with photographs, skeletal survey, and contemporaneous history is essential for protection and prosecution.

\medterm{Beneficence and Non-Maleficence}-- Core biomedical ethical principles guiding clinical practice and medical research. Beneficence requires healthcare providers to act in the best interest of the patient, taking positive steps to promote health, prevent harm, and relieve suffering. Non-Maleficence (expressed by the Latin phrase \textit{primum non nocere}—"first, do no harm") obligates practitioners to avoid inflicting intentional harm, unnecessary pain, or undue risk on patients. Ethical dilemmas arise when balancing the potential benefits of a treatment against its inherent risks and side effects.

\medterm{Bioethics}-- Interdisciplinary study of the ethical, legal, philosophical, and social implications of advancements in medicine, biology, healthcare technology, and biotechnology. Key topics: autonomy, informed consent, end-of-life decision-making (euthanasia, advance directives, do-not-resuscitate orders), reproductive technologies (surrogacy, gene editing, IVF), organ transplantation ethics, allocation of scarce healthcare resources, genetic privacy, and clinical trial ethics.

\medterm{Blood Spatter Analysis}-- The forensic interpretation of bloodstain patterns to reconstruct events surrounding a violent incident. Types: passive (drops, pools, flows), transfer (contact patterns), and impact/expirated spatter. Spatter characteristics depend on droplet size, velocity (low—gravity, medium—blunt force, high—gunshot/explosion), angle of impact (elongated shapes), and surface. Analysis helps determine position of victim/assailant, number of blows or shots, movement, and events after injury. Strict protocol and documentation (photography, measurements) are required for court presentation.

\medterm{Body Identification}-- The process of establishing the identity of a deceased person, essential in forensic and mass-disaster contexts. Methods: visual recognition, personal effects, fingerprint comparison, dental records (dental radiographs—best for single comparisons), DNA profiling (STR analysis, mitochondrial DNA for degraded samples), medical/surgical records (implants, scars, serial numbers), and serial-number records (pacemakers, prostheses). Anthropological analysis (skeletal features, age, sex, stature, ancestry) for decomposed or skeletal remains. Multiple methods combined provide certainty.

\medterm{Bruise}-- A contusion: localized hemorrhage into tissues due to blunt force trauma rupturing small blood vessels, with intact skin. Forensically significant for aging injuries: color changes (red/blue at 0-2 days, purple at 2-5 days, green at 5-7 days, yellow at 7-10 days, fading by 2-4 weeks) and pattern recognition (hand, belt, fingertip, bite) may indicate mechanism or abuse. Important variables: age, sex, site (loose skin bruising more readily), disease (bleeding disorders), and medications (anticoagulants). Not all bruises indicate inflicted injury; dating is approximate.

\medterm{Bullet Track}-- The path a projectile traverses through the body, defined by the entry wound, internal course (through organs, vessels, bones), and exit wound (or retained bullet). Forensic interpretation uses the track to determine trajectory (angle and direction of fire), shooter position, and sequence of events; multiple bullets require individual tracking. Ricochet and fragmentation alter tracks. Autopsy documentation includes measurement, X-ray/CT localization, and correlation with clothing damage and scene findings. Track analysis helps reconstruct shooting incidents and validate or refute witness accounts.

\medterm{Blunt Force Trauma}-- Injury produced when a blunt object strikes the body, or the body strikes a blunt surface, without a sharp edge penetrating the skin. Patterns include abrasions, contusions, lacerations with tissue bridging, and fractures; patterned injuries may reproduce the impacting object. Severity ranges from superficial damage to lethal organ rupture or intracranial hemorrhage. Mechanism, force, and direction inform manner-of-death determination.

\medterm{Cadaveric Spasm}-- Rare form of instantaneous post-mortem stiffening of a specific group of voluntary muscles at the exact moment of death, without an intervening period of muscle relaxation. Unlike normal rigor mortis (which develops 2--4 hours post-mortem), cadaveric spasm occurs immediately, trapping objects held in the hand at death (e.g., a weapon in suicide, grass/twigs held by a drowning victim). Pathophysiology: severe physical or emotional stress combined with extreme exhaustion immediately preceding death, leading to rapid ATP depletion. Highly significant in forensic investigations for establishing suicide or struggle.

\medterm{Capacity and Competence}-- Decision-making capacity is a clinical evaluation performed by a physician to determine whether a patient understands the nature, consequences, risks, and alternatives of a specific proposed medical decision at a given time. Competence is a formal legal determination made by a judge regarding an individual's overall legal ability to manage their affairs or make decisions. A patient with capacity has the legal and moral right to grant or refuse consent for medical procedures, even if that refusal leads to harm or death.

\medterm{Carbon Monoxide Poisoning}-- Toxicity from inhalation of carbon monoxide (CO), a colorless, odorless gas binding hemoglobin ~200-300 times more avidly than oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) and reducing oxygen delivery. Sources: fires, faulty heaters, engine exhaust, suicide by car exhaust. Features: headache, nausea, cherry-red skin/lips (later), and loss of consciousness; severe exposure fatal. Diagnosis: COHb level (postmortem blood), classical pink lividity, and cherry-pink hypostasis. Forensic relevance: accidental vs. suicidal exposure; house-fire deaths often have significant COHb. Treatment (antemortem): 100\% oxygen or hyperbaric oxygen.

\medterm{Cause of Death}-- The specific disease, injury, or toxic agent responsible for initiating the lethal physiological sequence leading directly to death (e.g., Gunshot wound to the head, Acute myocardial infarction).

\medterm{Chain of Custody}-- Meticulous paper trail and chronological documentation tracking the seizure, custody, control, transfer, analysis, and disposition of physical and electronic evidence in medico-legal investigations (e.g., blood samples for toxicology, bullets, sexual assault kits, biological fluids). Essential in forensic science to demonstrate that evidence presented in a court of law is authentic, uncontaminated, and has remained unaltered since collection. Any breach in the chain of custody can render evidence inadmissible.

\medterm{Concealed Pregnancy}-- Pregnancy that is hidden or not recognized until delivery, often by denial (psychogenic) or deliberate concealment; a medicolegal concept relevant to infanticide. Forensic issues: determination of pregnancy (autopsy of fetus, hyoid, lungs), whether the infant was born alive (hydrostatic test of lungs, aeration) or stillborn, viability, and cause of death. Distinguishing live birth from stillbirth and establishing cause of infant death (e.g., suffocation, neglect) are central. Context: concealment of birth is a separate statutory offense in some jurisdictions.

\medterm{Confidentiality}-- The ethical and legal duty of healthcare professionals to protect patient information from unauthorized disclosure. Founded on the principle of respect for patient autonomy and trust, confidentiality is a core requirement of medical ethics (GMC guidelines) and law (GDPR, Data Protection Act, Caldicott principles). Information should only be shared with the patient's explicit consent, or when required by law (public health reporting, court order), or when necessary to protect the patient or others from serious harm (safeguarding, GMC duty of candor). Breach of confidentiality can result in disciplinary action, professional sanctions, and legal liability. Exceptions: serious communicable disease reporting (HIV, TB), child protection concerns, fitness to drive notification, and terrorism prevention. The duty of confidentiality continues after death (access to records of the deceased requires executor or next-of-kin consent).

\medterm{Coroner}-- An elected or appointed official responsible for investigating sudden, unexpected, violent, or suspicious deaths and determining identity, cause, and manner of death; the office historically required no medical training. Responsibilities include authorizing autopsies, issuing death certificates, holding inquests, and ordering death notifications. Many jurisdictions have replaced coroners with medical examiner systems.

\medterm{Corpse}-- The dead body of a human being; the subject of medicolegal examination. Forensic assessment of a corpse includes identification, determination of death and time of death (rigor mortis, livor mortis, algor mortis, vitreous potassium, gastric emptying), external and internal examination, and collection of evidence. Corpses may be decomposed, mutilated, skeletalized, or otherwise altered, each requiring specialized techniques (entomology, anthropology, radiology). Preservation (cold storage, embalming) affects findings. The corpse is treated with dignity and handled under legal authority.

\medterm{Criminal Abortion}-- Abortion performed illegally, outside permitted legal indications and procedures (e.g., unsafe methods, unqualified practitioners, or non-medical settings), carrying high maternal risk. Methods historically included mechanical trauma, uterotonic drugs (oxytocin, prostaglandins), intrauterine solutions, and instrumentation—causing sepsis, hemorrhage, uterine perforation, and air embolism. Forensic evaluation: evidence of intervention (uterine injury, foreign material, products in the genital tract), pregnancy testing (hCG), complications, and the sequence of events. Overlaps with maternal death investigation.

\medterm{Cyanide Poisoning}-- Toxicity from cyanide compounds (hydrogen cyanide, potassium/sodium cyanide) that inhibit cytochrome oxidase (complex IV), blocking oxidative phosphorylation and cellular respiration despite adequate oxygen—histotoxic hypoxia. Sources: smoke inhalation (fires—plastic/acrylic), ingestion (industrial, suicidal), occupational exposure. Features: rapid collapse, seizures, bitter almond odor, rapid breathing then apnea, bright red lividity and venous blood (due to high venous oxygen). Diagnosis: blood cyanide levels, lactic acidosis, elevated anion gap. Death within minutes to hours. Antidotes (antemortem): sodium nitrite/thiosulfate, hydroxocobalamin.

\medterm{Decapitation}-- Separation of the head from the body, seen in severe trauma (vehicle accidents, train injuries, explosions), industrial accidents, hanging with judicial execution, or homicide. Complete decapitation is almost always rapidly fatal due to exsanguination and brain ischemia. Forensically: pattern of tissue/vertebral damage helps determine mechanism (sharp blade vs. blunt force avulsion); wound edges, tool marks, and associated injuries indicate whether accidental, suicidal, or homicidal. Scene reconstruction and identification are key.

\medterm{Decomposition}-- Postmortem breakdown of the body through autolysis and putrefaction. It begins within 24 to 72 hours at ambient temperature, producing green cecal discoloration, venous marbling, gas distension, skin slippage, and purge fluid. The sequence of fresh, bloated, active, advanced decay, and skeletal stages estimates the postmortem interval but depends on temperature, humidity, insect activity, and scavenging.

\medterm{Defense Mechanisms (Forensic)}-- Physical evidence patterns on a body demonstrating active resistance against an assailant prior to incapacitation.

\medterm{Defense Wounds}-- Injuries sustained by a victim attempting to ward off an attack, typically found on the flexor surfaces of hands, palms, wrists, and forearms (incised/stab wounds from knives or contusions/abrasions from blunt force).

\medterm{Defense Wounds (Inscribed)}-- Patterned incised or lacerated trauma localized to upper extremities indicating active physical defense.

\medterm{Diatom Test}-- Forensic technique used to support the diagnosis of drowning; microscopic silica-shelled diatoms present in drowning water enter lungs, penetrate alveolar capillaries, and circulate to distant closed organs (femur bone marrow, brain) only if active circulation was present at time of submersion.

\medterm{Domestic Violence}-- A pattern of coercive control and physical, sexual, psychological, or economic abuse by one intimate partner or household member against another. Forensic evaluation documents patterned injuries, defensive wounds, strangulation signs, burns, and injuries of differing ages. Providers have screening, safeguarding, and reporting obligations, and documentation records findings factually without attribution. Intimate partner homicide frequently follows a documented escalation pattern.

\medterm{Drowning (Dry/Wet Signs)}-- Cause of death resulting from submersion or immersion in liquid causing primary respiratory impairment. Wet Drowning (85--90\%): fluid enters the lungs, disrupting surfactant, causing alveolar collapse, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, hypoxia, cardiac arrest, and characteristic voluminous frothy foam emerging from the mouth and nostrils (Paltauf's foam). Dry Drowning (10--15\%): severe laryngospasm triggered by initial water contact blocks fluid from entering the lungs, causing death by asphyxiation. Post-mortem signs: washerwoman skin (wrinkling of skin on palms and soles), cadaveric spasm holding debris, petechial hemorrhages in temporal bones (Wydler's sign), and water in the stomach and sphenoid sinus.

\medterm{Drug Testing}-- Laboratory analysis of biological specimens to detect drugs, metabolites, or poisons, used in clinical, workplace, sports, and forensic contexts. Specimens include blood, urine, oral fluid, hair, and postmortem tissues. The workflow is immunoassay screening followed by confirmatory GC-MS or LC-MS/MS, with strict chain of custody for forensic admissibility. Interpretation must account for pharmacokinetics, tolerance, and postmortem redistribution.

\medterm{Entrance Wound}-- A skin defect created where a projectile enters the body, distinguished from an exit wound by smaller size, rounded shape, and abraded inward margin. Contact wounds show a muzzle imprint and soot; close-range wounds show soot and powder tattooing; intermediate-range wounds show stippling; distant wounds show a clean abrasion ring. Caliber cannot be reliably estimated from the wound.

\medterm{Euthanasia}-- The deliberate act of ending a person's life to relieve suffering, typically performed by a physician. Active euthanasia involves directly administering a lethal substance (e.g., barbiturate overdose followed by neuromuscular blockade) at the patient's explicit request. Voluntary euthanasia is performed with the patient's informed consent. Physician-assisted suicide (PAS) involves the physician prescribing a lethal dose of medication that the patient self-administers. Euthanasia is legal in a growing number of countries and jurisdictions under strict regulatory conditions (Belgium, Netherlands, Luxembourg, Canada, Colombia, Spain, New Zealand, and several Australian states, as well as PAS in Switzerland, Germany, Austria, and several US states). It is distinct from palliative sedation (using medication to relieve refractory suffering at the end of life, not intended to hasten death) and withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (allowing natural death).

\medterm{Euthanasia and Assisted Dying}-- Ethical and legal concepts regarding intentional termination of life to relieve intractable pain and suffering. Active Euthanasia: deliberate administration of a lethal agent (e.g., lethal dose of barbiturates) by a physician to terminate a patient's life upon their explicit request. Passive Euthanasia: withdrawal or withholding of life-sustaining treatment (e.g., mechanical ventilation, artificial nutrition), allowing natural death to occur. Physician-Assisted Suicide (Assisted Dying): a physician prescribes a lethal dose of medication that the patient self-administers. Legal status varies widely by international jurisdiction.

\medterm{Exhumation}-- The lawful disinterment and removal of a buried body or remains for re-examination, re-testing, or identification. Indications: new evidence, suspicion of poisoning or foul play, DNA testing, paternity/maternity testing, exhumation of a wrongly buried body, or repatriation. Procedures require legal authorization (court order); careful recording of grave location, coffin condition, and body preservation. Autopsy of exhumed bodies can detect poisoning, injuries, or medical errors even years after burial (formalin fixation, embalming affect testing).

\medterm{Exit Wound}-- A skin defect produced where a projectile leaves the body, typically larger, irregular, and stellate, with everted edges, no abrasion ring or soot. Size depends on deformation, yaw, and fragmentation; small non-deformed bullets may produce shored exits, and an exit may be absent if the projectile is retained. Combined with the entrance wound, it allows trajectory reconstruction.

\medterm{Expert Testimony}-- Opinion evidence given by a witness qualified by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education, admissible when it assists the trier of fact. Forensic physicians, pathologists, and toxicologists testify on cause and manner of death, injury, and toxicology, with opinions resting on reliable methods and adequate data. The role is educative and objective, distinguishing fact from opinion and disclosing limitations.

\medterm{Fingerprint}-- Friction-ridge skin pattern on fingers, palms, and soles that is individual, persistent, and classifiable; recording and comparing these patterns is a primary method of personal identification. Comparison of ridge minutiae between an unknown print and a known exemplar, with no unexplained differences, supports identification. Automated Fingerprint Identification Systems search large databases to generate candidate matches for examiner confirmation.

\medterm{Forensic Anthropology}-- The application of anthropological methods to identify human remains and analyze evidence, mainly skeletal material. Tasks: determination of biological profile (age at death, sex, ancestry, stature), distinguishing human from non-human bone, estimation of time since death (bone weathering, DNA), identifying trauma (perimortem vs. postmortem), and assisting mass-grave/disaster victim identification. Methods: osteometrics, radiography, histology, and stable isotope analysis. Works with forensic pathologists, odontologists, and entomologists in death investigation.

\medterm{Forensic Entomology}-- The study of insects and other arthropods in legal investigations, principally to estimate the postmortem interval (PMI) and to link suspects to scenes. Blow flies (Calliphoridae) and flesh flies colonize a corpse within minutes; the succession and developmental stages (eggs, larvae, pupae) are used to estimate time since death and season of death. Also: displacement of a body (different species in different habitats), presence of drugs in larvae, and neglect/mylasis in living victims. Collection must follow standardized protocols for legal admissibility.

\medterm{Forensic Examination}-- Structured medicolegal evaluation of a living or deceased person, scene, or evidence to document findings relevant to a legal matter. Components include sexual assault examination with evidence collection, child abuse assessment, injury documentation, driving-impairment evaluation, and autopsy. Standardized protocols, contemporaneous charting, photography, and evidence kits with strict chain of custody preserve findings, conducted by trained forensic clinicians or pathologists.

\medterm{Forensic Evidence}-- Physical, biological, documentary, or testimonial material gathered, preserved, and analyzed to establish facts in a legal investigation. Categories include biological evidence (blood, semen, hair), trace evidence (fibers, glass, gunshot residue), pattern evidence (fingerprints, toolmarks, bloodstain patterns), and digital evidence. Reliability depends on validated methods, transparent error reporting, rigorous chain of custody, contamination control, and mitigation of contextual bias.

\medterm{Forensic Medicine (Legal Medicine)}-- Branch of medicine that applies medical, biological, and scientific knowledge to legal questions and the administration of justice. Sub-fields: Forensic Pathology (determination of cause and manner of death via autopsy), Clinical Forensic Medicine (evaluation of living victims of sexual assault, domestic abuse, child abuse, torture, and blood alcohol/drug testing in drivers), Forensic Toxicology (detection of drugs and poisons in legal contexts), Forensic Psychiatry (evaluation of criminal responsibility and competency to stand trial), and Forensic Odontology (identification via dental records).

\medterm{Forensic Odontology}-- The application of dental science in legal contexts. Main uses: human identification through comparison of dental records (restorations, radiographs, unique anatomy—antemortem vs. postmortem), age estimation (tooth eruption, development, root transparency), bite mark analysis (pattern matching to suspect dentition—now increasingly questioned scientifically), and postmortem dental examination. Dental identification is highly reliable and often the primary method for decomposed or burned remains. Expert testimony requires rigorous methodology.

\medterm{Forensic Pathology}-- A branch of pathology and medicine applying medical and pathological knowledge to legal questions, primarily determining cause and manner of death and documenting injuries. The forensic pathologist performs autopsies in sudden, unnatural, suspicious, or violent deaths, interprets injuries (blunt vs. sharp vs. gunshot vs. thermal), determines time of death, collects evidence, and provides expert testimony. Works with law enforcement, medical examiners, coroners, and specialists (anthropology, odontology, toxicology). Legal requirements govern performance and reporting.

\medterm{Forensic Psychiatry}-- The medical specialty at the interface of psychiatry and the law. Core activities: competency to stand trial, criminal responsibility/insanity defense (mental state at time of offense), dangerousness/risk assessment, civil commitment, testamentary capacity, and personal injury claims. Forensic psychiatrists provide expert opinions, write reports, and testify. Also involved in evaluation of sex offenders, malingering, and the psychiatric effects of trauma. Ethical framework emphasizes objectivity and boundaries, as the patient is often the court.

\medterm{Forensic Radiography}-- The use of imaging (X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound) in forensic and medico-legal investigation. Applications: identification (dental, skeletal features, medical devices with serial numbers), detection of injuries (fractures, projectiles, glass), age estimation, diagnosis of medical conditions, and examination of remains (radiology of decomposed/mummified bodies). Postmortem CT (PMCT) and virtual autopsy (virtopsy) complement conventional autopsy, providing non-invasive documentation, evidence preservation, and radiation-safe examination of potentially hazardous remains.

\medterm{Forensic Toxicology}-- The analysis of drugs, poisons, and their metabolites in biological specimens (blood, urine, vitreous humor, hair, tissues) to determine their role in intoxication, poisoning, or death. Uses: postmortem toxicology (cause of death—overdose, poisoning, lethal concentrations), human performance (driving under influence, drug-facilitated crime), and clinical/antemortem. Methods: immunoassay screening, GC-MS, LC-MS/MS, quantitative confirmation. Interpretation considers distribution, postmortem redistribution, metabolism, tolerance, and drug interactions. Central to forensic death investigation.

\medterm{Gettler Test}-- Historical quantitative chemical test for drowning, comparing chloride concentration in blood harvested from the left and right heart ventricles.

\medterm{Gunshot Wound}-- An injury produced by a projectile discharged from a firearm; forensic classification by range and direction. Entrance wound: rounded/ovoid defect with an abraded ring and often a zone of soot (close range) or stippling/tattooing (intermediate range); contact wounds show muzzle imprint. Exit wound: typically larger, irregular, with everted edges and no abrasion ring. Internal track depends on bullet velocity, yaw, fragmentation, and tissue. Shotgun wounds produce pellet spread patterns useful for range estimation. Interpretation determines range, angle, and manner.

\medterm{Handwriting Analysis}-- The scientific examination of handwriting (and documents) for the purpose of identifying the author or establishing authenticity. Compares individual features: letter formation, slant, spacing, pressure, line quality, and characteristic defects; questioned documents may involve signatures, alterations, erasures, or obliterations. Basis is the principle that no two people write identically and an individual's handwriting has consistency and variation. Limitations: skilled forgery, deliberate disguise, and examiner subjectivity; admissible with validated methodology and often complemented by other forensic evidence.

\medterm{Hanging and Strangulation Signs}-- Medico-legal findings resulting from mechanical constriction of the neck structures. Hanging: neck constriction by a ligature tightened by the weight of the victim's body. Ligature mark: pale, hard, parchment-like groove running obliquely upward toward the knot, usually situated above the thyroid cartilage. Strangulation: neck constriction by a ligature tightened by external force (ligature strangulation, horizontal mark around neck) or by hands/fingers (manual strangulation/throttling, showing fingernail abrasions and contusions). Internal autopsy findings: fracture of hyoid bone and thyroid cartilage cornua, intramuscular hemorrhages of neck muscles, mucosal petechiae, and Tardieu spots.

\medterm{Hanging (Complete vs Incomplete)}-- Form of violent asphyxial death produced by suspension of the body by a ligature around the neck. Complete Hanging: feet off ground, total body weight exerts pressure. Incomplete Hanging: part of body touches ground. Asphyxia results from occlusion of jugular veins ($2\text{ kg}$ force) or carotid arteries ($5\text{ kg}$ force).

\medterm{Hesitation Wounds}-- Multiple superficial, tentative parallel incised cuts surrounding a deeper primary fatal cut wound, classically observed in suicides involving sharp instruments (wrists, neck).

\medterm{Incised Wound}-- Clean-cut wound produced by a sharp-edged instrument (scalpel, knife, glass) that is longer on the skin surface than it is deep, featuring clean edges without tissue bridging or marginal abrasion.

\medterm{Infanticide}-- The killing of a child, in law typically defined as the killing of a newborn or infant (usually within the first year, or specifically within 24 hours of birth in some jurisdictions) by its mother while mentally disturbed by the effects of childbirth or lactation. Forensic evaluation: distinguishing live birth from stillbirth (lung aeration/hydrostatic tests, stomach gas), establishing viability, determining cause of death (suffocation, neglect, trauma), and assessing maternal mental state. Considered a separate, less-severely-punished offense in many jurisdictions.

\medterm{Informed Consent}-- Autonomous voluntary authorization given by a competent patient to undergo a specific medical intervention, procedure, or clinical trial. Core elements required for valid informed consent: (1) Disclosure of adequate information (nature of procedure, benefits, material risks, alternatives, and consequences of refusal), (2) Patient comprehension of the disclosed information, (3) Decision-making capacity of the patient, and (4) Voluntariness (freedom from coercion, manipulation, or undue influence). Exceptions: emergency situations where immediate intervention is required to save life or prevent severe harm and patient lacks capacity.

\medterm{Injury Classification (Abrasion, Laceration, Contusion, Incised Wounds)}-- Forensic categorization of mechanical wounds. Abrasion: superficial friction injury affecting only the epidermis (e.g., scrape, graze, pattern abrasion). Contusion (Bruise): extravasation of blood into subcutaneous tissues caused by blunt trauma crushing blood vessels while leaving overlying skin intact; changes color over time (red/purple $\rightarrow$ blue/black $\rightarrow$ green $\rightarrow$ yellow). Laceration: tear or split of skin and deep tissues caused by blunt impact or crushing force, characterized by irregular, bruised edges, tissue bridging (intact nerves/vessels across the gap), and hair bulbs crushed. Incised Wound (Cut): sharp force injury caused by a blade, longer than it is deep, with clean-cut, unbruised margins and no tissue bridging.

\medterm{Laceration (Forensic)}-- Tear or disruption of tissue produced by blunt force trauma exceeding skin elasticity, featuring irregular bruised edges, tissue bridging (intact nerves, vessels across wound floor), and hair crushing.

\medterm{Ligature}-- Any cord, wire, rope, fabric, or flexible object used to constrict the neck, bind limbs, suspend the body, or inflict injury. The resulting pale groove-shaped abrasion may be horizontal with petechiae in homicidal strangulation, or oblique and upward in hanging. The mark may reproduce the implement's width and texture, and recovered ligatures are retained with knots intact for comparison.

\medterm{Living Will (Advance Statements)}-- A legally binding written document that specifies a person's preferences for medical treatment if they become unable to make decisions for themselves in the future (loss of capacity). Living wills are a type of advance directive (or advance decision/advance statement in the UK). They allow individuals to refuse specific treatments (e.g., cardiopulmonary resuscitation, mechanical ventilation, artificial nutrition and hydration, antibiotics) in specified future scenarios (e.g., terminal illness, permanent unconsciousness/vegetative state, severe dementia). Legal requirements: the document must be signed and witnessed, signed in the presence of a witness, and it must clearly state the treatments being refused and the circumstances in which the refusal applies. In the UK: the Mental Capacity Act 2005 provides the legal framework for advance decisions. They are legally binding for refusing life-sustaining treatment if they are valid and applicable to the current circumstances. A valid advance decision can be overridden if the person has capacity and changes their mind when the situation arises. Related concepts: (1) Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA): allowed in the UK from 2007 for health and welfare decisions as well as decisions about property and financial affairs (in England and Wales), giving a named person legal authority to make decisions on behalf of the incapacitated patient. (2) Do Not Attempt Resuscitation (DNAR/DNACPR): a medical order signed by a senior clinician indicating that CPR should not be attempted in the event of cardiac arrest. (3) Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment (POLST): a medical order form used in the US that translates patient preferences into actionable medical orders. Advance care planning (ACP) discussions are encouraged to clarify patient values, goals, and preferences for future medical care.

\medterm{Livor Mortis (Lividity)}-- Post-mortem purplish-red discoloration of dependent body parts resulting from gravitational settling of blood in uncompressed capillaries and small veins following cessation of circulation. Onset: begins within 30 minutes to 2 hours after death; becomes fully developed and "fixed" (no longer blanches upon digital pressure due to hemolysis and extravasation) after 8--12 hours. Color variations provide forensic clues: cherry-red (carbon monoxide poisoning), bright pink (cyanide, cold exposure), brown (methemoglobinemia). Shifting of lividity patterns indicates that the body was moved post-mortem.

\medterm{Livor Mortis (Postmortem Lividity)}-- Purplish-red discoloration of dependent body parts following death, caused by gravitational pooling of blood in uncompressed capillaries and venules. Starts within 30 min--2 hours, becomes fixed after 6--12 hours. Color changes assist in diagnosing cause of death (cherry-red in CO, bright red in cold exposure/cyanide, chocolate brown in methemoglobinemia).

\medterm{Manner of Death}-- Legal classification of the circumstances surrounding a death, categorized into 5 official types: Natural, Accident, Suicide, Homicide, or Undetermined.

\medterm{Mechanism of Death}-- The physiologic or biochemical derangement that causes death (the final failure) as opposed to the underlying disease or injury (cause of death) and the circumstances (manner). Examples: ventricular fibrillation, cerebral hemorrhage, sepsis, respiratory failure, exsanguination, cardiac tamponade. The mechanism is an intermediate process; e.g., a cause of blunt force head trauma may act via the mechanism of cerebral edema and herniation. Correct certification distinguishes cause (disease/injury) from mechanism (physiologic derangement) from manner.

\medterm{Medical Examiner}-- An appointed physician, usually a board-certified forensic pathologist, charged with investigating sudden, unexpected, violent, or suspicious deaths, ordering autopsies, determining cause and manner of death, and certifying the death certificate. Unlike the elected coroner, a medical examiner must meet professional qualifications. Such systems coordinate with law enforcement, toxicology, and odontology and testify as expert witnesses.

\medterm{Medical Jurisprudence}-- The branch of law (and its study) that deals with the rights and duties of physicians and the legal aspects of medical practice, distinct from but overlapping with forensic medicine. Topics: consent (informed), confidentiality and privileged communication, professional negligence/malpractice, medical ethics, licensing, regulation of medical practice, medico-legal aspects of organ donation and transplantation, and expert testimony. Medical jurisprudence provides the legal framework within which forensic medicine operates.

\medterm{Medical Negligence (Malpractice)}-- Legal tort occurring when a healthcare provider fails to exercise the standard of reasonable care, skill, or diligence expected of a prudent practitioner of similar standing, resulting in injury, harm, or death to a patient. Legal elements required to establish medical negligence (the "4 Ds"): (1) Duty of care established between provider and patient, (2) Dereliction (breach) of that duty, (3) Direct causation (the breach directly caused the injury), and (4) Damages (measurable physical, financial, or emotional harm suffered by the patient).

\medterm{Medical Records}-- A documented account of a patient's clinical history, examination, investigations, treatment, and outcome maintained by providers. In medicolegal practice, records are contemporaneous evidence in malpractice, injury, criminal, and death cases, so accuracy, timeliness, and unaltered amendments are essential. Access is governed by confidentiality statutes and court order. Forensic value is enhanced by detailed injury descriptions, timelines, and photographs.

\medterm{Medicolegal}-- Pertaining to the application of medical knowledge to legal questions and the intersection of medicine and the law. Covers forensic pathology, toxicology, psychiatry, and clinical issues: informed consent, malpractice, medical records, disability, personal injury, occupational disease, and public health law. Medicolegal investigation of death involves determining cause, manner, and time of death, and issuing death certificates. Documents (clinical records, expert reports) must meet evidentiary standards.

\medterm{Morgue}-- A facility (mortuary) where bodies are stored and medicolegal/hospital autopsies are performed. Features: refrigerated body storage, autopsy rooms with ventilation and safety (surgical instruments, saws), photographic and evidence-collection equipment, toxicology specimen handling, and secure chain-of-custody areas. Also: viewing rooms for identification and temporary holding pending burial/cremation. Standards govern hygiene, safety (blood-borne pathogens, chemicals), and legal documentation.

\medterm{Multidisciplinary Disaster Victim Identification}-- The coordinated process of identifying victims of mass-fatality incidents (natural disasters, transport crashes, terrorist attacks) using multiple disciplines: fingerprinting, dental records, DNA profiling, forensic anthropology/radiology, and personal effects, with ante-mortem data collection and comparison. Follows standardized protocols (Interpol DVI) with phases: scene management, body recovery, mortuary examination, ante-mortem data, reconciliation, and family support. Priority is accurate, ethical, and timely identification of all victims.

\medterm{Mummification}-- Postmortem alteration occurring in hot, dry, arid, ventilated environments where rapid desiccation of tissues occurs before putrefaction can proceed, resulting in dry, leathery, dark brown skin clinging to underlying skeleton.

\medterm{Paternity Testing}-- The use of genetic markers to establish or exclude a biological parent-child relationship, the reference method being short tandem repeat DNA profiling of the child, alleged father, and mother. Modern STR analysis commonly yields paternity probability exceeding 99.99 percent for true fathers. Applications include child support, custody, inheritance, and remains identification. Collection and analysis follow chain-of-custody and accredited-laboratory standards.

\medterm{Post-Mortem}-- An examination of a body after death to determine the cause of death, identify disease processes, and evaluate the effects of medical or surgical treatment. Also called autopsy, necropsy. Types: hospital (clinical) post-mortem — performed with consent to investigate the cause of death, assess treatment, or advance medical knowledge; medico-legal (coroner's/forensic) post-mortem — ordered by a coroner or legal authority when the death is sudden, unexpected, suspicious, or unnatural (required by law; consent not needed). Procedure: external examination, internal examination (thoracic, abdominal, and cranial cavities), histopathology (tissue sampling), toxicology (blood, urine, vitreous humor), microbiology, and other specialized tests. The report includes: cause of death (immediate, underlying, and contributing factors), and relevant pathological findings. Post-mortem examination is essential for quality assurance in medicine (verification of clinical diagnosis, identification of missed diagnoses, and audit of surgical and medical care). In obstetrics, perinatal post-mortem is crucial for understanding stillbirth, congenital anomalies, and maternal death.

\medterm{Postmortem Artifact}-- Any structural, chemical, or physical change introduced into a body after death (e.g., embalming artifacts, insect activity, postmortem animal scavenging, resuscitation trauma) that mimics antemortem disease or trauma.

\medterm{Postmortem Interval}-- The time elapsed since death, estimated by forensic scientists. Indicators: algor mortis (cooling—most accurate early), rigor mortis (onset 2-6 h, full 12-24 h, resolution ~24-48 h), livor mortis (dependent lividity; fixed ~4-12 h), vitreous potassium (increases after ~12-24 h), gastric emptying, liver temperature, decomposition stage, and forensic entomology (insect succession). PMI estimation is approximate and affected by environmental temperature, body size, clothing, and circumstances. Used to establish a timeline and corroborate or refute accounts.

\medterm{Postmortem Interval (PMI)}-- Estimated elapsed time between actual death and the time of autopsy or body discovery, calculated utilizing algor mortis, livor mortis, rigor mortis, vitreous potassium levels, and insect succession patterns.

\medterm{Pugilistic Stance}-- The characteristic body position adopted by fire victims—flexed arms, elbows bent, fists clenched, knees flexed—resulting from heat-induced contraction (denaturation) of muscles (mainly flexors) and tendons, not from conscious movement. It occurs with thermal injury and can be present regardless of cause (even if death preceded fire). Its presence indicates the body was exposed to heat, not that the person was fighting. Important for distinguishing antemortem from postmortem burning and interpreting fire deaths.

\medterm{Punctate Hemorrhages (Tardieu Spots)}-- Small, pinpoint sub-ecchymotic hemorrhages (petechiae) resulting from the rupture of small capillaries under conditions of intense mechanical venous engorgement and asphyxia. Commonly observed on conjunctivae, sclerae, facial skin, epicardium, pleural surfaces, and thymus in deaths due to mechanical asphyxia (strangulation, hanging, suffocation, traumatic asphyxia). First described by Auguste Ambroise Tardieu in 1859.

\medterm{Putrefaction}-- Postmortem decomposition of body tissues brought about by bacterial and enzymatic action, characterized by green discoloration of lower abdomen (cecum), marbling of cutaneous veins, gas distension, foul odor, and liquefaction.

\medterm{Rape}-- Sexual penetration of the mouth, vagina, or anus accomplished without consent or with a person unable to consent, often through force, threat, fraud, or impairment; many statutes subsume it under sexual assault. Forensic examination within about 120 hours documents injuries and collects biological evidence using a standardized kit with strict chain of custody. Prophylaxis and trauma-informed consent are central.

\medterm{Res Ipsa Loquitur}-- Latin legal doctrine meaning "the thing speaks for itself." Applied in medical malpractice cases where an injury is of a nature that ordinarily does not occur in the absence of negligence, the instrumentality causing harm was under the exclusive control of the defendant, and the plaintiff did not contribute to the injury. Examples: retaining a surgical sponge or instrument inside a patient's abdomen, or amputating the wrong limb. When invoked, it creates a rebuttable presumption of negligence, shifting the burden of proof to the defendant.

\medterm{Rigor Mortis}-- Post-mortem stiffening of body muscles resulting from loss of ATP synthesis following death. Without ATP, actin and myosin filaments remain permanently cross-linked in a rigid state. Onset: detectable in small muscles (jaw, eyelids) within 2--4 hours, becomes fully established in all voluntary muscles by 12 hours, remains fixed for 12 hours, and gradually resolves over the subsequent 12--24 hours as autolytic proteolytic enzymes break down muscle proteins ("rule of 12s": 12 hours onset, 12 hours duration, 12 hours resolution). Accelerated by high ambient temperature, fever, or violent exertion prior to death.

\medterm{Sexual Assault}-- A broad legal category of any non-consensual sexual contact or penetration, encompassing rape, indecent assault, and drug-facilitated assault. Examination documents general and anogenital injuries, collects biological and trace samples, and screens for drug-facilitated assault by extended-window testing. Consent capacity, intoxication, and coercion are central legal issues. Absence of genital injury does not exclude assault, and delayed presentation is common.

\medterm{Sharp Force Trauma}-- Injury produced by an edged or pointed instrument that cuts or penetrates the skin, divided into incised wounds (longer than deep, clean margins) and stab wounds (deeper than wide). Wound length, depth, angle, hesitation marks, and defense injuries infer weapon type and manner. Tissue bridging distinguishes sharp-force from blunt-force lacerations. Penetration of major vessels may cause rapid exsanguination.

\medterm{Skeletonization}-- Final stage of postmortem decomposition where soft tissues, organs, and skin have completely decayed or been scavenged, leaving only clean osseous skeletal remains.

\medterm{Stab Wound}-- A penetrating sharp-force injury in which wound track depth exceeds skin defect length, produced by a pointed or sharp-edged implement. The skin defect approximates blade width only when perpendicular to Langer's lines, and a single-edged blade produces one sharp and one blunt end. Chest and abdominal stab wounds often cause fatal hemorrhage or tamponade despite a small external appearance.

\medterm{Standard of Care}-- The conduct, skill, diligence, and judgment a reasonably prudent practitioner of the same specialty would exercise under similar circumstances; the benchmark for medical negligence. It is established through guidelines, peer practice, and expert testimony rather than fixed statute. Breach is but one malpractice element, with duty, causation, and damages, and permits a range of acceptable options.

\medterm{Stippling (Tattooing)}-- Patterned dermal abrasions and punctate hemorrhages surrounding a firearm entrance wound, caused by unburned and partially burned gunpowder grains striking the skin at close range ($15\text{--}75\text{ cm}$).

\medterm{Strangulation}-- Compression of the neck causing occlusion of the airway (asphyxia) and/or cervical vessels (carotid/jugular), leading to unconsciousness and death. Types: ligature strangulation (cord, rope, cloth—external mark above the larynx, often horizontal), manual (manual throttling—fingertip bruises, thumb imprint, often vertical), and garroting. Signs: petechial hemorrhages (face, conjunctivae), facial congestion/cyanosis, neck soft tissue hemorrhage, hyoid/laryngeal fractures, and ligature marks. High rate of homicidal cases; survivor evaluation requires careful examination and documentation.

\medterm{Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS)}-- Sudden, unexpected death of an infant under 1 year of age that remains unexplained after a thorough medico-legal investigation, including a complete autopsy, crime scene investigation, and review of clinical history. Peak incidence: 2--4 months of age. Pathophysiology: "triple-risk model" involving a vulnerable infant (brainstem respiratory control deficit), a critical developmental period, and an extrinsic stressor (prone sleeping position, soft bedding, exposure to cigarette smoke, overheating). Preventive campaign: "Back to Sleep" (placing infants supine for sleep) has reduced SIDS incidence by over 50\% globally.

\medterm{Suffocation}-- Death or injury from failure of oxygen to reach the lungs, a category of asphyxia distinct from strangulation. Subtypes include smothering (airway obstruction), choking or foreign-body obstruction, mechanical asphyxia (positional, chest compression), and entrapment in oxygen-deficient environments. Findings may be subtle: facial and conjunctival petechiae, congestion, and patterned injuries. Investigation relies on scene findings and circumstantial reconstruction.

\medterm{Thanatology}-- Scientific study of death, dying, post-mortem phenomena, and the psychological, social, and ethical aspects of bereavement. In forensic medicine, thanatology focuses on establishing clinical and legal definitions of death (brain death vs. somatic death), post-mortem changes (algor mortis, livor mortis, rigor mortis, decomposition, adipocere, mummification), and estimating the post-mortem interval (PMI).

\medterm{Time of Death}-- The moment or estimated time at which biological death occurred, a key medicolegal question. Estimated from postmortem changes: body cooling (algor mortis), rigor mortis, lividity (livor mortis), decomposition/putrefaction, insect colonization, and chemical changes (vitreous potassium, vitreous glucose, body temperature). Clinical evidence (last seen, medical records, stomach contents, personal effects) and scene findings contribute. Accuracy is limited; estimates are reported as ranges with confidence intervals. Legal importance: timeline, alibi corroboration, and manner determination.

\medterm{Trajectory}-- The path a projectile follows in flight and, in wound ballistics, the course it traverses through tissue from entrance to exit or rest. Internal trajectory is reconstructed from entrance and exit defects, the organ track, recovered fragments, and clothing damage; angles disclose shooter position and victim posture. Yaw, deformation, and fragmentation must be considered. Accurate analysis underpins shooting reconstruction.

\medterm{Vitriolage}-- The deliberate throwing or application of a corrosive chemical (vitriol—sulfuric acid, historically; now any strong acid/base) onto a person to disfigure, disable, or kill; a form of assault. Injuries: severe facial and body burns, scarring, blindness, respiratory injury from fumes, and psychological trauma. Forensic evaluation: chemical identification (burn pattern, cloth damage, laboratory analysis), extent/severity of injury, age and nature of scarring, and medicolegal reporting. Legal status varies; often treated as aggravated assault with life imprisonment in some jurisdictions.

\medterm{Witness}-- An individual who gives sworn testimony; a clinician may appear as a witness of fact (describing observations and care) or as an expert offering interpretive opinion. Testimony must be truthful, objective, and within personal knowledge or reliable methodology, distinguishing fact from opinion and avoiding advocacy. Prior statements and records are disclosable, and inconsistencies may be explored on cross-examination.

\medterm{Wound}-- An injury to living tissue caused by mechanical force; in forensic medicine, wounds are classified by mechanism: abrasion (scrape—loss of epidermis, no bleeding), laceration (tear from blunt force—ragged edges, tissue bridges), incised/incision (clean cut by sharp edge—linear, edges gape), stab/puncture (penetrating injury by pointed weapon—depth exceeds width), contusion (bruise), and gunshot wound. Wound interpretation (shape, edges, depth, location, associated findings) helps determine weapon type, force, angle, and manner (accident, suicide, homicide). Documentation includes measurement and photography.
