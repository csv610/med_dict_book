\medterm{Abscess}-- A localized collection of pus within a tissue, organ, or cavity, typically caused by bacterial infection. Abscesses form when bacteria (most commonly Staphylococcus aureus including MRSA) invade tissue, triggering an intense inflammatory response with neutrophil accumulation and tissue necrosis. The body attempts to wall off the infection, creating a walled-off cavity filled with purulent material (dead neutrophils, bacteria, necrotic tissue). Clinical presentation includes a painful, swollen, erythematous, warm mass that may fluctuate (indicating fluid collection). Systemic symptoms (fever, leukocytosis) may be present. Diagnosis is primarily clinical; ultrasound confirms the presence and size of a fluid collection. Treatment requires incision and drainage (I and D) for Source control, as antibiotics alone cannot penetrate the abscess wall effectively. Small abscesses (< 2 cm) may respond to warm compresses and antibiotics. Culture should be obtained to guide antibiotic therapy, particularly for recurrent abscesses or MRSA risk factors. Complications include cellulitis, bacteremia, and sepsis if untreated. Common locations include skin and soft tissue (furuncles, carbuncles), perianal (perianal abscess), intra-abdominal (from perforated viscus), dental (dental abscess), and brain (cerebral abscess).

\medterm{Anaphylaxis}-- Anaphylaxis is a severe, potentially life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by rapid onset of multisystem involvement, primarily affecting the skin, respiratory tract, cardiovascular system, and gastrointestinal tract. It is mediated by IgE-dependent mast cell and basophil degranulation (Type I hypersensitivity) or by direct mast cell activation (non-IgE mediated, e.g., radiocontrast, opioids, vancomycin). Common triggers: foods (peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, fish, eggs, milk, soy, wheat), medications (penicillins, NSAIDs, neuromuscular blocking agents, radiocontrast), insect stings (Hymenoptera: bees, wasps, fire ants), and exercise (exercise-induced anaphylaxis, often fooddependent). Clinical criteria: acute onset (minutes to hours) with involvement of skin/mucosa PLUS respiratory compromise (wheeze, stridor, dyspnea, hypoxia) OR reduced blood pressure (systolic less than 90 mmHg in adults, or greater than 30 percent decrease from baseline) OR gastrointestinal symptoms (vomiting, crampy abdominal pain) with skin involvement. Management: immediate intramuscular epinephrine (0.3-0.5 mg of 1:1,000 solution in the mid-outer thigh, repeat every 5-15 minutes as needed) is the first-line and most critical intervention. Adjunctive treatments: high-flow oxygen, IV fluids (normal saline or lactated Ringer’s for hypotension), H1 antihistamine (diphenhydramine 25-50 mg IV), H2 antihistamine (famotidine 20 mg IV), corticosteroids (methylprednisolone 125 mg IV or prednisone 40-60 mg PO), and albuterol nebulizer for bronchospasm. Patients should be observed for at least 4-6 hours (biphasic reaction occurs in 1-20 percent of cases). All patients discharged after anaphylaxis should receive an epinephrine auto-injector and referral to an allergist.

\medterm{Bends (Decompression Sickness)}-- A condition resulting from rapid ascent from deep water or pressurized environment, causing dissolved inert gases (primarily nitrogen) to form bubbles in tissues and blood. Symptoms range from joint pain (the bends), skin rash (cutis marmorata), and neurologic deficits (paresthesias, weakness, confusion) to respiratory distress (the chokes) and cardiovascular collapse. Treatment: 100% oxygen and recompression in a hyperbaric chamber. Prevention: staged ascent with decompression stops and adherence to dive tables or computers.

\medterm{Burns and Scalds}-- Tissue damage caused by thermal injury: burns from dry heat (flame, hot surfaces, electricity, radiation) and scalds from moist heat (hot liquids, steam). Classification by depth: superficial (epidermal, red, painful), partial-thickness (dermal involvement, blisters), and full-thickness (all skin layers, often painless due to nerve destruction). Scalds typically cause partial-thickness injuries with characteristic splash patterns. First aid: remove source, cool with running water for 20 minutes, cover with sterile dressing. Management of significant burns: fluid resuscitation (Parkland formula), burn wound care, infection prevention, and surgical debridement and grafting for deep wounds.

\medterm{Cardiopulmonary resuscitation}-- A combination of chest compressions and mouthto-mouth breathing that keep oxygenated blood circulating to the brain and tissues. Commonly known as CPR.

\medterm{Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)}-- An emergency life-saving procedure performed to restore circulation and breathing in a person who is in cardiac arrest. The basic life support sequence: check responsiveness, activate emergency services, begin chest compressions (at least 100--120\/min, depth 5--6 cm in adults, allow full chest recoil), open airway (head-tilt chin-lift), give rescue breaths (30 compressions to 2 breaths). Compression-only CPR is recommended for untrained bystanders. Advanced cardiac life support adds defibrillation (for shockable rhythms VF\/pVT), advanced airway placement, IV\/IO access, and medications (epinephrine, amiodarone). High-quality CPR with minimal interruptions is the single most important determinant of survival.

\medterm{Defibrillation}-- The delivery of a controlled electrical shock to the heart to terminate life-threatening arrhythmias, specifically ventricular fibrillation (VF) and pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT), allowing the sinoatrial node to resume normal rhythm. Defibrillation is time-critical: survival decreases by 7--10% per minute without it. Automated external defibrillators (AEDs) analyze rhythm and deliver shocks with voice prompts for bystander use. Biphasic waveforms (150--200 J) are more effective than monophasic (360 J) with less myocardial damage. For shockable rhythms: defibrillate immediately, then resume CPR for 2 minutes before rhythm check. Energy selection: adult 120--200 J biphasic; pediatric 2--4 J/kg.

\medterm{Heat Stroke}-- Heat stroke is a life-threatening thermoregulatory failure characterized by a core body temperature exceeding 40 degrees Celsius (104 degrees Fahrenheit) accompanied by central nervous system dysfunction (altered mental status, seizure, or coma) in the setting of extreme heat exposure, representing the most severe form of heat-related illness. It is classified into two forms: classic (passive) heat stroke, which affects the very young, the elderly, and individuals with chronic medical conditions during environmental heat waves, developing over days of sustained heat exposure; and exertional heat stroke, which affects young, active individuals (athletes, military personnel, outdoor laborers) performing strenuous physical activity in hot and humid conditions, developing over hours. The pathophysiology involves overwhelming of the body's thermoregulatory mechanisms, with core temperature rising beyond the capacity of evaporative cooling, leading to direct cellular toxicity, denaturation of proteins, and a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) that can progress to multiorgan dysfunction. Clinical presentation includes hyperthermia, CNS dysfunction (ataxia, confusion, delirium, seizures, coma), hot and dry skin in classic heat stroke (anhidrosis due to failure of sweat production), or hot and moist skin in exertional heat stroke (where sweating may persist), along with tachycardia, hypotension, tachypnea, and signs of end-organ damage including acute kidney injury, hepatic injury, rhabdomyolysis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Diagnosis is clinical based on elevated core temperature with CNS dysfunction in the appropriate setting; laboratory findings may include elevated creatine kinase, transaminases, creatinine, and coagulation abnormalities. Treatment focuses on rapid cooling (target core temperature below 39 degrees Celsius within 30 minutes) using evaporative cooling (mist and fan), cold water immersion, ice packs to the axillae, groin, and neck, cold IV crystalloids, and cold gastric or peritoneal lavage in refractory cases, along with supportive care including fluid resuscitation, airway management, and monitoring for complications.

\medterm{Heat Stroke / Heat Exhaustion}-- Heat-related illnesses ranging from mild (heat cramps, heat oedema, heat syncope) to severe (heat exhaustion, heat stroke). Heat exhaustion: a condition of hypovolaemia and electrolyte depletion from excessive sweating and inadequate fluid intake during heat exposure. Symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, weakness, fatigue, tachycardia, hypotension, hyperventilation, normal mental status, core temperature 37-40 degrees C (98-104 F). Management: remove from heat, rest supine, cool environment, oral or IV rehydration (0.9% saline or sports drinks). Heat stroke: a life-threatening emergency defined by core temperature >40 degrees C (104 F) with central nervous system dysfunction (altered mental status, confusion, delirium, seizures, coma). Anhidrosis (hot, dry skin) is common in classic heat stroke, but profuse sweating may still be present in exertional heat stroke (occurring in young athletes, military recruits). Pathophysiology: overwhelming heat stress exceeds the body's thermoregulatory capacity (hypothalamus), leading to direct cellular toxicity (protein denaturation, mitochondrial damage), systemic inflammatory response (SIRS, cytokine release), and multi-organ dysfunction. End-organ damage: encephalopathy, rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), myocardial injury, liver failure (centrilobular necrosis). Treatment: immediate aggressive cooling (ABCs, remove clothing, ice packs to groin/axillae/neck, cold water immersion, evaporative cooling with mist and fans, cold IV fluids 4-10 degrees C, cold gastric/rectal/pleural/peritoneal lavage in refractory cases). Target: reduce core temperature to 39 degrees C then stop active cooling to avoid overshoot hypothermia. Monitor for and treat complications (seizures, rhabdomyolysis, AKI, DIC, arrhythmias). Mortality: 10-50%, worse with delayed cooling, extremes of age, and comorbidities.

\medterm{Hypothermia}-- A medical emergency defined by a core body temperature below 35 degrees C (95 F). Classification: mild (32-35 C, 90-95 F): shivering, impaired fine motor skills, amnesia, dysarthria, apathy; moderate (28-32 C, 82-90 F): shivering stops, confusion, dilated pupils, arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation, Osborne J waves on ECG), hyporeflexia; severe (<28 C, <82 F): coma, areflexia, fixed/dilated pupils (may mimic death), severe bradycardia, hypotension, pulmonary oedema, ventricular fibrillation or asystole. Primary hypothermia: environmental exposure (cold water immersion, inadequate clothing in cold weather, mountaineering, homelessness). Secondary hypothermia: impaired thermoregulation (drugs--alcohol, sedatives; endocrine--hypothyroidism, hypoglycaemia, adrenal insufficiency; neurological--stroke, spinal cord injury, Wernicke; sepsis; burns; extremes of age). The patient may appear dead but should not be pronounced dead until rewarmed to >32 C. Assessment: core temperature (low-reading rectal thermometer, oesophageal probe), ECG (J wave/Osborn wave at the QRS-ST junction, prolonged PR, QRS, QT intervals, atrial fibrillation, ventricular fibrillation). Management: (1) Mild: passive external rewarming (warm environment, blankets, warm oral fluids). (2) Moderate-severe: active external rewarming (forced warm air, warm blankets), active internal/core rewarming (warm IV fluids 38-42 C, heated humidified oxygen, gastric/bladder/colonic lavage with warm fluids, pleural or peritoneal lavage with warm saline, arteriovenous or venovenous rewarming (continuous arteriovenous rewarming--CAVR), extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) or cardiopulmonary bypass for severe hypothermia with cardiac arrest. Handle gently (ventricular fibrillation threshold is low). CPR and defibrillation per ALS protocol (defibrillation may be ineffective if core temp <30 C, limit shocks to 3 until rewarmed). Prognosis depends more on the cause of hypothermia than the degree. Survival with full neurological recovery after prolonged arrest in hypothermia, especially in drowning (cold water protective effect).

\medterm{Hypothermia Management}-- Hypothermia (core temperature less than 35 degrees Celsius) requires careful management to avoid complications of rewarming. Classification: mild (32-35 degrees C), moderate (28-32 degrees C), severe (less than 28 degrees C). The hypothermic heart is irritable and prone to ventricular fibrillation, particularly during movement or rewarming. The "core temperature drop" phenomenon (afterdrop) occurs when cold peripheral blood returns to the core during rewarming, causing further core temperature decline. Management principles: handle the patient gently (rough handling can trigger ventricular fibrillation), monitor cardiac rhythm continuously, and avoid unnecessary movement. Rewarming methods: passive external rewarming (insulating blankets, warm environment) for mild hypothermia with intact shivering; active external rewarming (forcedair warming blankets, heating pads, warm water immersion of extremities) for moderate hypothermia; active internal rewarming (warmed IV fluids at 40-42 degrees C, warmed humidified oxygen, body cavity lavage [pleural, peritoneal, pericardial], extracorporeal rewarming [CPB or ECMO]) for severe hypothermia or patients with cardiovascular instability

\medterm{Pulmonary Embolism}-- Pulmonary embolism (PE) is obstruction of pulmonary arteries by thrombus (usually from DVT). Risk factors: Virchow triad (stasis, hypercoagulability, endothelial injury). Wells score (clinical probability), PERC rule (low risk exclusion). Management: anticoagulation (LMWH, DOAC, UFH, warfarin). Massive PE: systemic thrombolysis (alteplase 100 mg over 2h, tenecteplase 0.5 mg/kg single bolus) if no contraindication; catheter-directed thrombolysis/mechanical thrombectomy if contraindication; surgical embolectomy if failed. Submassive: consider catheter-directed therapy if clinical deterioration. IVC filter if anticoagulation contraindicated.
