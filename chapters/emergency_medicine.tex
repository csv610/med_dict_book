\medterm{Abscess}-- A localized collection of pus within a tissue, organ, or cavity, typically caused by bacterial infection. Abscesses form when bacteria (most commonly Staphylococcus aureus including MRSA) invade tissue, triggering an intense inflammatory response with neutrophil accumulation and tissue necrosis. The body attempts to wall off the infection, creating a walled-off cavity filled with purulent material (dead neutrophils, bacteria, necrotic tissue). Clinical presentation includes a painful, swollen, erythematous, warm mass that may fluctuate (indicating fluid collection). Systemic symptoms (fever, leukocytosis) may be present. Diagnosis is primarily clinical; ultrasound confirms the presence and size of a fluid collection. Treatment requires incision and drainage (I and D) for Source control, as antibiotics alone cannot penetrate the abscess wall effectively. Small abscesses (< 2 cm) may respond to warm compresses and antibiotics. Culture should be obtained to guide antibiotic therapy, particularly for recurrent abscesses or MRSA risk factors. Complications include cellulitis, bacteremia, and sepsis if untreated. Common locations include skin and soft tissue (furuncles, carbuncles), perianal (perianal abscess), intra-abdominal (from perforated viscus), dental (dental abscess), and brain (cerebral abscess).

\medterm{Acute Aortic Syndromes}-- A spectrum of life-threatening conditions involving the thoracic aorta, including aortic dissection, intramural hematoma (IMH), and penetrating aortic ulcer (PAU). Aortic dissection involves an intimal tear creating a false lumen. Classification (Stanford): Type A involves the ascending aorta (surgical emergency); Type B involves only the descending aorta (medical management unless complicated). Clinical presentation: sudden onset of severe, tearing or ripping chest or back pain radiating to the interscapular region, hypertension or hypotension, pulse deficits, aortic regurgitation murmur, or neurological deficits. Diagnosis: CT angiography (gold standard), transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), or MRI. Management: anti-impulse therapy (IV beta-blockers like esmolol/labetalol targeting HR < 60 bpm and SBP 100--120 mmHg), followed by emergency surgery for Type A or TEVAR/medical management for Type B.

\medterm{Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS)}-- A spectrum of conditions caused by reduced coronary blood flow and myocardial ischemia: unstable angina, non-ST-elevation MI (NSTEMI), and ST-elevation MI (STEMI). Usual cause: rupture/erosion of an atherosclerotic plaque with superimposed thrombosis. Features: chest pain (pressure/crushing, radiating to arm/jaw), dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea; atypical in women, diabetics, elderly. Diagnosis: ECG (ST elevation for STEMI), serial troponin. Management: aspirin, antiplatelet (P2Y12), anticoagulant, nitroglycerin, morphine (as needed), statin, beta-blocker; reperfusion—primary PCI within 90 minutes (STEMI) or fibrinolysis.

\medterm{Acute Coronary Syndromes}-- A spectrum of conditions comprising unstable angina (UA), non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). Pathophysiology: atherosclerotic plaque rupture or erosion leading to thrombus formation and acute coronary artery occlusion. STEMI: complete occlusion presenting with ST-segment elevation in contiguous leads ($\ge 1$ mm in limb leads, $\ge 2$ mm in precordial leads) or new LBBB. NSTEMI: partial occlusion causing cardiac troponin elevation without ST elevation. Unstable angina: ischemic symptoms at rest or increasing frequency without troponin elevation. Management: STEMI requires immediate reperfusion via primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) within 90 minutes (door-to-balloon) or fibrinolysis within 30 minutes if PCI is unavailable within 120 minutes. Medical therapy: dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor), anticoagulation (heparin), high-intensity statin, beta-blocker, and ACE inhibitor.

\medterm{Adrenal Crisis}-- An acute, life-threatening manifestation of severe adrenal insufficiency. Causes: primary (Addison disease), secondary (pituitary failure), or tertiary (exogenous corticosteroid withdrawal). Precipitants: systemic infection, trauma, surgery, or abrupt steroid cessation. Clinical presentation: severe hypotension refractory to vasopressors and fluid resuscitation, fever, abdominal pain, vomiting, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, and altered mental status. Emergency management: immediate administration of IV hydrocortisone 100 mg bolus, followed by 200 mg/24 hours continuous infusion; aggressive isotonic saline fluid resuscitation; dextrose for hypoglycemia; and identification and treatment of the underlying precipitating cause.

\medterm{Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS)}-- A set of clinical interventions for the urgent treatment of cardiac arrest, stroke, myocardial infarction, and life-threatening arrhythmias. Includes airway management, ECG rhythm interpretation, electrical defibrillation/cardioversion, transcutaneous pacing, and administration of emergency medications (epinephrine, amiodarone, atropine, adenosine, lidocaine).

\medterm{Airway Management}-- The assessment and control of the patient's airway to ensure patency, oxygenation, and ventilation, a critical emergency skill. Techniques: positioning (head-tilt/chin-lift, jaw thrust), suction, oral/nasal airways, bag-valve-mask ventilation, supraglottic devices (LMA, i-gel), and definitive airway (endotracheal intubation—direct laryngoscopy, video laryngoscopy; surgical cricothyroidotomy as rescue). Rapid-sequence intubation (RSI) uses induction agents and paralytics. Confirm placement (ETCO2, capnography, auscultation, CXR). Failure requires back-up strategies (difficult airway algorithm).

\medterm{Altitude Illness}-- Acute mountain sickness (AMS): headache + 1 symptom (nausea, fatigue, dizziness, insomnia) at >2500m. HACE: AMS + ataxia/altered mental status (cerebral edema). HAPE: dyspnea at rest, cough, frothy sputum, crackles, hypoxemia (noncardiogenic pulmonary edema). Prevention: gradual ascent (300-500m/day above 3000m), acetazolamide 125 mg q12h prophylaxis. Treatment: descent (immediate for HACE/HAPE), oxygen, acetazolamide, dexamethasone 8 mg load then 4 mg q6h (HACE), nifedipine 30 mg ER q12h (HAPE), portable hyperbaric chamber (Gamow bag).

\medterm{Anaphylaxis}-- A severe, potentially life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by rapid onset of multisystem involvement, primarily affecting the skin, respiratory tract, cardiovascular system, and gastrointestinal tract. It is mediated by IgE-dependent mast cell and basophil degranulation (Type I hypersensitivity) or by direct mast cell activation (non-IgE mediated, e.g., radiocontrast, opioids, vancomycin). Common triggers: foods (peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, fish, eggs, milk, soy, wheat), medications (penicillins, NSAIDs, neuromuscular blocking agents, radiocontrast), insect stings (Hymenoptera: bees, wasps, fire ants), and exercise (exercise-induced anaphylaxis, often fooddependent). Clinical criteria: acute onset (minutes to hours) with involvement of skin/mucosa PLUS respiratory compromise (wheeze, stridor, dyspnea, hypoxia) OR reduced blood pressure (systolic less than 90 mmHg in adults, or greater than 30 percent decrease from baseline) OR gastrointestinal symptoms (vomiting, crampy abdominal pain) with skin involvement. Management: immediate intramuscular epinephrine (0.3-0.5 mg of 1:1,000 solution in the mid-outer thigh, repeat every 5-15 minutes as needed) is the first-line and most critical intervention. Adjunctive treatments: high-flow oxygen, IV fluids (normal saline or lactated Ringer’s for hypotension), H1 antihistamine (diphenhydramine 25-50 mg IV), H2 antihistamine (famotidine 20 mg IV), corticosteroids (methylprednisolone 125 mg IV or prednisone 40-60 mg PO), and albuterol nebulizer for bronchospasm. Patients should be observed for at least 4-6 hours (biphasic reaction occurs in 1-20 percent of cases). All patients discharged after anaphylaxis should receive an epinephrine auto-injector and referral to an allergist.

\medterm{Asphyxia}-- A life-threatening lack of oxygen due to drowning, choking, or an obstruction of the airways.

\medterm{Aspiration}-- The inhalation of foreign material (oropharyngeal secretions, gastric contents, food, liquid, or blood) into the larynx and lower respiratory tract. Aspiration pneumonitis (Mendelson syndrome) is a chemical lung injury caused by acidic gastric fluid. Aspiration pneumonia is an infectious lung process developing after inhalation of colonized oral secretions. Risk factors: impaired consciousness (alcohol intoxication, stroke, seizure, general anesthesia), dysphagia, enteral tube feeding, and vomiting. Clinical features: sudden cough, dyspnea, wheezing, hypoxemia, fever, and infiltrate on chest radiograph (commonly right lower lobe due to steeper right mainstem bronchus angle). Management: airway suctioning, supplemental oxygen, bronchodilators, and empiric antibiotics for aspiration pneumonia.

\medterm{Avulsion}-- An injury in which tissue is forcibly torn from the body by shearing or pulling. Common forms include dental, fingertip, nail, and degloving avulsions. Management focuses on hemorrhage control, debridement, tissue preservation, and urgent consultation. Avulsed teeth should be reimplanted within 60 minutes, handled by the crown, and stored in milk, saline, or saliva.

\medterm{Bag-Mask Ventilation}-- Manual ventilation of a non-breathing or inadequately breathing patient using a self-inflating bag (AMBU) attached to a mask sealed over the face. Effective technique: correct mask size, jaw thrust/head extension, two-person technique (two-hand seal), adequate tidal volumes with ETCO2 monitoring. Indicated in respiratory failure, arrest, or pre-intubation; complications: gastric insufflation, aspiration, barotrauma. Used for brief support and as a bridge to a definitive airway.

\medterm{Basic Life Support (BLS)}-- Level of medical care used for victims of life-threatening illnesses or injuries until full medical care can be provided. Focuses on the High-Quality CPR compression-to-ventilation ratio (30:2 in adults), chest compression depth (2-2.4 inches, 100-120/min), early use of an Automated External Defibrillator (AED), and relief of foreign body airway obstruction.

\medterm{Battle Sign (Mastoid Ecchymosis)}-- Ecchymosis (bruising) over the mastoid process behind the ear. Pathognomonic sign of a basilar skull fracture, specifically involving the petrous temporal bone. Arises from extravasation of blood along the path of the posterior auricular artery. Typically appears 1-3 days after traumatic head injury. Often accompanied by periorbital ecchymosis ("raccoon eyes"), hemotympanum, CSF rhinorrhea, or CSF otorrhea. Requires non-contrast CT of the head and immediate neurosurgical consultation. Lumbar puncture and nasogastric tube insertion are contraindicated.

\medterm{Bends (Decompression Sickness)}-- A condition resulting from rapid ascent from deep water or pressurized environment, causing dissolved inert gases (primarily nitrogen) to form bubbles in tissues and blood. Symptoms range from joint pain (the bends), skin rash (cutis marmorata), and neurologic deficits (paresthesias, weakness, confusion) to respiratory distress (the chokes) and cardiovascular collapse. Treatment: 100\% oxygen and recompression in a hyperbaric chamber. Prevention: staged ascent with decompression stops and adherence to dive tables or computers.

\medterm{Bite Wound}-- A tissue injury inflicted by animal or human teeth that introduces oral and skin flora into the wound. Cat bites involve Pasteurella multocida, dog bites Capnocytophaga canimorsus, and human bites Eikenella corrodens. Hand, face, and genital wounds are high-risk. Management includes irrigation, debridement, tetanus and rabies prophylaxis, amoxicillin-clavulanate for high-risk bites, and delayed suturing on the hand.

\medterm{Bites and Stings}-- Injuries caused by the biting or stinging apparatus of arthropods (insects, spiders, ticks), marine animals, snakes, and mammals. Insect stings (bees, wasps, hornets, ants): local reaction (pain, erythema, edema, pruritus at sting site, self-limited over hours to days); large local reaction (extensive swelling spreading beyond the sting site, diameter >10 cm, peaking at 24-48 hours, resolves in 5-10 days); systemic allergic reaction/anaphylaxis (urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension, cardiovascular collapse — medical emergency requiring intramuscular epinephrine). Management: remove stinger (scrape, do not squeeze), cold compress, antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine), and NSAIDs for pain. Anaphylaxis: IM epinephrine (0.3-0.5 mg, anterolateral thigh, repeat q5-15min as needed), IV fluids, supplemental oxygen, and hospital observation. Venom immunotherapy for patients with prior systemic reactions. Tick bites: Lyme disease (Borrelia burgdorferi, erythema migrans rash), Rocky Mountain spotted fever, and tick-borne encephalitis. Remove tick with fine-tipped tweezers, grasp as close to skin as possible, pull straight upward. No folklore remedies (matches, petroleum jelly, nail polish). Snake bites: pit vipers (rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths) cause local tissue destruction, coagulopathy, and systemic effects; treatment is antivenom (Fab fragments). Coral snakes cause neurotoxicity (descending paralysis, respiratory failure). Management: immobilisation, splinting, pressure immobilisation bandage for elapid bites (not for pit viper bites, which increases local necrosis). Mammal bites (dogs, cats, humans): high risk of infection (Pasteurella multocida in cat bites, Capnocytophaga canimorsus in dog bites, Eikenella corrodens in human bites). Management: wound irrigation, debridement, rabies prophylaxis if indicated, tetanus vaccination update, and prophylactic antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate) for high-risk bites (cat, hand, deep, delayed presentation, immunosuppressed). Marine stings (jellyfish, Portuguese man-of-war): nematocyst discharge causes painful, linear, urticarial eruptions. Management: rinse with vinegar (acetic acid) to inactivate nematocysts, remove tentacles with gloved hands, hot water immersion (45°C for 20 minutes) for pain, and antivenom for box jellyfish (Chironex fleckeri) stings.

\medterm{Blunt Trauma}-- Injury caused by a blunt impact without skin penetration, most often from traffic collisions, falls, assaults, or blast waves. Mechanisms include direct impact, deceleration shear, and crush, producing solid-organ laceration, hollow viscus rupture, and fractures. Management follows ATLS: primary survey with hemorrhage control, contrast CT imaging, serial reassessment, and laparotomy for the unstable patient.

\medterm{Burns and Scalds}-- Tissue damage caused by thermal, chemical, electrical, or radiation exposure. Burns from dry heat (flame, hot surfaces) and scalds from moist heat (hot liquids, steam) are classified by depth: superficial (first-degree: epidermal involvement, painful erythema without blisters), partial-thickness (second-degree: epidermal and dermal involvement, painful blisters), and full-thickness (third-degree: destruction of all skin layers and subcutaneous tissue, painless leathery or charred appearance). Total Body Surface Area (TBSA) is estimated using the Rule of Nines or Lund-Browder chart. Initial management: airway assessment for inhalation injury, fluid resuscitation using the Parkland formula (4 mL x kg x \%TBSA over 24 hours, half given in the first 8 hours), pain management, sterile dressings, and referral to a burn center.

\medterm{Cardiac Arrest}-- The abrupt cessation of effective cardiac pumping with loss of effective circulation, leading to unconsciousness, apnea, and absent pulses. Causes (H's and T's): hypovolemia, hypoxia, hydrogen ion (acidosis), hypo/hyperkalemia, hypothermia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins, thrombosis (coronary/pulmonary). Rhythms: VF/VT (shockable), asystole/PEA (non-shockable). Management: high-quality CPR (100-120/min, 5-6 cm depth), early defibrillation, epinephrine (1 mg q3-5 min), airway/ventilation, treat reversible causes; post-ROSC targeted temperature management and PCI. Prognosis depends on rapidity of CPR/defibrillation.

\medterm{Cardiac Tamponade}-- A life-threatening condition caused by fluid accumulation within the pericardial space under increased pressure, compromising cardiac diastolic filling and severely reducing cardiac output. Causes: trauma, pericarditis, post-cardiac surgery, aortic dissection, or malignancy. Clinical features: Beck triad (hypotension, muffled heart sounds, jugular venous distension), pulsus paradoxus (systolic BP drop > 10 mmHg during inspiration), tachycardia, and tachypnea. Diagnosis: bedside echocardiography demonstrating pericardial effusion with right ventricular diastolic collapse and IVC plethora. Treatment: emergency pericardiocentesis (ultrasound-guided) or surgical pericardial window, alongside IV fluid boluses to maintain preload while preparing for drainage.

\medterm{Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)}-- An emergency life-saving procedure performed to restore circulation and breathing in a person in cardiac arrest. Basic life support (BLS) sequence: check responsiveness, activate emergency medical services, and begin chest compressions (rate 100--120/min, depth 5--6 cm in adults, allowing full chest recoil). Give rescue breaths at a 30:2 ratio (compressions to breaths). Compression-only CPR is recommended for untrained bystanders. Advanced cardiovascular life support (ACLS) includes defibrillation for shockable rhythms (VF/pulseless VT), advanced airway management, IV/IO vascular access, and pharmacotherapy (epinephrine 1 mg every 3--5 min, amiodarone 300 mg bolus). High-quality CPR with minimal interruptions is the single most important determinant of cardiac arrest survival.

\medterm{Choking}-- Acute upper airway obstruction by a foreign body, producing sudden respiratory distress and risk of asphyxia. Cues include the universal choking sign, inability to speak or cough, stridor, and cyanosis. Treatment alternates five back blows and five abdominal thrusts in responsive adults, using chest thrusts in obese or pregnant patients. Complete obstruction with unconsciousness requires CPR and laryngoscopy.

\medterm{Coma}-- A state of profound unconsciousness characterized by the inability to wake up, lack of awareness, and absence of voluntary responses to external stimuli. Causes are categorized as structural CNS lesions (stroke, hemorrhage, trauma, brain tumor, herniation) or metabolic/toxic encephalopathies (hypoxia, hypoglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, hepatic encephalopathy, drug overdose, severe hyponatremia, sepsis). Assessment: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS < 8 indicates severe impairment requiring endotracheal intubation for airway protection), pupillary light reflex, corneal reflex, and doll's eye (oculocephalic) reflex. Emergency management: immediate ABCs, administration of the 'coma cocktail' if indicated (dextrose for hypoglycemia, thiamine, naloxone for opioid toxicity), non-contrast head CT, and treatment of the underlying cause.

\medterm{Cricothyrotomy}-- An emergency surgical airway created by incising the cricothyroid membrane to place a cuffed tube, used when orotracheal intubation is impossible. Indications include severe facial trauma, upper airway obstruction, hemorrhage, and the failed airway. It is the final rescue airway in adults. Needle cricothyrotomy with jet ventilation is generally preferred in young children.

\medterm{Crush Injury}-- Tissue damage from prolonged compression of a body part by a heavy object, producing ischemia, reperfusion, and rhabdomyolysis. Compartment syndrome is a frequent threat. Crush syndrome adds hyperkalemia, acidosis, myoglobinuric acute kidney injury, arrhythmia, and coagulopathy. Priorities include fluid resuscitation begun before releasing the compressing object, fasciotomy, urine alkalinization, and treatment of hyperkalemia.

\medterm{Defibrillation}-- The delivery of a controlled electrical shock to the heart to terminate life-threatening arrhythmias, specifically ventricular fibrillation (VF) and pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT), allowing the sinoatrial node to resume normal rhythm. Defibrillation is time-critical: survival decreases by 7--10\% per minute without it. Automated external defibrillators (AEDs) analyze rhythm and deliver shocks with voice prompts for bystander use. Biphasic waveforms (150--200 J) are more effective than monophasic (360 J) with less myocardial damage. For shockable rhythms: defibrillate immediately, then resume CPR for 2 minutes before rhythm check. Energy selection: adult 120--200 J biphasic; pediatric 2--4 J/kg.

\medterm{Dehydration}-- A state of total body water depletion resulting from excessive fluid loss, inadequate fluid intake, or both. Causes: gastrointestinal losses (vomiting, diarrhea), renal losses (diabetic ketoacidosis, diabetes insipidus, diuretic overuse), skin losses (burns, excessive sweating), or inadequate intake. Classified by severity: mild (3--5\% body weight loss), moderate (6--9\%), and severe ($\ge 10\%$, leading to hypovolemic shock). Clinical signs: dry mucous membranes, decreased skin turgor, sunken eyes, tachycardia, hypotension, oliguria, and altered mental status. Management: oral rehydration therapy with glucose-electrolyte solutions for mild-to-moderate dehydration; rapid IV fluid resuscitation (isotonic 0.9\% normal saline or lactated Ringer's) for severe dehydration or shock.

\medterm{Disaster Medicine}-- The branch of emergency medicine addressing prevention, preparedness, response, and recovery for mass-casualty events. Triage systems include START and SALT, with red, yellow, green, and black tagging by acuity. The guiding ethic is the greatest good for the greatest number, reversing routine single-patient priorities. Effective response depends on incident command, mutual aid, and drills.

\medterm{Emergency Department}-- The hospital unit providing continuous acute care to unscheduled patients with illness or injury. Staffed by emergency physicians and nurses, it uses triage systems such as the Emergency Severity Index to prioritize by acuity. Core functions are resuscitation, diagnosis, stabilization, treatment, and disposition. Common challenges include overcrowding, boarding, and ambulance diversion.

\medterm{Emergency Ultrasound}-- Bedside ultrasound performed and interpreted by the emergency physician to answer focused clinical questions. Applications include FAST for trauma, cardiac assessment in arrest or shock, suspected aneurysm, ectopic pregnancy, hydronephrosis, biliary disease, and procedural guidance. Advantages are speed, portability, and absence of radiation; limitations include operator dependence and restricted acoustic windows.

\medterm{External Bleeding}-- Visible blood loss from the body surface from arteries, veins, or capillaries, usually due to laceration or penetrating injury. Control follows the Stop the Bleed approach: firm direct pressure, wound packing, and tourniquet use for exsanguinating extremity hemorrhage. Loss exceeding roughly 1500 mL or uncontrolled hemorrhage prompts blood products and massive transfusion protocol, with operative or angiographic intervention if persistent.

\medterm{Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma (FAST)}-- A bedside ultrasound examination in trauma to detect free intraperitoneal/pericardial fluid (hemorrhage). Views: right upper quadrant (Morison pouch), left upper quadrant (splenorenal), pelvic (Pouch of Douglas/rectovesical), and subxiphoid pericardial. Positive findings prompt laparotomy in unstable patients. Sensitivity for hemoperitoneum is good; the extended FAST (eFAST) adds pneumothorax assessment. Rapid, repeatable, and portable; a core component of ATLS shock evaluation.

\medterm{Foreign Body Aspiration}-- Inhalation of a foreign object into the airway, most common in young children (coins, nuts, small toys) and elderly. Features: sudden choking, cough, wheeze (often unilateral), decreased breath sounds, respiratory distress; chronic aspiration may present with recurrent pneumonia. Diagnosis: history, chest X-ray (air trapping, atelectasis, radiopaque object), CT, bronchoscopy. Management: immediate Heimlich maneuver (choking adult/infant) for complete obstruction; rigid bronchoscopy for retrieval; surgery rarely. Complications: pneumonitis, lung abscess, asphyxia.

\medterm{Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)}-- A standardized scale assessing level of consciousness after brain injury, scoring eye opening (1-4), verbal response (1-5), and motor response (1-6) for a total of 3-15. Severity: 13-15 mild, 9-12 moderate, 8 or less severe (coma). Used to triage, monitor trends, and predict outcome; the motor component is the most predictive. Limitations: intubation (verbal untestable), alcohol, and drugs. Serial GCS assessment guides management (e.g., need for intubation, neurosurgery).

\medterm{Head Injury}-- Trauma to the scalp, skull, or brain; a spectrum from mild concussion to severe traumatic brain injury (TBI). Classification: scalp lacerations, skull fractures (linear, depressed, basilar), and intracranial hemorrhage (epidural, subdural, subarachnoid, intraparenchymal), diffuse axonal injury, cerebral contusion. Presentation: headache, vomiting, amnesia, loss of consciousness, focal deficits, altered mental status. Red flags: worsening GCS, unequal pupils, seizure, coagulopathy. Management: ABCs, immobilization, CT scan (per criteria), neurosurgical consultation for mass lesions; observation and CT for mild TBI. Basilar skull signs: raccoon eyes, Battle sign, CSF rhinorrhea/otorrhea.

\medterm{Heat-Related Illnesses (Heat Stroke and Heat Exhaustion)}-- Heat-related illnesses range from mild (heat cramps, heat edema, heat syncope) to severe (heat exhaustion, heat stroke). Heat exhaustion: hypovolemia and electrolyte depletion from excessive sweating (headache, dizziness, nausea, fatigue, core temp $37\text{--}40^\circ\text{C}$). Heat stroke: life-threatening emergency defined by core temperature $>40^\circ\text{C}$ ($104^\circ\text{F}$) accompanied by central nervous system dysfunction (altered mental status, delirium, seizures, coma). Classified as classic (passive, occurring in elderly during heat waves) or exertional (strenuous physical activity in hot humid weather). Pathophysiology: thermoregulatory failure leading to direct cellular toxicity, SIRS, rhabdomyolysis, AKI, ARDS, DIC, and multi-organ failure. Treatment: immediate aggressive cooling (cold water immersion, evaporative misting and fan, ice packs to groin/axillae/neck, cold IV fluids) targeting core temperature $<39^\circ\text{C}$ within 30 minutes.

\medterm{Hyperkalemia}-- Serum potassium above 5.5 mEq/L (severe >6.5), a life-threatening electrolyte emergency. Causes: renal failure, potassium-sparing diuretics, ACE-I/ARB, tumor lysis, rhabdomyolysis, Addison disease, metabolic acidosis, excess intake. ECG changes: peaked T waves, prolonged PR, widened QRS, loss of P waves, sine wave, VF/asystole. Treatment: stabilize myocardium (IV calcium gluconate/chloride), shift into cells (insulin + glucose, beta-agonists, sodium bicarbonate if acidotic), and remove potassium (diuretics, cation exchange resins, dialysis). Continuous ECG monitoring; treat empirically if ECG changes are present.

\medterm{Hyperkalemic Emergency}-- Severe hyperkalemia (serum potassium > 6.5 mEq/L) or any hyperkalemia accompanied by ECG changes represents a life-threatening cardiac emergency. ECG progression: peaked T waves, prolonged PR interval, flattened P waves, QRS widening, sine-wave pattern, and ventricular fibrillation/asystole. Staged emergency treatment: (1) Cardiac membrane stabilization: IV calcium gluconate 1--2 g or calcium chloride 1 g over 2--5 minutes; (2) Intracellular potassium shift: IV regular insulin 10 units with 50\% dextrose (D50W), inhaled albuterol 10--20 mg, and IV sodium bicarbonate (if metabolic acidosis present); (3) Potassium elimination: loop diuretics (furosemide), gastrointestinal cation exchangers (sodium zirconium cyclosilicate, patiromer), or emergency hemodialysis.

\medterm{Hyperpyrexia}-- Extremely elevated body temperature >41.5°C (106.7°F), a medical emergency. Causes: heat stroke (environmental, classic or exertional, with CNS dysfunction), malignant hyperthermia (anesthesia trigger: succinylcholine, volatile anesthetics, treat with dantrolene), neuroleptic malignant syndrome (antipsychotics, rigidity, autonomic instability, elevated CK, treat with dantrolene and bromocriptine), serotonin syndrome (SSRIs/MAOIs, clonus, hyperreflexia, agitation, treat with cyproheptadine and supportive care), CNS infection/injury, and thyrotoxicosis. Management: ABCs, immediate cooling (evaporative, ice packs, cold IV fluids, cooling blanket), dantrolene, and treatment of underlying cause. Remove from heat source.

\medterm{Hypothermia}-- A medical emergency defined by a core body temperature below $35^\circ\text{C}$ ($95^\circ\text{F}$). Classified as mild ($32\text{--}35^\circ\text{C}$: shivering, dysarthria, apathy), moderate ($28\text{--}32^\circ\text{C}$: cessation of shivering, delirium, Osborn J waves on ECG), and severe ($<28^\circ\text{C}$: coma, fixed pupils, severe bradycardia, ventricular fibrillation, or asystole). Evaluation includes rectal or esophageal core temperature monitoring and ECG. Management principles: gentle handling to avoid triggering ventricular fibrillation; passive external rewarming for mild cases; active external and internal rewarming (warmed IV fluids $40\text{--}42^\circ\text{C}$, heated humidified oxygen, cavity lavage, ECMO/cardiopulmonary bypass) for moderate-to-severe hypothermia. Patients should not be pronounced dead until rewarmed to $>32^\circ\text{C}$ ('not dead until warm and dead').

\medterm{Hypovolemic Shock}-- A state of inadequate organ tissue perfusion resulting from severe intravascular volume loss (>15-30\% of circulating volume). Causes: acute hemorrhage (trauma, GI bleeding) or non-hemorrhagic fluid loss (severe vomiting, diarrhea, burns, dehydration). Clinical signs: tachycardia, hypotension, cold clammy extremities, oliguria, altered mental status. Treatment: rapid IV isotonic crystalloids (Normal Saline/Lactated Ringer's), blood product transfusion (packed red blood cells, FFP, platelets in 1:1:1 ratio for massive hemorrhage), and source control.

\medterm{ICU (Intensive Care Unit)}-- A specialized hospital unit providing continuous monitoring and advanced organ support for critically ill patients. ICU admission criteria include: airway compromise, respiratory failure (PaO$_2$/FiO$_2$ <300), hemodynamic instability (MAP <65 despite fluids), severe metabolic disturbance, and multi-organ dysfunction. Staffing comprises a multidisciplinary team: intensivists, ICU nurses, physiotherapists, pharmacists, and dietitians. Core interventions: mechanical ventilation, vasopressors/inotropes, renal replacement therapy, central venous and arterial line monitoring, and nutritional support. Prognostic scoring systems (APACHE II, SOFA, SAPS II) quantify mortality risk and guide clinical decision-making. Common ICU complications: ventilator-associated pneumonia, catheter-related bloodstream infection, pressure injuries, and ICU-acquired weakness.

\medterm{Insect Sting}-- Tissue injury and envenomation by Hymenoptera (bees, wasps, hornets, fire ants). Local reactions produce pain, erythema, edema, and pruritus. Systemic anaphylaxis with bronchospasm, hypotension, and urticaria requires immediate intramuscular epinephrine 0.3 to 0.5 mg and observation for biphasic reactions. Local care includes stinger removal by scraping, cold compresses, antihistamines, and referral for venom immunotherapy in systemic reactors.

\medterm{Internal Bleeding}-- Hemorrhage within body cavities, not visible externally, most commonly in the chest, abdomen, retroperitoneum, or skull. Clues include hypotension, tachycardia, falling hematocrit, abdominal distension, dullness to chest percussion, and altered mental status. Diagnosis relies on FAST ultrasound, contrast CT, and peritoneal aspiration in unstable patients. Management combines blood-product resuscitation with source control via embolization or surgery.

\medterm{Intubation}-- Placement of an endotracheal tube (ETT) into the trachea to secure the airway, providing a definitive airway for ventilation, oxygenation, and airway protection. Indications: airway obstruction, respiratory failure, inadequate oxygenation/ventilation, GCS 8 or less, cardiac arrest. Procedure: preoxygenation, rapid-sequence intubation (RSI—induction + paralysis), laryngoscopy (direct or video), tube placement with cuff inflation, confirmation (ETCO2, capnography, bilateral breath sounds, CXR), securement. Complications: esophageal intubation, aspiration, dental trauma, hypotension, hypoxia. Failed airway requires backup (LMA, cricothyroidotomy).

\medterm{Intubation (Endotracheal)}-- The placement of a flexible plastic tube into the trachea via the mouth (orotracheal) or nose (nasotracheal) to secure an airway, maintain patency, permit mechanical ventilation, and prevent aspiration. Rapid Sequence Intubation (RSI) utilizes an induction agent (etomidate, propofol, ketamine) followed immediately by a neuromuscular blocker (succinylcholine, rocuronium).

\medterm{Major Trauma}-- Injury of significant severity (often multiple injuries) causing physiologic derangement and risk of death or disability; managed per ATLS: primary survey (A-E: airway, breathing, circulation with hemorrhage control, disability, exposure/environment), then secondary survey (head-to-toe examination), with adjuncts (FAST, X-ray, CT). Major trauma mechanisms: road traffic collisions, falls, penetrating injury, blast. Priorities: identify and treat life-threatening injuries (tension pneumothorax, massive hemorrhage, cardiac tamponade), early transfusion (massive hemorrhage protocol), and transfer to a trauma center.

\medterm{Mass Casualty}-- An incident in which casualties exceed immediately available resources, requiring disaster protocols and altered standards of care. Field management integrates START triage, patient distribution across facilities, and an incident command structure. Repeated re-triage and reassessment of resources are mandatory. The goal is the greatest good for the greatest number, with attention to provider safety and support.

\medterm{Multi-Organ Failure}-- Progressive dysfunction of two or more organ systems after an overwhelming insult such as sepsis, trauma, hemorrhagic shock, burns, or pancreatitis. Severity is quantified by the SOFA score across respiratory, coagulation, hepatic, cardiovascular, neurologic, and renal domains. Management is supportive intensive care with ventilation, renal replacement, and vasoactive support. Mortality rises steeply with the number of failing organs.

\medterm{Multiple Trauma}-- Trauma involving injury to more than one body region or system (polytrauma); common in high-energy mechanisms (RTC, falls, blast). Associated with high mortality from hemorrhage, head injury, and multi-organ failure. Managed with systematic assessment (ATLS primary and secondary surveys), injury severity scoring (ISS), early hemorrhage control (tourniquets, pelvic binder, REBOA/surgery), damage-control resuscitation (permissive hypotension, balanced transfusion), and definitive surgical management. Outcomes depend on prehospital time, injury severity, and coordinated multidisciplinary care.

\medterm{Near Drowning}-- A submersion event with survival for at least 24 hours. The principal injury is hypoxia from laryngospasm, aspiration, and respiratory distress syndrome, often compounded by hypothermia. Treatment begins with rescue breathing and CPR, oxygen, and intubation for respiratory failure. Cervical precautions apply only with suspected trauma. Rewarming and serial observation follow for 6 to 24 hours.

\medterm{Needle Decompression}-- An emergency procedure for immediate relief of a tension pneumothorax. Involves inserting a large-bore catheter-over-needle (14-16 gauge, $\ge$8 cm length) into the second intercostal space in the midclavicular line or fifth intercostal space in the anterior axillary line on the affected side. Converts tension pneumothorax into a simple pneumothorax prior to definitive chest tube (tube thoracostomy) placement.

\medterm{Neonatal Emergency}-- A life-threatening condition in the first 28 days of life requiring rapid resuscitation. The neonatal algorithm warms and dries the infant, clears the airway, and assesses heart rate, escalating to positive-pressure ventilation, compressions at a 3:1 ratio, and epinephrine if heart rate stays below 60. Common emergencies include respiratory distress, ductal-dependent cyanotic heart disease, sepsis, hypoglycemia, and bilious vomiting.

\medterm{Nucleus pulposus}-- The gel-like shock-absorbing central portion of each spinal disc.

\medterm{Obstetric Emergencies}-- Life-threatening conditions occurring during pregnancy, labor, or the postpartum period. Includes ectopic pregnancy (ruptured: surgical emergency; stable: methotrexate), preeclampsia/eclampsia (hypertension, proteinuria, seizures; treated with IV magnesium sulfate for seizure prophylaxis and IV labetalol/hydralazine for BP control), placental abruption (painful vaginal bleeding, uterine tenderness), placenta previa (painless vaginal bleeding), and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH: blood loss > 1000 mL, managed with uterine massage, oxytocin, methylergonovine, misoprostol, and tranexamic acid).

\medterm{Oxygen Therapy}-- The administration of supplemental oxygen to treat or prevent hypoxia, a core emergency intervention. Delivery: nasal cannula (low flow, 1-6 L/min), simple face mask (5-10 L/min), non-rebreather mask (10-15 L/min, high concentration), venturi mask (controlled FiO2), high-flow nasal cannula (heated, humidified), and NIV/ventilator. Target SpO2 94-98 percent generally; 88-92 percent in COPD with CO2 retention. Risks: oxygen toxicity (long-term high FiO2), CO2 retention, absorption atelectasis, fire hazard. Titrate to oxygenation while avoiding hyperoxia.

\medterm{Parkland Formula}-- A burn resuscitation formula used to calculate the 24-hour IV fluid requirement (Lactated Ringer's solution) for burn patients: $\text{Fluid Volume (mL)} = 4 \times \text{Body Weight (kg)} \times \text{\% Total Body Surface Area (TBSA) burned (second- and third-degree burns)}$. Half of the calculated volume is administered over the first 8 hours post-burn; the remaining half is infused over the subsequent 16 hours. Targeted to urine output 0.5-1.0 mL/kg/h.

\medterm{Pediatric Emergencies}-- Urgent medical conditions in pediatric patients evaluated using the Pediatric Assessment Triangle (PAT: Appearance, Work of Breathing, Circulation to Skin). Common conditions include severe croup (stridor at rest, treated with nebulized racemic epinephrine and dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg), bronchiolitis (wheezing/crackles in infants, supportive care), pediatric anaphylaxis (epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg IM), febrile seizures, pediatric dehydration, and septic shock (treated with 20 mL/kg isotonic fluid boluses and early broad-spectrum antibiotics).

\medterm{Pelvic Fracture}-- Disruption of the pelvic ring, usually from high-energy collisions or falls, sometimes from low-energy falls in the elderly. The Young-Burgess classification distinguishes lateral, anteroposterior, vertical-shear, and combined patterns that determine stability and hemorrhage risk. Retroperitoneal hemorrhage is a leading cause of preventable traumatic death. Initial management includes a pelvic binder, early transfusion, and angiographic embolization.

\medterm{Penetrating Trauma}-- Injury from a foreign object such as a bullet or knife that breaches the skin and traverses tissue. Severity depends on kinetic energy, tissue density, yaw, and fragmentation, producing a permanent tract and a temporary cavity. Evaluation addresses entry, exit, and trajectory. Management ranges from local wound care to operative exploration, with tetanus prophylaxis and antibiotics.

\medterm{Pericardiocentesis}-- Emergency ultrasound-guided aspiration of fluid from the pericardial sac to relieve cardiac tamponade. Performed via subxiphoid approach (needle angled at $30\text{--}45^\circ$ toward the left shoulder) or parasternal approach. Diagnostic and therapeutic, rapidly improving stroke volume and systemic blood pressure.

\medterm{Periorbital Ecchymosis (Raccoon Eyes)}-- Bilateral periorbital ecchymosis (dark purplish discoloration around the eyes) sparing the tarsal plate. Classic physical exam sign of a basilar skull fracture involving the anterior cranial fossa (cribriform plate or orbital roof). Arises from tracking of venous blood into periorbital soft tissues. Delayed presentation (1-3 days post-trauma). Accompanied by anosmia, CSF rhinorrhea, or Battle sign. Non-contrast head CT is essential.

\medterm{Point of Care Testing}-- Diagnostic testing performed at the bedside to yield rapid results that guide emergency management. Common applications include glucose, hemoglobin, blood gases with lactate, troponin, D-dimer, urinalysis, pregnancy, and rapid infectious assays. Benefits include reduced turn-around time and faster decisions in stroke, sepsis, and acute coronary syndrome; trade-offs include lower precision and the need for quality assurance and confirmatory testing.

\medterm{Post-traumatic headache}-- A persistent headache resulting from a head or neck injury, sometimes lasting for a year or more.

\medterm{Procedural Sedation}-- Levels: minimal (anxiolysis), moderate (conscious sedation), deep, general anesthesia. Pre-procedure: fasting (2h clear liquids, 6h light meal), ASA class, airway assessment (Mallampati), consent, monitoring (BP, HR, SpO2, ETCO2, ECG), rescue equipment. Agents: propofol (0.5-1 mg/kg bolus, 2575 mcg/kg/min infusion; hypotension, apnea), ketamine (1-2 mg/kg IV, 4-5 mg/kg IM; preserves airway/respiration, emergence reaction, hypertension), midazolam (0.02-0.04 mg/kg; amnesia, respiratory depression with opioids), fentanyl (1-2 mcg/kg; respiratory depression), etomidate (0.2-0.3 mg/kg; hemodynamic stability, myoclonus, adrenal suppression). Reversal: flumazenil (BZD), naloxone (opioids). Discharge: Aldrete score >=9, responsible adult, no driving.

\medterm{Psychiatric Emergencies}-- Acute behavioral disturbances, severe agitation, suicidal ideation, or psychosis posing immediate danger to the patient or others. Management of the acutely agitated patient: verbal de-escalation techniques first-line; pharmacologic restraint if de-escalation fails (IM olanzapine 10 mg, ziprasidone 20 mg, or haloperidol 5 mg plus lorazepam 2 mg). Suicide risk assessment: evaluation of ideation, plan, intent, and means, requiring safety precautions and involuntary psychiatric hold if imminent risk is identified.

\medterm{Pulmonary Embolism}-- Obstruction of pulmonary arteries by thrombus (usually from DVT). Risk factors: Virchow triad (stasis, hypercoagulability, endothelial injury). Wells score (clinical probability), PERC rule (low risk exclusion). D-dimer (age-adjusted: age x10 ug/L if >50) for low/intermediate probability. CTPA (gold standard); VQ scan (pregnant, contrast allergy); echocardiography (RV strain: McConnell sign, 60/60 sign, TAPSE). Risk stratification: massive (hemodynamic instability, SBP <90 or vasopressors), submassive (normotensive + RV dysfunction + biomarker elevation), low-risk (normotensive, no RV dysfunction). Management: anticoagulation (LMWH, DOAC, UFH, warfarin). Massive PE: systemic thrombolysis (alteplase 100 mg over 2h, tenecteplase 0.5 mg/kg single bolus) if no contraindication; catheter-directed thrombolysis/mechanical thrombectomy if contraindication; surgical embolectomy if failed. Submassive: consider catheter-directed therapy if clinical deterioration. IVC filter if anticoagulation contraindicated.

\medterm{Puncture Wound}-- A deep, narrow wound from a sharp object such as a nail, needle, or tooth that carries bacteria deeply into tissue. Foot and hand punctures risk osteomyelitis and septic arthritis. Pseudomonas is the classic pathogen in plantar punctures through rubber soles. Management includes irrigation, debridement, tetanus prophylaxis, and antibiotics for deep or contaminated wounds.

\medterm{Replantation}-- Surgical reattachment of a completely amputated part, usually a digit or hand, to restore function. Favorable indications include the thumb, multiple digits, the hand at wrist level, and any part in a child. Contraindications include severe crush, contamination, comorbidity, and ischemia beyond roughly 6 hours. The part is wrapped in saline gauze, sealed, and iced for transfer.

\medterm{Resuscitation}-- The emergency restoration of physiologic function after cardiac or respiratory arrest, severe hemorrhage, or shock. Components: recognition and call for help, high-quality CPR, defibrillation for shockable rhythms, airway/ventilation, IV/IO access, medications (epinephrine, amiodarone), and treatment of reversible causes. Includes fluid/blood resuscitation in hemorrhagic shock (massive transfusion, balanced products), and post-resuscitation care (targeted temperature management, oxygenation, perfusion). Follows AHA/BLS/ACLS/PALS and ATLS algorithms; goal is return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and neurologically intact survival.

\medterm{Sepsis}-- Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Defined clinically by a change in Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score $\ge$2 points. Septic shock: subset of sepsis with persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain mean arterial pressure (MAP) $\ge$65 mmHg and serum lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation. Emergency management: 1-hour bundle (measure lactate, obtain blood cultures, administer broad-spectrum antibiotics, infuse 30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension/lactate $\ge$4, apply vasopressors like norepinephrine).

\medterm{Shock}-- A state of inadequate tissue perfusion and oxygenation leading to cellular dysfunction and organ failure; classified by cause. Hypovolemic (hemorrhage, dehydration), cardiogenic (MI, arrhythmia, cardiomyopathy), obstructive (tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, pulmonary embolism), and distributive (septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic). Features: hypotension, tachycardia, altered mental status, cool/clammy skin (or warm in septic), oliguria, lactic acidosis. Management: ABCs, treat underlying cause, restore perfusion (IV fluids/blood for hypovolemic, vasopressors for distributive, inotropes for cardiogenic), monitor lactate. Early recognition and goal-directed therapy improve outcomes.

\medterm{SIRS}-- Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome, a clinical response to a nonspecific insult (infection, trauma, pancreatitis, burns, autoimmune). Diagnosis requires \(\ge\)2 of: temperature >38°C or <36°C, heart rate >90 bpm, respiratory rate >20 breaths/min or PaCO2 <32 mmHg, WBC >12,000 or <4,000 or >10\% bands. SIRS is non-specific but was historically used as a screening tool for sepsis (qSOFA and SOFA scores are now preferred). Sepsis: SIRS + confirmed infection. Severe sepsis: sepsis + organ dysfunction. Septic shock: sepsis + hypotension despite adequate fluid resuscitation. Management: source control, IV fluids, vasopressors, and antibiotics.

\medterm{Snake Bite}-- Envenomation by Crotalidae (pit vipers) or Elapidae (coral snakes). Crotalid venom causes local tissue destruction and consumptive coagulopathy; elapid venom causes neurotoxicity with descending paralysis and respiratory failure. Management includes immobilizing the limb at heart level, removing constricting items, and avoiding tourniquets, ice, and incision. Treatment uses Crotalidae polyvalent immune Fab antivenom, analgesia, and fluids, with poison control consultation.

\medterm{Spider Bite}-- Envenomation by Loxosceles (brown recluse) or Latrodectus (black widow) species. Loxosceles venom causes a dermonecrotic lesion that may progress to an eschar with hemolysis. Latrodectus venom causes severe muscle spasm, abdominal rigidity, hypertension, and diaphoresis resembling an acute abdomen. Management is supportive with wound care, analgesia, opioids, benzodiazepines, tetanus update, and antivenom in severe cases.

\medterm{Status Epilepticus}-- A continuous seizure lasting more than 5 minutes or repeated seizures without recovery of consciousness between them; a medical emergency with high morbidity/mortality. Types: generalized convulsive (most common, dangerous) and non-convulsive. Causes: medication non-compliance, epilepsy, stroke, infection (encephalitis), metabolic, trauma, alcohol withdrawal, tumor. Management: ABCs, glucose/thiamine, first-line benzodiazepine (lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV, midazolam IM), second-line anticonvulsants (fosphenytoin, valproate, levetiracetam), third-line anesthetic doses (propofol, midazolam infusion) with EEG monitoring for refractory status. Treat cause and investigate (CT, LP, labs).

\medterm{Sudden Cardiac Death}-- Unexpected death from a cardiac cause, generally within one hour of symptom onset or, if unwitnessed, within 24 hours of last being seen well. The leading cause in adults is coronary disease producing ventricular fibrillation or pulseless tachycardia. Intervention requires emergency activation, CPR, and prompt defibrillation, followed by targeted temperature management and implantable defibrillators for secondary prevention.

\medterm{Tension Pneumothorax}-- A life-threatening emergency occurring when air enters the pleural space via a one-way valve mechanism, continuously increasing intrapleural pressure, causing complete collapse of the ipsilateral lung, mediastinal shift to the contralateral side, compression of the vena cava, severely compromised venous return, and obstructive shock. Clinical presentation: severe dyspnea, cyanosis, hypotension, tachycardia, absent breath sounds on the affected side, hyperresonance to percussion, tracheal deviation away from the affected side, and distended neck veins. Immediate intervention: emergency needle decompression (14-gauge needle placed in the 2nd intercostal space midclavicular line or 5th intercostal space anterior axillary line) before chest radiograph, followed by tube thoracostomy (chest tube).

\medterm{Toxic Ingestion}-- Exposure to a toxic substance by swallowing, accidental or intentional. Management begins with airway, breathing, and circulatory support, history, and examination for toxidromes (anticholinergic, cholinergic, opioid, sympathomimetic). Decontamination uses activated charcoal within one hour and whole-bowel irrigation for selected agents. Antidotes include naloxone, N-acetylcysteine, fomepizole, and digoxin Fab; hemodialysis applies to salicylates, methanol, and lithium.

\medterm{Toxicology}-- The study of the adverse effects of chemicals and drugs on living organisms, applied clinically as poisoning/toxicology management and analytically in drug testing. Clinical toxicology: recognition and management of poisoning (toxidromes—anticholinergic, cholinergic, opioid, sympathomimetic, sedative-hypnotic), decontamination (activated charcoal, whole-bowel irrigation), enhanced elimination (dialysis, alkalinization), and antidotes (naloxone, flumazenil, N-acetylcysteine, fomepizole). Analytical toxicology: testing blood/urine for drugs of abuse, therapeutic drugs, and poisons. Principles: supportive care first, specific antidotes when indicated, and consultation with poison control.

\medterm{Trauma}-- Physical injury to the body caused by external forces; a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, especially in young people. Mechanisms: blunt (road traffic collision, falls, assaults) and penetrating (stab, gunshot); blast injuries have primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary effects. Trauma care follows ATLS principles: systematic primary survey (A-E), identification and treatment of life threats, resuscitation, secondary survey, and definitive care. Scoring systems (ISS, RTS) assess severity and prognosis. Prevention, rapid transport, and organized trauma systems reduce mortality.

\medterm{Trauma Scoring Systems}-- Tools that quantify the severity of injury to predict outcome and guide triage and research. Anatomic: Injury Severity Score (ISS)—based on Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) of three most injured body regions, 1-75. Physiologic: Revised Trauma Score (RTS)—GCS, systolic BP, respiratory rate. Combined: TRISS—ISS + RTS + age. Used for triage decisions (trauma team activation), outcome prediction (mortality), benchmarking, and research. Limitations: individual variability, weighting differences.

\medterm{Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)}-- Injury to the brain caused by external mechanical force; a spectrum from concussion to severe injury with intracranial hemorrhage or diffuse axonal injury. Mechanisms: acceleration-deceleration, direct impact, penetrating. Types: concussion (transient neurologic dysfunction), contusion, diffuse axonal injury (DAI), epidural hematoma (middle meningeal artery—lucid interval), subdural hematoma (bridging veins—elderly, anticoagulation), subarachnoid hemorrhage, and intracerebral hemorrhage. Presentation: headache, vomiting, amnesia, altered GCS, focal deficits, seizures. Management: ABCs, oxygenation, ICP control (elevate head, hyperosmolar therapy, sedation, decompressive craniectomy), seizure prophylaxis, and neurosurgery for mass lesions; avoid secondary injury (hypoxia, hypotension).

\medterm{Triage}-- The sorting and prioritization of patients according to the severity of their condition to ensure the most efficient use of limited resources, especially in emergencies and mass casualties. Systems: five-level triage (ESI—Emergency Severity Index, levels 1-5 based on resources and acuity), military (immediate, delayed, minimal, expectant), and disaster triage (START—Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment; walking wounded first, color tags red/yellow/green/black). Decisions balance medical need, resource availability, and potential for benefit; dynamic and reassessed.

\medterm{Troponins}-- Proteins found in heart muscle that leak into the circulation during a heart attack or other heart injury.

\medterm{Universal Precautions}-- Infection control measures applied to all patients to prevent transmission of blood-borne and other pathogens, regardless of perceived infection risk: hand hygiene, gloves, masks, eye protection, gowns, and safe handling/disposal of sharps and waste. Assumes all blood and body fluids are potentially infectious (HIV, hepatitis B/C). Standard precautions extend to all body substances, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes. Essential in emergency and surgical settings; reduces occupational exposure and nosocomial infection.

\medterm{Venous Access}-- The establishment of access to the venous system for fluid resuscitation, medication, blood products, and blood sampling. Routes: peripheral venous cannulation (IV—common), intraosseous (IO—emergency when IV fails, tibia/humerus), central venous catheter (CVC—subclavian, internal jugular, femoral—for vasopressors, CVP monitoring, hemodialysis), and ultrasound-guided access. Indications: shock, cardiac arrest, poor peripheral access, long-term therapies, monitoring. Complications: infection, thrombosis, hematoma, arterial puncture, pneumothorax (central), air embolism. Timely, aseptic access is critical in resuscitation.
