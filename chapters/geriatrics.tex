\chapter{Geriatrics \& Gerontology}

\medterm{Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD)}-- Progressive degenerative disease of the central retina (macula) causing bilateral, irreversible loss of central vision in adults aged $>50$ years, while preserving peripheral vision. Classified into dry (non-exudative, 85--90\%, characterized by drusen accumulation beneath the retinal pigment epithelium and geographic atrophy) and wet (exudative, 10--15\%, characterized by choroidal neovascularization leaking blood and fluid into the subretinal space). Risk factors: advanced age, cigarette smoking, hypertension, and genetic susceptibility (CFH gene mutations). Treatment: antioxidant/vitamin supplementation (AREDS2 formula) for intermediate dry AMD; intravitreal anti-VEGF injections (ranibizumab, aflibercept) for wet AMD.

\medterm{Age-Related Physiological Changes}-- Progressive structural and functional decline occurring across organ systems with advancing age. Cardiovascular: decreased arterial compliance, left ventricular hypertrophy, reduced responsiveness to beta-adrenergic stimulation. Respiratory: decreased chest wall compliance, loss of elastic recoil, decreased forced expiratory volume ($\text{FEV}_1$). Renal: loss of functioning nephrons, decreased glomerular filtration rate (GFR declines by ~1~mL/min/year after age 40). Gastrointestinal: slowed gastric emptying, decreased colonic motility. Nervous system: neuronal loss, brain volume reduction, decreased neurotransmitter synthesis. Endocrine: impaired glucose tolerance, decreased growth hormone and sex steroids (andropause/menopause). Immuno-senescence: impaired cell-mediated immunity.

\medterm{Agitation in Dementia}-- Behavioral and psychological symptom of dementia (BPSD) characterized by emotional distress, pacing, aggression, and motor restlessness; non-pharmacological interventions (environmental modification, music therapy) are first-line.

\medterm{Alzheimer Disease}-- The most common neurodegenerative cause of dementia, accounting for approximately sixty to seventy percent of all dementia cases. It is characterized by progressive cognitive decline, memory loss, and functional impairment, with pathologic hallmarks of extracellular amyloid-beta (A-beta) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. The pathophysiology begins decades before clinical symptoms, with accumulation of amyloid-beta (A-beta) plaques, followed by tau hyperphosphorylation, neurofibrillary tangle formation, synaptic dysfunction, neuronal loss, and brain atrophy, particularly affecting the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, and temporoparietal association areas. Clinical presentation includes progressive impairment of short-term memory as the earliest and most prominent feature, followed by deficits in language (anomia, fluent aphasia), visuospatial function (topographical disorientation), executive function (poor judgement, impaired planning), and apraxia. Diagnosis is based on clinical criteria, cognitive testing (MMSE, MoCA), and supportive biomarkers including CSF analysis (reduced A-beta-42, elevated total tau and phospho-tau) and amyloid PET imaging. Management includes acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) for mild-to-moderate disease and the NMDA receptor antagonist memantine for moderate-to-severe disease, along with behavioural interventions and caregiver support.

\medterm{Alzheimer's Disease}-- A progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the most common cause of dementia, accounting for 60 to 80 percent of cases. It is characterized pathologically by accumulation of extracellular amyloid-beta plaques and intracellular tau-containing neurofibrillary tangles in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus, along with progressive neuronal loss and cerebral atrophy. The disease typically begins with insidious memory impairment, particularly of recent events, and progresses over years with declining cognitive function, behavioural changes, and loss of independence. APOE epsilon-4 is the strongest genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer disease. Definitive diagnosis requires pathological examination, though clinical criteria and biomarkers have high diagnostic accuracy.

\medterm{Atypical Disease Presentation}-- Phenomenon in older adults where acute serious medical conditions (e.g., pneumonia, UTI, myocardial infarction) present with non-specific geriatric syndromes (acute delirium, functional decline, anorexia, falls) rather than classic signs like fever or chest pain.

\medterm{Beers Criteria}-- Expert consensus guidelines developed by the American Geriatrics Society (AGS) identifying potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) that should generally be avoided in older adults due to unfavorable risk-benefit ratios. Categories: medications to avoid in most older adults (e.g., first-generation antihistamines, long-acting benzodiazepines, tricyclic antidepressants, NSAIDs, muscle relaxants), medications to avoid in patients with specific diseases or syndromes (e.g., NSAIDs in heart failure), and medications to be used with caution. The criteria aim to reduce adverse drug events, polypharmacy, and healthcare costs in geriatric care.

\medterm{Caregiver Burden}-- Multidimensional strain, exhaustion, and psychological distress experienced by informal family caregivers providing long-term care to frail, chronically ill, or cognitively impaired older adults (particularly those with Alzheimer's disease). Physical, emotional, financial, and social burdens increase the caregiver's risk for depression, anxiety, cardiovascular illness, and premature mortality. Interventions: caregiver education, support groups, respite care services, adult day health programs, and psychological counseling.

\medterm{Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA)}-- Multidisciplinary diagnostic process used to evaluate the medical, psychological, functional, and social capabilities of vulnerable older individuals to create an integrated management plan. Core domains assessed: medical status (comorbidities, polypharmacy, nutrition, vision/hearing), physical function (ADLs, mobility, fall risk), cognitive and affective status (Mini-Mental State Exam, Geriatric Depression Scale), and social/environmental support. Proven to improve functional status, decrease hospital readmissions, and lower institutionalization rates.

\medterm{Debility}-- A state of generalized weakness, loss of strength, and reduced functional capacity, often associated with chronic illness, malnutrition, prolonged bed rest, or advanced age. Debility is a nonspecific term that may reflect underlying conditions such as sarcopenia, cachexia, deconditioning, or frailty. Management focuses on addressing the underlying cause, nutritional support, physical therapy, and gradual mobilization to prevent further functional decline.

\medterm{Delirium}-- Acute, fluctuating decline in attention, awareness, and cognition, typically developing over hours to days. Differentiated from dementia by its sudden onset, fluctuating course, and marked impairment of attention. Subtypes: hyperactive (agitation, hallucinations), hypoactive (lethargy, withdrawal, often missed), and mixed. Common precipitating factors in vulnerable older adults (mnemonic "I WATCH CONFUSED"): Infection (UTI, pneumonia), Withdrawal, Acute metabolic disturbance, Trauma/pain, CNS pathology, Hypoxia, Opioids/medications, Nutrition, Subdural hematoma, Endocrine, and Sensory deprivation. Non-pharmacological management (reorientation, early mobilization, sleep hygiene) is first-line; low-dose antipsychotics are reserved for severe agitation threatening safety.

\medterm{Delirium (Geriatric)}-- Acute, fluctuating disturbance in attention, awareness, and cognition, highly prevalent in hospitalized elderly patients. Triggered by underlying medical illness, infections (UTI, pneumonia), medications (anticholinergics, sedatives), or surgery. Subtypes: Hyperactive, Hypoactive (frequently missed), and Mixed.

\medterm{Dementia}-- Chronic, global, usually progressive impairment of cognitive functions (memory, language, executive function, visuospatial skills, orientation) severe enough to interfere with daily social and occupational functioning. Major etiologies in the elderly: Alzheimer's disease (60--70\%, neurofibrillary tangles and amyloid beta plaques), Vascular dementia (15--20\%, lacunar infarcts, multi-infarct brain injury), Dementia with Lewy Bodies (10--15\%, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, REM sleep disorder), and Frontotemporal dementia (behavioral/personality changes). Reversible causes (hypothyroidism, vitamin $\text{B}_{12}$ deficiency, normal pressure hydrocephalus, neurosyphilis) must be excluded.

\medterm{Deprescribing}-- Planned, supervised process of dose reduction or discontinuation of potentially inappropriate, ineffective, or harmful medications in elderly patients to reduce polypharmacy and lower adverse event risks.

\medterm{Elder Abuse and Neglect}-- Intentional act or failure to act by a caregiver or trusted individual that causes harm or serious risk of harm to a vulnerable older adult. Categories: physical abuse, psychological/emotional abuse, financial exploitation (most common), sexual abuse, neglect (withholding food, shelter, medical care), and self-neglect. Risk factors: cognitive impairment, physical dependency, caregiver stress, isolation, and substance abuse. Signs: unexplained bruises, fractures, malnutrition, poor hygiene, sudden changes in financial assets, and withdrawal. Mandatory reporting laws require healthcare providers to report suspected elder mistreatment to adult protective services.

\medterm{Fall Risk Assessment}-- Systematic clinical evaluation of intrinsic and extrinsic factors predisposing an older adult to falls, which represent a leading cause of injury, hip fractures, loss of independence, and accidental death in geriatrics. Intrinsic risk factors: gait and balance impairment, muscle weakness (sarcopenia), peripheral neuropathy, visual impairment, orthostatic hypotension, cognitive decline, and polypharmacy (particularly sedatives, antihypertensives, and anticholinergics). Extrinsic risk factors: poor lighting, slippery floors, throw rugs, lack of grab bars, and improper footwear. Screening tests: Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, Berg Balance Scale, and functional reach test.

\medterm{Falls in the Elderly}-- Leading cause of accidental injury and death in adults aged 65 and older. Multifactorial etiology: muscle weakness, gait instability, sensory deficits, environmental hazards, and psychotropic/antihypertensive medications.

\medterm{Frailty}-- A clinical syndrome in older adults characterized by decreased physiologic reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors, resulting in increased risk of falls, hospitalization, disability, and death. Fried frailty phenotype: \(\ge\)3 of 5 criteria present — unintentional weight loss (>10 lbs in past year), exhaustion (self-reported), weakness (grip strength), slow gait speed, and low physical activity. Frailty is distinct from disability and comorbidity. Pre-frail: 1-2 criteria. Management: multi-domain intervention (exercise including resistance training, nutritional optimization with protein supplementation, medication review, and management of comorbidities). Frailty is potentially reversible with appropriate intervention.

\medterm{Frailty Syndrome}-- Biologic syndrome of decreased physiological reserve and vulnerability to stressors, resulting from dysregulated multi-system decline. Fried frailty phenotype criteria ($\ge$3 present for diagnosis): unintentional weight loss ($>10$~lbs in past year), exhaustion, muscle weakness (reduced grip strength), slow walking speed, and low physical activity level. Frail older adults are at significantly higher risk for falls, hospitalization, disability, institutionalization, and mortality. Management: progressive resistance exercise training, nutritional optimization (protein supplementation), and reduction of polypharmacy.

\medterm{Frontotemporal Dementia}-- A group of neurodegenerative disorders characterized by progressive degeneration of the frontal and temporal lobes, presenting with personality changes, behavioral abnormalities, language impairment, and cognitive decline. FTD is the most common cause of dementia in patients under sixty years of age and accounts for approximately five to ten percent of all dementia cases. The three main clinical variants are behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD), which presents with progressive personality changes such as apathy, disinhibition, loss of empathy, perseverative behaviours, hyperorality, and dietary changes (bvFTD), semantic variant primary progressive aphasia (svPPA) with loss of word meaning and object knowledge, and non-fluent/agrammatic variant PPA (nfvPPA) with effortful, agrammatic speech. The pathophysiology involves accumulation of abnormal proteins: about 50 percent of FTD cases have tau inclusions (tauopathy), 40 percent have TDP-43 inclusions (TDP-43 proteinopathy), and 10 percent have FUS inclusions. Approximately 30-50 percent of FTD cases have a family history, with autosomal dominant mutations in the MAPT (tau), GRN (progranulin), and C9orf72 genes being the most common. C9orf72 hexanucleotide repeat expansions are the most common genetic cause of both FTD and ALS, and these conditions may co-occur. Diagnosis is by clinical criteria (Rascovsky criteria for bvFTD, Gorno-Tempini criteria for PPA variants), neuropsychological testing, and MRI brain showing focal atrophy of the frontal and/or temporal lobes. There is no disease-modifying treatment; management is symptomatic with SSRIs for behavioural symptoms, antipsychotics for agitation, and speech therapy for language deficits. Prognosis is poor, with median survival of 6-10 years from diagnosis.

\medterm{Functional Incontinence}-- Urinary incontinence in patients with normal lower urinary tract function who are unable to reach the toilet in time due to physical mobility limitations, severe cognitive impairment, or environmental barriers.

\medterm{Geriatric Anesthesia}-- Geriatric patients have decreased physiologic reserve and increased anesthesia risk. Age-related changes include decreased cardiac output, reduced pulmonary function, decreased renal function, altered pharmacokinetics (reduced volume of distribution, decreased hepatic metabolism), and increased brain sensitivity to anesthetics. Frailty assessment predicts postoperative complications. Delirium is a common postoperative complication, particularly in patients with dementia. Minimally invasive technchniques, regional anesthesia, and multimodal analgesia improve outcomes. Preoperative optimisation of comorbidities reduces perioperative risk. Postoperative cognitive dysfunction and functional decline are concerns requiring appropriate discharge planning and follow-up.

\medterm{Geriatric Delirium}-- Acute, fluctuating disturbance in attention, awareness, and cognition, highly prevalent in hospitalized elderly patients. Triggered by underlying medical illness, infections (UTI, pneumonia), medications (anticholinergics, sedatives), or surgery. Subtypes: Hyperactive, Hypoactive (frequently missed), and Mixed.

\medterm{Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS)}-- Validated 30-item (or short 15-item) self-report screening tool specifically designed to detect depression in older adults. Unlike general depression scales, the GDS minimizes somatic symptoms (fatigue, sleep disturbance) that often overlap with normal aging or physical illness, focusing instead on psychological symptoms (feelings of helplessness, hopelessness, loss of interest). A score of $>5$ on the 15-item GDS warrants further clinical evaluation for major depressive disorder.

\medterm{Geriatric Dermatology}-- Geriatric dermatology addresses skin changes and diseases of aging. Senile purpura (easy bruising from fragile vessels). Actinic keratoses (premalignant from sun exposure). Seborrheic keratoses (benign proliferations). Pruritus senilis (generalized itching from xerosis). Skin cancer incidence increases with age. Impaired wound healing and increased infection risk. Polypharmacy increases adverse drug reactions. Careful medication review and skin cancer screening are essential.

\medterm{Geriatric Failure to Thrive}-- Complex syndrome in older adults characterized by weight loss ($>5\%$), decreased appetite, poor nutrition, physical inactivity, depression, and cognitive decline, associated with increased mortality.

\medterm{Geriatrician}-- A physician who specializes in the care of older patients.

\medterm{Geriatric Psychiatry}-- The subspecialty of psychiatry focused on the prevention, evaluation, diagnosis, and treatment of mental and emotional disorders in older adults. Geriatric patients often present atypically, with depression manifesting as somatic complaints and cognitive decline. Common conditions include late-life depression, dementia, delirium, and late-onset psychosis. Polypharmacy, medical comorbidity, and altered drug metabolism complicate treatment.

\medterm{Geriatrics (see Medicine of Ageing)}-- The medical specialty concerned with the health and care of older adults. See Medicine of Ageing.

\medterm{Gerontology \& Geriatrics}-- Gerontology is the multidisciplinary scientific study of the biological, psychological, and social aspects of aging. Geriatrics is the specialized branch of medicine focused on the health care, disease prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of medical conditions in older adults. Core principles of geriatric care emphasize maintaining functional independence, optimizing quality of life, preserving cognition, and minimizing adverse outcomes from polypharmacy and hospitalization.

\medterm{Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADLs)}-- Complex cognitive and physical tasks required to live independently within a community, evaluated using Lawton scale: Using the telephone, Shopping, Food preparation, Housekeeping, Laundry, Mode of transportation, Responsibility for own medications, and Ability to handle finances.

\medterm{Lewy Body Dementia}-- The second most common neurodegenerative cause of dementia after Alzheimer disease and is characterized by the presence of alpha-synuclein-containing Lewy bodies in cortical and subcortical neurons. LBD encompasses two clinical entities: dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), where dementia occurs within one year of the onset of parkinsonism, and Parkinson disease dementia (PDD), where dementia occurs after at least one year of established Parkinson disease. The core clinical features include dementia (typically with prominent fluctuations in cognition and alertness, well-formed recurrent visual hallucinations, and parkinsonism), REM sleep behaviour disorder (acting out dreams, often a prodromal feature), and autonomic dysfunction. Other supportive features include severe neuroleptic sensitivity (a critical clinical point: antipsychotics can precipitate severe rigidity and autonomic instability in LBD), repeated falls, syncope, and depression. The diagnostic criteria include the presence of two or more core features for probable DLB and one core feature for possible DLB. Diagnosis is clinical, supported by dopamine transporter (DaT) SPECT imaging showing reduced presynaptic dopamine uptake in the basal ganglia (the most useful diagnostic test), and polysomnography may confirm REM sleep behaviour disorder. Management includes cholinesterase inhibitors (rivastigmine, donepezil) for cognition and visual hallucinations—these are more effective than in Alzheimer disease—and careful use of atypical antipsychotics (quetiapine, clozapine) for severe hallucinations or agitation, with avoidance of typical antipsychotics due to neuroleptic sensitivity. Levodopa may be used for parkinsonism but has limited efficacy. Prognosis is poor, with progressive decline over several years, parkinsonism worsening, and eventual death from complications of immobility and dysphagia.

\medterm{MIND Diet}-- The MIND diet (Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay) is a hybrid eating pattern combining elements of the Mediterranean and DASH diets, specifically designed to preserve cognitive function and reduce dementia risk. Emphasis is on green leafy vegetables ($\ge$6 servings/week), berries ($\ge$2 servings/week, specifically blueberries and strawberries for their flavonoid content), nuts, whole grains, fish, poultry, olive oil, and wine ($\le$1 glass/day); red meat, butter/margarine, cheese, pastries/sweets, and fried foods are limited. The MIND diet trial (Morris et al.) showed that higher adherence was associated with significantly slower cognitive decline equivalent to 7.5 years of younger age; observational data suggest even moderate adherence reduces Alzheimer disease risk by ~35\%. Protective mechanisms: antioxidant (vitamin E, flavonoids), anti-inflammatory (polyunsaturated fats, polyphenols), and vascular (blood pressure reduction, improved cerebral blood flow) effects.

\medterm{Multi-Infarct Dementia}-- Subtype of vascular dementia resulting from cumulative stroke damage due to multiple recurrent ischemic cerebral cortical and subcortical strokes. Clinical presentation is classically characterized by a stepwise deterioration in cognitive function, where each sudden drop in performance corresponds to a discrete vascular event, accompanied by focal neurological deficits (hemiparesis, pseudobulbar palsy, gait apraxia, exaggerated deep tendon reflexes). Brain MRI demonstrates multiple lacunar and cortical infarcts with extensive white matter hyperintensities (leukoaraiosis). Management focuses on secondary stroke prevention (antithrombotics, blood pressure control, statins).

\medterm{Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus (NPH)}-- Reversible cause of dementia characterized by enlarged cerebral ventricles with normal or transiently elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressure on lumbar puncture. Pathophysiology: impaired CSF absorption by arachnoid villi. Classic triad (mnemonic "Wet, Wobbly, Weird"): (1) Gait apraxia (magnetic, broad-based, short-stepped gait—usually the first and most prominent symptom), (2) Urinary incontinence (urgency and loss of control), and (3) Dementia (subcortical, psychomotor slowing). Brain MRI shows ventriculomegaly out of proportion to sulcal atrophy. Diagnostic test: high-volume lumbar puncture (tap test) showing temporary improvement in gait. Treatment: surgical placement of a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt.

\medterm{Osteoporosis}-- Systemic skeletal disorder characterized by low bone mineral density (BMD), microarchitectural deterioration of bone tissue, and increased bone fragility, predisposing to low-trauma (fragility) fractures of the hip, vertebrae, and distal radius. Most common in postmenopausal women due to estrogen deficiency accelerating osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. Diagnosis: Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scan showing a BMD T-score $\le -2.5$ at the femoral neck or lumbar spine. Prevention and treatment: weight-bearing exercise, calcium and vitamin D supplementation, bisphosphonates (alendronate, zoledronic acid), denosumab, and anabolic agents (teriparatide).

\medterm{Overflow Incontinence}-- Involuntary loss of urine associated with overdistension and incomplete emptying of the bladder, caused by bladder outlet obstruction (BPH) or detrusor underactivity (diabetic neuropathy).

\medterm{Polypharmacy}-- Concurrent use of multiple medications by a single patient, typically defined as taking 5 or more prescription drugs daily. Highly prevalent in older adults with multiple chronic comorbidities. Consequences: increased risk of adverse drug reactions, drug-drug interactions, prescribing cascades (treating drug side effects with additional drugs), cognitive impairment, falls, non-adherence, and hospitalization. Management involves routine medication reconciliation, structured deprescribing, and adherence to guidelines such as the Beers Criteria.

\medterm{Presbycusis}-- Age-related, progressive, bilateral, symmetrical sensorineral hearing loss resulting from degeneration of cochlear hair cells, spiral ganglion neurons, and the stria vascularis in the inner ear. Typically affects high-frequency sounds first, causing difficulty understanding speech in noisy environments and loss of consonant discrimination (e.g., distinguishing "s", "f", "t"). Audiometry demonstrates a characteristic sloping high-frequency hearing loss. Management: hearing aids, assistive listening devices, and auditory rehabilitation.

\medterm{Presbyopia}-- Age-related decline in the eye's accommodative ability, resulting in difficulty focusing on near objects. Pathophysiology: loss of crystalline lens elasticity and decreased power of the ciliary muscle, typically becoming clinically noticeable around age 40--45. Patients present with eye strain, headaches, and the need to hold reading material at arm's length. Corrected with convex reading glasses, bifocal/progressive lenses, or accommodative contact lenses.

\medterm{Pressure Sore (Decubitus Ulcer)}-- Decubitus ulcer (bedsores, pressure ulcers) are localised areas of tissue necrosis caused by prolonged pressure over bony prominences, most commonly in immobile patients. Sites: sacrum (most common), heels, elbows, hips, and occiput. Pathophysiology: pressure exceeding capillary closing pressure (~32 mmHg) causes ischaemia, followed by reperfusion injury, inflammation, and necrosis. Risk factors: immobility, sensory loss, malnutrition, moisture (incontinence), advanced age, and poor perfusion. NPUAP Staging: Stage 1 (non-blanchable erythema), Stage 2 (partial-thickness skin loss), Stage 3 (full-thickness loss without bone exposure), Stage 4 (full-thickness loss with bone/tendon/muscle involvement), Unstageable (eschar or slough covering base), Deep Tissue Injury (purple/maroon intact skin). Management: pressure redistribution (turn q2h, specialized mattresses), wound debridement, moist wound care, infection control, nutritional support, and surgical repair for advanced stages. Prevention is paramount: risk assessment (Braden scale), regular repositioning, pressure-relieving surfaces, skin care, and nutrition.

\medterm{Pressure Ulcer}-- Localized injury to skin and underlying soft tissue, typically over a bony prominence (sacrum, ischium, calcaneus), resulting from sustained pressure or pressure in combination with shear. Staged 1--4 (Stage 1: non-blanchable erythema; Stage 4: full-thickness skin loss exposing bone, muscle, or tendon).

\medterm{Pressure Ulcers (Decubitus Ulcers)}-- Localized injury to the skin and underlying soft tissue, usually over a bony prominence (sacrum, ischium, trochanter, calcaneus), resulting from sustained mechanical pressure combined with friction and shear forces. Staging: Stage 1 (non-blanchable erythema of intact skin), Stage 2 (partial-thickness skin loss with exposed dermis), Stage 3 (full-thickness skin loss with visible subcutaneous fat), Stage 4 (full-thickness skin and tissue loss with exposed muscle, tendon, or bone), Unstageable (obscured by eschar/slough), and Deep Tissue Injury. Risk factors evaluated by the Braden Scale: immobility, incontinence, malnutrition, and impaired sensation. Prevention: repositioning every 2 hours, pressure-relieving mattresses, moisture management, and nutritional support.

\medterm{Sarcopenia}-- Age-associated progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and functional capacity. Primary sarcopenia is driven by intrinsic aging processes (decline in anabolic hormones, motor neuron loss, chronic low-grade inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction); secondary sarcopenia results from disuse, disease, or malnutrition. Diagnosis requires demonstration of low muscle strength (reduced grip strength) combined with low muscle quantity/quality (measured by DXA or BIA). Sarcopenia increases the risk of physical disability, falls, frailty, and mortality. Treatment: progressive resistance exercise training combined with adequate dietary protein intake ($1.2\text{--}1.5\text{ g/kg/day}$).

\medterm{Senile Gait}-- Altered gait pattern commonly observed in very old adults, characterized by decreased walking speed, shortened step length, widened base of support, increased double-support time, and diminished arm swing. Resulting from a combination of subclinical neurological, musculoskeletal, and sensory impairments (decreased proprioception, sarcopenia, joint stiffness, subtle frontal lobe dysfunction). Differentiated from specific pathological gaits (parkinsonian, ataxic, hemiparetic). Evaluation requires screening for reversible causes (cataracts, joint pain, B12 deficiency) and physical therapy gait training.

\medterm{STOPP/START Criteria}-- Evidence-based screening tool for elderly drug regimens. STOPP (Screening Tool of Older Persons' Prescriptions) identifies inappropriate medications; START (Screening Tool to Alert doctors to Right Treatment) identifies omitted evidence-based medications.

\medterm{Stress Incontinence}-- Involuntary urinary leakage occurring during activities that acutely increase intra-abdominal pressure (coughing, sneezing, laughing, exercise), resulting from urethral hypermobility or intrinsic sphincter deficiency.

\medterm{Sundowning Syndrome}-- Neuropsychiatric phenomenon characterized by increased confusion, disorientation, anxiety, agitation, and restlessness appearing in the late afternoon or evening, commonly affecting individuals with Alzheimer's disease or other dementias.

\medterm{Urge Incontinence}-- Involuntary leakage of urine accompanied or immediately preceded by a sudden, intense urge to void, caused by detrusor muscle overactivity ('overactive bladder'). Common in elderly patients; managed with bladder training, antimuscarinics (solifenacin), or beta-3 agonists (mirabegron).

\medterm{Vascular Dementia}-- The second most common cause of dementia after Alzheimer disease and results from cerebrovascular disease causing sufficient brain injury to impair cognitive function. It is caused by large vessel cerebrovascular disease (multi-infarct dementia from multiple cortical and subcortical infarcts), small vessel cerebrovascular disease (subcortical ischemic vascular dementia from lacunar infarcts and white matter changes), strategic single infarct (infarction in a cognitively critical area such as the dominant thalamus, angular gyrus, or medial temporal lobe), or global hypoperfusion injury from cardiac arrest or hypotension causing watershed infarcts between vascular territories. Clinical presentation of vascular dementia is characterized by a stepwise deterioration and a patchy pattern of cognitive deficits, in contrast to the gradual progressive decline of Alzheimer disease. The cognitive profile includes executive dysfunction (impaired planning, organisation, and abstraction) more than memory loss, slowness of processing (bradyphrenia), and subcortical features (apathy, emotional lability, depression). Focal neurological signs (hemiparesis, sensory loss, visual field deficits, pseudobulbar palsy, gait disturbance) are common. The Hachinski ischemic Score helps differentiate vascular dementia from Alzheimer disease. Diagnosis is by clinical assessment, cognitive testing, and neuroimaging (CT or MRI) showing multiple infarcts, extensive white matter disease (leukoaraiosis), or a strategically located infarct. Management includes control of vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, smoking cessation, antiplatelet therapy), and symptomatic treatment of cognitive and behavioural symptoms. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors have limited benefit in vascular dementia, unlike their use in Alzheimer disease. Prognosis is variable and depends on the progression of underlying cerebrovascular disease.
