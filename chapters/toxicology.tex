\chapter{Toxicology}

\medterm{Activated Charcoal}-- Fine black powder produced by controlled pyrolysis of organic materials, processed to yield a massive micro-porous surface area (500--1500~$\text{m}^2/\text{g}$) capable of non-specifically adsorbing toxic substances within the gastrointestinal lumen, preventing systemic absorption. Standard dose: 1~g/kg (typically 50~g in adults), administered orally or via nasogastric tube within 1--2 hours of toxic ingestion. Multi-dose activated charcoal (MDAC) enhances elimination of drugs undergoing enterohepatic or enteroenteric circulation (carbamazepine, dapsone, phenobarbital, quinine, theophylline). Substances poorly adsorbed by charcoal ("PHAILS"): Pesticides/Petroleum, Hydrocarbons, Alcohol/Acids/Alkali, Iron, Lithium, Solvents. Contraindications: unprotected airway (risk of fatal aspiration pneumonitis), bowel obstruction, perforated viscus, or ingestion of corrosive substances.

\medterm{Anticholinergic Toxidrome}-- Clinical toxidrome resulting from competitive inhibition of acetylcholine at central and peripheral muscarinic receptors. Causes: antihistamines (diphenhydramine), tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline), atropine, scopolamine, belladonna alkaloids (Datura stramonium / Jimson weed), and antiparkinsonian drugs. Mnemonics: "Blind as a bat" (mydriasis, loss of accommodation), "Mad as a hatter" (delirium, agitation, hallucinations), "Red as a beet" (flushed skin from cutaneous vasodilation), "Hot as a hare" (hyperthermia from anhidrosis), "Dry as a bone" (dry mouth, dry skin), "Bowel and bladder lose their tone" (paralytic ileus, urinary retention), "And the heart runs alone" (tachycardia). Specific antidote: physostigmine (acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that crosses the blood-brain barrier), indicated for severe central anticholinergic toxicity when ECG shows normal QRS duration.

\medterm{Antidote}-- Any therapeutic agent that neutralizes, counteracts, or reverses the physiological or biochemical toxic effects of a poison. Mechanisms: physical adsorption (activated charcoal), chemical neutralization (digoxin immune Fab), receptor competition/antagonism (naloxone for opioids, flumazenil for benzodiazepines), metabolic inhibition (fomepizole inhibiting alcohol dehydrogenase in methanol/ethylene glycol toxicity), enzyme reactivation (pralidoxime for organophosphates), or substrate replenishment (N-acetylcysteine for acetaminophen, pyridoxine for isoniazid). Availability of specific antidotes is limited to a minor subset of human poisonings, necessitating supportive care as the foundation of clinical toxicology management.

\medterm{Arsenic Poisoning}-- Acute or chronic exposure to arsenic compounds, a metalloid toxin found in contaminated groundwater, industrial processes, pesticides, and wood preservatives. Inactivates pyruvate dehydrogenase by binding to lipoic acid, disrupting cellular ATP production via citric acid cycle inhibition. Acute clinical features: severe garlic-like breath odor, rice-water diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal pain, QT prolongation, torsades de pointes, and hypovolemic shock. Chronic clinical features: hyperpigmentation, hyperkeratosis of palms and soles ("raindrops on a dusty road"), Mees lines (transverse white bands on nails), peripheral sensorimotor neuropathy ("glove-and-stocking"), and increased risk of squamous cell skin carcinoma, lung, and bladder cancer. Treatment: gastric lavage, IV hydration, and chelation therapy with dimercaprol (BAL) acutely, or oral succimer (DMSA) for subacute/chronic exposure.

\medterm{Arsenic Toxicity}-- Heavy metal poisoning from contaminated groundwater or industrial exposure. Acute: severe gastroenteritis ('rice-water' diarrhea), garlic breath odor. Chronic: hyperpigmentation, hyperkeratosis of palms/soles, Mees lines on nails, peripheral neuropathy, and angiosarcoma. Antidote: Dimercaprol (BAL) or Succimer.

\medterm{Aspirin Toxicity (Salicylate Toxicity)}-- Acute or chronic poisoning by acetylsalicylic acid or salicylate-containing products (oil of wintergreen). Pathophysiology: direct stimulation of the medullary respiratory center causes hyperventilation and primary respiratory alkalosis; uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation leads to metabolic acidosis, hyperthermia, and ATP depletion. Classic acid-base disturbance: mixed primary respiratory alkalosis and primary high anion gap metabolic acidosis. Clinical features: tinnitus (early sign), vertigo, nausea, vomiting, tachypnea, pyrexia, confusion, cerebral edema, and coma. Diagnostic testing: elevated serum salicylate levels, arterial blood gas, metabolic panel. Management: activated charcoal, fluid resuscitation, alkalinization of urine with intravenous sodium bicarbonate ($\text{NaHCO}_3$) to trap salicylate ions in renal tubules and enhance urinary elimination, and hemodialysis for severe toxicity (salicylate $>90$~mg/dL, altered mental status, pulmonary edema, or renal failure).

\medterm{Atropine Poisoning / Antidote}-- Belladonna alkaloid that functions as a competitive muscarinic acetylcholine receptor antagonist. As a poison, excessive atropine or ingestion of atropaceous plants (Atropa belladonna, Datura) induces a classic anticholinergic toxidrome (mydriasis, tachycardia, hyperthermia, dry mucous membranes, urinary retention, delirium). As a life-saving antidote, atropine is administered in massive escalating doses (2--5~mg IV repeated every 3--5 minutes) to reverse central and peripheral muscarinic toxicity caused by organophosphate and carbamate insecticide poisonings, nerve agents (sarin, VX), or severe bradycardia. Goal of atropinization: drying of tracheobronchial secretions and reversal of bronchospasm, rather than normalization of heart rate or pupil size.

\medterm{Beta-Blocker Toxicity}-- Overdose of $\\beta$-adrenergic antagonists causing bradycardia, hypotension, AV block, hypoglycemia, and cardiogenic shock. Lipophilic agents (propranolol) cause delirium and seizures. Antidote / Treatment: High-dose IV Glucagon (stimulates adenylate cyclase independently of $\\beta$-receptors), IV calcium, insulin-euglycemia therapy.

\medterm{Calcium Channel Blocker Toxicity}-- Severe overdose of CCBs (verapamil, diltiazem, amlodipine) resulting in profound vasodilation, refractory hypotension, severe bradycardia, and hyperglycemia (due to inhibition of L-type calcium channel-mediated pancreatic insulin release). Treatment: High-Dose Insulin Euglycemia Therapy (HIET), IV Calcium gluconate, lipid emulsion.

\medterm{Carbon Monoxide Poisoning}-- Inhalation toxicity caused by carbon monoxide (CO), a colorless, odorless, tasteless gas generated by incomplete combustion of carbonaceous fuels (house fires, motor vehicle exhaust, faulty gas heaters). Pathophysiology: CO binds to hemoglobin with an affinity 200--250 times greater than oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin (COHb), which shifts the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to the left (Haldane effect), severely impairing tissue oxygen delivery. CO also inhibits cytochrome c oxidase in the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Clinical features: headache (most common early symptom), dizziness, nausea, confusion, seizures, "cherry-red" skin (rare post-mortem finding), and myocardial ischemia. Delayed neuropsychiatric sequelae (DNS) can occur weeks later. Diagnosis: co-oximetry of arterial or venous blood (standard pulse oximetry is falsely normal). Treatment: 100\% normobaric oxygen via non-rebreather mask (reduces COHb half-life from 320 to 80 minutes); hyperbaric oxygen (HBO) therapy for COHb $>25\%$, pregnancy, loss of consciousness, seizures, or cardiac ischemia.

\medterm{Chelation Therapy}-- Administration of chelating agents containing electron-donor ligand groups (thiol, amine, or carboxylate) that form stable, non-toxic coordination complexes with heavy metal ions, facilitating their excretion via urine or bile. Common chelators: Dimercaprol (British Anti-Lewisite, BAL—for acute lead, arsenic, mercury poisoning; IM administration in oil); Succimer (DMSA—water-soluble oral analog of BAL for pediatric lead poisoning); Calcium Disodium EDTA ($\text{CaNa}_2\text{EDTA}$—for severe lead poisoning, given after BAL to prevent brain lead redistribution); Penicillamine (for copper accumulation in Wilson disease, arsenic, lead); Deferoxamine (specific iron chelator); and Deferasirox (oral iron chelator for chronic transfusion overload). Risks: nephrotoxicity, essential trace mineral depletion (zinc, copper), and hypersensitivity reactions.

\medterm{Cholinergic Toxidrome}-- Life-threatening toxidrome resulting from excessive stimulation of muscarinic and nicotinic acetylcholine receptors due to acetylcholinesterase inhibition or direct cholinergic agonist exposure. Causes: organophosphate and carbamate insecticides, nerve agents (sarin, soman, VX), Alzheimer medications (donepezil), and pilocarpine. Muscarinic features (mnemonic "DUMBELS"): Diarrhea, Urination, Miosis, Bradycardia/Bronchospasm/Bronchorrhea, Emesis, Lacrimation, Salivation. (Alternative mnemonic "SLUDGEM": Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI distress, Emesis, Miosis). Bronchorrhea and bronchospasm ("killer B's") are the primary cause of death. Nicotinic features (mnemonic "MTWThF"): Muscle fasciculations, Tachycardia, Weakness, Paralysis, Hypertension. Central features: confusion, seizures, coma. Treatment: airway management, immediate atropine (muscarinic antagonist), pralidoxime (2-PAM, reactivates acetylcholinesterase), and benzodiazepines for seizures.

\medterm{Cyanide Poisoning}-- Rapidly lethal cellular hypoxia caused by inhalation, ingestion, or dermal exposure to cyanide salts, hydrogen cyanide gas, or smoke from burning nitrogen-containing synthetic polymers (polyurethane, silk, nylon). Pathophysiology: cyanide ($\text{CN}^-$) binds with high affinity to the ferric ($\text{Fe}^{3+}$) iron of cytochrome c oxidase (complex IV) in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, halting oxidative phosphorylation, shutting down aerobic ATP production, and shifting cellular metabolism to anaerobic glycolysis. Clinical features: rapid onset of dyspnea, headache, vomiting, agitation, seizures, severe metabolic acidosis with markedly elevated blood lactate, and bright red venous blood (due to inability of tissues to extract oxygen). Antidote therapy: Cyanokit (hydroxocobalamin, which binds cyanide to form non-toxic cyanocobalamin / vitamin B12, excreted in urine; gold standard), or Nithiodote / Cyanide Antidote Kit (sodium nitrite to form methemoglobin which sequesters CN, followed by sodium thiosulfate to convert CN into thiocyanate via the rhodanese enzyme).

\medterm{Cyanide Toxicity}-- Lethal inhibitor of cytochrome c oxidase in mitochondrial electron transport chain, causing cellular hypoxia despite normal blood oxygenation. Symptoms: almond odor, severe lactic acidosis, seizures, coma. Antidotes: Hydroxocobalamin (Cyanokit) or Sodium Thiosulfate with Nitrites.

\medterm{Detoxification}-- The process of removing toxic substances from the body or rendering them harmless. In medical contexts, medically supervised withdrawal from addictive substances (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines), involving gradual dose reduction or substitution with longer-acting agents to manage withdrawal symptoms safely. Physiologic detoxification is performed primarily by the liver (phase I: cytochrome P450 oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis; phase II: conjugation with glucuronide, sulfate, glutathione, or glycine to increase water solubility for renal or biliary excretion). Detoxification is distinct from addiction treatment and is only the first step in substance use disorder management.

\medterm{Digoxin Toxicity}-- Cardiac glycoside toxicity causing nausea, vomiting, confusion, visual disturbances (yellow-green halos / xanthopsia), hyperkalemia, and lethal cardiac arrhythmias (bidirectional ventricular tachycardia, PVCs, AV block). Triggered by hypokalemia. Antidote: Digoxin-specific Fab antibody fragments (DigiFab).

\medterm{Digoxin Toxicity (Digibind)}-- Toxic elevation of cardiac glycoside levels leading to life-threatening arrhythmia and electrolyte disturbances. Causes: acute overdose or chronic accumulation due to renal impairment, hypokalemia (which enhances digoxin binding to $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$ ATPase), hypercalcemia, or drug interactions (amiodarone, verapamil, quinidine). Pathophysiology: inhibition of $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$ ATPase increases intracellular sodium and calcium, increasing cardiac inotropy but causing delayed afterdepolarizations. Symptoms: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, visual disturbances (classic yellow-green halos around lights, xanthopsia), and cardiac arrhythmias (PVCs, ventricular bigeminy, paroxysmal atrial tachycardia with AV block, sinus bradycardia, bidirectional ventricular tachycardia). Acute toxicity presents with severe hyperkalemia. Specific antidote: Digoxin Immune Fab (Digibind / Digifab), consisting of antigen-binding fragments of specific digoxin antibodies that rapidly bind intravascular free digoxin, reversing toxicity within 30 minutes.

\medterm{Envenomation (Snakebite)}-- Inoculation of complex biological venoms containing enzymatic and non-enzymatic toxins into human tissue via fangs or stings. Snake families: Viperidae (vipers, pit vipers—rattlesnakes, copperheads, Russell's viper) producing hemotoxic, cytotoxic, and necrotizing venoms; Elapidae (cobras, mambas, kraits, coral snakes) producing presynaptic and postsynaptic neurotoxic venoms. Hemotoxic features: local edema, tissue necrosis, compartment syndrome, consumptive coagulopathy (disseminated intravascular coagulation-like syndrome), bleeding, and acute kidney injury. Neurotoxic features: ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, dysphagia, descending flaccid paralysis, and respiratory failure. Initial management: immobilization of the limb at heart level, pressure immobilization bandaging (for elapid bites), supportive airway/circulatory care, and prompt administration of specific or polyvalent antivenom (Fab fragments or equine whole IgG antibodies). Tourniquets, incision, and suction are strictly contraindicated.

\medterm{Ethanol Toxicity}-- Acute intoxication from excessive ethyl alcohol consumption, causing central nervous system depression, ataxia, dysarthria, disinhibition, respiratory depression, and hypoglycemia. Chronic toxicity causes hepatic cirrhosis, Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, and dependency.

\medterm{Ethylene Glycol Toxicity}-- Ingestion of toxic alcohol found in antifreeze/coolant, metabolized by alcohol dehydrogenase into toxic glycolic acid and oxalic acid. Causes central nervous system depression, severe high anion gap metabolic acidosis, acute kidney injury from calcium oxalate crystal deposition in renal tubules, and elevated osmolal gap. Antidote: Fomepizole (or Ethanol) to inhibit alcohol dehydrogenase.

\medterm{Fomepizole}-- Competitive inhibitor of the enzyme Alcohol Dehydrogenase (ADH); specific antidote indicated for toxic alcohol ingestions (Methanol and Ethylene Glycol) to prevent their conversion into lethal toxic metabolites (formic acid and glycolic/oxalic acid).

\medterm{Gastric Lavage}-- Emergency toxicological procedure ('stomach pumping') involving insertion of a large-bore orogastric tube to flush and aspirate stomach contents; limited to life-threatening ingestions presenting within 60 minutes.

\medterm{Heavy Metal Toxicity (Lead, Mercury, Arsenic)}-- Pathological condition resulting from the accumulation of toxic heavy metals in body tissues, disrupting enzyme function by binding to sulfhydryl groups ($-\text{SH}$) on essential proteins. Common environmental and occupational toxins: Lead (causes microcytic anemia, basophilic stippling, colic, lead lines on gums, encephalopathy, drop wrist/foot), Mercury (elemental: pulmonary edema, tremor, erethism; inorganic: corrosive gastroenteritis, acute tubular necrosis; organic/methylmercury: Minamata disease, ataxia, constriction of visual fields, dysarthria, teratogenicity), Arsenic (mees lines, hyperkeratosis, garlic breath, peripheral neuropathy), and Cadmium (Itai-itai disease, osteomalacia, renal tubular dysfunction, lung cancer). Management involves removing the source of exposure, supportive care, and chelation therapy (succimer, BAL, $\text{CaNa}_2\text{EDTA}$, penicillamine).

\medterm{Hydroxocobalamin}-- Specific fast-acting antidote for Cyanide poisoning; binds cyanide ions directly to form non-toxic Vitamin B12a (Cyanocobalamin), excreted harmlessly in urine.

\medterm{Iron Toxicity}-- Acute poisoning from accidental or intentional ingestion of elemental iron supplements (frequently pediatric accidental ingestion of colorful iron tablets). Toxic dose: $>20$~mg/kg causes local toxicity; $>60$~mg/kg causes severe systemic toxicity. Pathophysiology: free circulating iron generates hydroxyl free radicals via the Fenton reaction, causing severe gastrointestinal mucosal ulceration, cellular lipid peroxidation, systemic vasodilation, and uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation. Clinical stages: Stage 1 (0.5--6 hrs: severe hemorrhagic gastroenteritis, abdominal pain, vomiting, hematemesis); Stage 2 (6--24 hrs: apparent recovery / latent phase); Stage 3 (12--48 hrs: metabolic acidosis, systemic shock, acute liver failure, hypoglycemia, coagulopathy); Stage 4 (2--5 days: hepatotoxicity and acute liver necrosis); Stage 5 (2--4 weeks: gastric outlet obstruction secondary to cicatricial scarring). Diagnosis: serum iron level at 4 hours, abdominal X-ray (radio-opaque iron pills). Specific antidote: intravenous deferoxamine (iron chelator), turning urine a characteristic "vin rosé" color.

\medterm{Lead Poisoning}-- Occupational and environmental hazard from exposure to lead. Affects the nervous system, kidneys, and hematopoietic system. Particularly dangerous for children causing developmental delays. Measured by blood lead levels.

\medterm{Lead Poisoning (Plumbism)}-- Toxic accumulation of lead in body tissues, predominantly affecting the hematological, central nervous, renal, and gastrointestinal systems. Sources: lead-based paint chips (in older homes), contaminated soil/water, lead pipes, imported ceramics, traditional medicines, and battery recycling. Pathophysiology: inhibits $\delta$-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase ($\delta$-ALAD) and ferrochelatase in the heme synthesis pathway, causing accumulation of protoporphyrin and microcytic hypochromic anemia with characteristic basophilic stippling of red blood cells. Clinical presentation in children: developmental delay, irritability, cognitive impairment, hyperactivity, and encephalopathy (at high levels). Presentation in adults: abdominal colic ("lead colic"), gout ("saturnine gout"), hypertension, peripheral motor neuropathy (wrist drop, foot drop), and Burton lines (bluish-gingival pigmentation). Diagnosis: blood lead level (BLL). Treatment: environmental abatement, succimer (DMSA) for BLL $>45~\mu\text{g/dL}$, and BAL plus $\text{CaNa}_2\text{EDTA}$ for severe encephalopathy (BLL $>70~\mu\text{g/dL}$).

\medterm{Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST)}-- Life-threatening adverse reaction resulting from systemic absorption of local anesthetics (bupivacaine, lidocaine) into bloodstream. Symptoms progress from circumoral numbness, metallic taste, tinnitus, and agitation to seizures, coma, and refractory cardiac arrest. Treatment: Intravenous Lipid Emulsion (Intralipid 20\%).

\medterm{Mercury Poisoning}-- Clinical toxicity resulting from exposure to elemental (metallic), inorganic, or organic (methylmercury) forms of mercury. Elemental mercury: inhalation of mercury vapor from broken thermometers or industrial processes causes acute interstitial pneumonitis, pulmonary edema, and chronic neurotoxicity (tremor, erethism mercurialis—personality changes, timidity, memory loss; "Mad Hatter syndrome"). Inorganic mercury salts ($\text{HgCl}_2$): ingestion causes corrosive gastroenteritis, acute tubular necrosis, and renal failure. Organic mercury (methylmercury): ingestion of contaminated bioaccumulated seafood (Minamata disease) or seed grain causes severe central nervous system damage—paresthesias, ataxia, dysarthria, visual field constriction ("tunnel vision"), deafness, and profound fetal teratogenicity. Diagnostic evaluation: 24-hour urine mercury (for elemental/inorganic) or whole blood mercury (for organic). Treatment: chelation with succimer (DMSA) or dimercaprol (BAL, contraindicated in methylmercury due to brain redistribution).

\medterm{Methanol Poisoning}-- Toxic alcohol poisoning resulting from ingestion of methanol, found in windshield washer fluid, solid alcohol fuels (Sterno), adulterated moonshine, and industrial solvents. Pathophysiology: methanol itself causes mild CNS depression, but is metabolized by alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) to formaldehyde, which is rapidly converted by aldehyde dehydrogenase to toxic formic acid (formate). Formic acid inhibits cytochrome c oxidase in the retina and basal ganglia, causing metabolic acidosis and severe end-organ damage. Clinical features: 12--24 hour latent period followed by visual disturbances ("looking through a snowstorm", optic disc hyperemia, blindness), severe high anion gap metabolic acidosis with elevated osmolal gap, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and basal ganglia necrosis (putaminal necrosis). Treatment: ADH inhibitors—fomepizole (gold standard) or ethanol (competitive substrate)—to block toxic metabolite formation; sodium bicarbonate for severe acidosis; folic acid to accelerate formate elimination; and hemodialysis for severe acidosis, visual impairment, or methanol level $>50$~mg/dL.

\medterm{Methanol Toxicity}-- Ingestion of toxic alcohol present in windshield washer fluid and moonshine, metabolized by alcohol dehydrogenase into Formic Acid. Causes visual impairment ('blindness like being in a snowstorm' due to optic papillitis), central nervous system depression, high anion gap metabolic acidosis, and elevated osmolal gap. Antidote: Fomepizole or Ethanol.

\medterm{N-Acetylcysteine (NAC)}-- Specific antidote for Acetaminophen (Paracetamol) toxicity; acts as a precursor for cellular Glutathione synthesis, neutralizing toxic reactive metabolite NAPQI ($N$-acetyl-$p$-benzoquinone imine) to prevent centrilobular hepatic necrosis.

\medterm{Naloxone}-- Pure competitive opioid receptor antagonist that rapidly reverses opioid-induced central nervous system and respiratory depression; administered IV, IM, or intranasally in suspected opioid overdose.

\medterm{Naloxone (Narcan)}-- Pure, competitive opioid receptor antagonist with high affinity for $\mu$, $\kappa$, and $\delta$ opioid receptors, rapidly displacing exogenous opioids from binding sites in the central nervous system. Indications: emergency reversal of acute opioid overdose presenting with respiratory depression, sedation, and miosis. Route of administration: intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, endotracheal, or intranasal spray. Onset of action: 1--2 minutes IV; duration of action: 30--90 minutes. Clinical caution: because the half-life of naloxone is shorter than most long-acting opioids (methadone, sustained-release morphine), recurrent respiratory depression can occur, requiring continuous monitoring or repeated doses/infusions. Administration in opioid-dependent individuals precipitates acute opioid withdrawal syndrome (agitation, rhinorrhea, lacrimation, vomiting, piloerection, tachycardia), which is distressing but non-life-threatening.

\medterm{Opioid Toxidrome}-- Classic clinical syndrome caused by acute overdose of natural, synthetic, or semi-synthetic opioid analgesics (morphine, heroin, fentanyl, oxycodone, methadone, codeine). Pathophysiology: stimulation of central $\mu$-opioid receptors in the brainstem and cerebral cortex. Classic clinical triad: (1) Respiratory depression (hypopnea, bradypnea, apnea), (2) Central nervous system depression (sedation, stupor, coma), and (3) Miosis ("pinpoint pupils"). Additional features: bradycardia, hypotension, hypothermia, decreased bowel sounds, and pulmonary edema (frequent with heroin and fentanyl overdose). Exceptions to miosis: meperidine (causes mydriasis due to normeperidine accumulation), propoxyphene, and co-ingestion of sympathomimetics or anticholinergics. Primary immediate goal of management: restoration of adequate ventilation using bag-valve-mask oxygenation followed by titrated administration of naloxone.

\medterm{Organophosphate Poisoning}-- Toxicity resulting from exposure to organophosphate esters, widely used as agricultural insecticides (malathion, parathion, chlorpyrifos) and military nerve agents (sarin, soman, tabun, VX). Pathophysiology: irreversible inhibition of acetylcholinesterase ($\text{AChE}$) via phosphorylation of the enzyme's active serine site, leading to massive accumulation of acetylcholine at muscarinic, nicotinic, and central nervous system synapses. Over time (hours to days), the enzyme-inhibitor complex undergoes "aging" (loss of an alkyl group), making acetylcholinesterase permanently unreactivatable by oximes. Clinical features: severe cholinergic toxidrome with profuse bronchorrhea, bronchospasm, miosis, muscle fasciculations, flaccid paralysis, seizures, and respiratory failure. Treatment: immediate decontamination, personal protective equipment for responders, aggressive high-dose atropine IV to block muscarinic hypersecretion, pralidoxime (2-PAM) IV prior to aging to reactivate $\text{AChE}$, and diazepam/midazolam for seizure prevention.

\medterm{Pralidoxime (2-PAM)}-- Oxime compound utilized as a specific antidote for organophosphate insecticide and nerve agent poisonings. Mechanism of action: nucleophilic attack on the phosphorylated serine residue of the inhibited acetylcholinesterase enzyme, cleaving the organophosphate moiety and restoring active acetylcholinesterase. Pralidoxime acts primarily at nicotinic neuromuscular junctions, reversing muscle weakness, fasciculations, and paralysis (functions where atropine is ineffective). Crucial therapeutic window: must be administered before "aging" of the organophosphate-enzyme complex occurs, after which chemical reactivation is impossible. Always co-administered with atropine (atropine addresses muscarinic hypersecretions, while pralidoxime addresses nicotinic neuromuscular toxicity). Ineffective against carbamate toxicity (where enzyme inhibition is spontaneously reversible).

\medterm{Rhabdomyolysis (Toxic/Traumatic)}-- Rapid breakdown and necrosis of skeletal muscle tissue, releasing intracellular muscle constituents (myoglobin, creatine kinase, potassium, phosphate, urate) into the systemic circulation. Toxic etiologies: alcohol abuse, cocaine, amphetamines, statins (especially combined with fibrates or macrolides), MDMA, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, serotonin syndrome, carbon monoxide, colchicine, and snake/insect envenomation. Traumatic/physical causes: crush injury, prolonged immobilization ("found down"), compartment syndrome, strenuous exercise, and grand mal seizures. Clinical triad ($<50\%$ of cases): myalgia, muscle weakness, and dark red-brown urine ("tea-colored" or "cola-colored"). Pathophysiology of kidney injury: myoglobin precipitates in renal tubules, causing tubular obstruction, direct tubulotoxicity, and intrarenal vasoconstriction. Laboratory diagnostics: serum creatine kinase (CK) $>5\times$ upper limit of normal ($>1000$~U/L); urine dipstick positive for "blood" but negative for RBCs on microscopy. Management: aggressive IV fluid hydration with isotonic saline to maintain urine output at 200--300~mL/hr, treatment of hyperkalemia, and hemodialysis for severe renal failure or refractory electrolyte abnormalities.

\medterm{Salicylate Toxicity (Aspirin Overdose)}-- Acute or chronic overdose of aspirin, causing direct stimulation of respiratory center (primary Respiratory Alkalosis) followed by uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation (primary Metabolic Acidosis), tinnitus, hyperthermia, vomiting, and altered mental status. Treatment: Urinary alkalinization with IV Sodium Bicarbonate.

\medterm{Sedative-Hypnotic Toxidrome}-- Clinical syndrome resulting from acute toxicity or overdose of central nervous system depressants, primarily benzodiazepines (diazepam, alprazolam, lorazepam), barbiturates (phenobarbital, secobarbital), non-benzodiazepine hypnotics (zolpidem, zopiclone), GHB, and ethanol. Mechanism: enhancement of gamma-aminobutyric acid ($\text{GABA}$) neurotransmission at $\text{GABA}_\text{A}$ receptor complexes in the brain. Clinical presentation: CNS depression, ataxia, dysarthria, nystagmus, lethargy, stupor, and coma. Vital signs: typically mild hypothermia, mild hypotension, and mild bradypnea; severe respiratory depression and cardiovascular collapse occur with barbiturates, heavy ethanol co-ingestion, or intravenous boluses. Pupils are usually mid-position or small but reactive (unlike opioids). Management: airway protection, supportive care, and mechanical ventilation. Specific antagonist: flumazenil (competitive $\text{GABA}_\text{A}$ benzodiazepine receptor antagonist); flumazenil must be used with extreme caution due to risk of precipitating life-threatening seizures in chronic benzodiazepine users or co-ingestions of pro-convulsant drugs (tricyclic antidepressants).

\medterm{Snakebite Envenomation}-- Envenomation resulting from venomous snakebites (pit vipers: rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths; elapids: coral snakes). Pit viper envenomation causes localized tissue destruction (pain, progressive edema, bullae, ecchymosis) and systemic manifestations (coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, hypotension). Management: limb immobilization at heart level, avoidance of harmful remedies (no tourniquets, ice, or incision/suction), and prompt administration of antivenom (Crotalidae polyvalent immune Fab / CroFab or Anavip) for progressive edema, coagulopathy, or systemic toxicity.

\medterm{Sympathomimetic Toxidrome}-- Clinical syndrome resulting from hyperstimulation of $\alpha$- and $\beta$-adrenergic receptors caused by illicit drugs (cocaine, amphetamines, methamphetamine, MDMA/ecstasy, synthetic cathinones/"bath salts") or therapeutic agents (ephedrine, pseudoephedrine, phenylpropanolamine, MAO inhibitors). Clinical presentation: agitation, paranoia, psychosis, severe diaphoresis (sweating), hyperthermia, mydriasis (dilated pupils), tachycardia, hypertension, and tremors. Differentiating feature from anticholinergic toxidrome: patients with sympathomimetic toxidrome have **profuse sweating** ("wet skin"), active bowel sounds, and preserved salivation, whereas anticholinergic patients have dry skin, absent bowel sounds, and dry mouth. Complications: hypertensive emergency, acute myocardial infarction, aortic dissection, intracranial hemorrhage, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, and ventricular arrhythmias. Treatment: aggressive sedation with IV benzodiazepines (lorazepam, diazepam), cooling measures, and control of hypertension (phentolamine, nitroprusside; pure $\beta$-blockers are contraindicated due to risk of unopposed $\alpha$-adrenergic stimulation).

\medterm{Toxicokinetics}-- Study of the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) of xenobiotics (drugs, toxins, environmental chemicals) under conditions of toxic overdose or poisoning. Toxicokinetics differs from standard pharmacokinetics because therapeutic kinetic models often break down in high-dose exposures: absorption may be delayed or prolonged due to bezoar formation, sustained-release formulations, or toxic ileus; protein binding may become saturated, dramatically increasing free active drug concentrations; metabolic pathways (e.g., hepatic glucuronidation) become saturated, shifting zero-order clearance to capacity-limited elimination; volume of distribution ($V_d$) may expand; and renal or hepatic organ failure alters normal clearance pathways. Understanding toxicokinetics guides antidote timing, multi-dose charcoal utilization, urinary alkalinization, and decisions regarding extracorporeal clearance (hemodialysis, hemoperfusion).

\medterm{Toxicology}-- Branch of medical science concerned with the nature, effects, detection, mechanism of action, clinical diagnosis, and treatment of poisons, toxins, and toxic substances. Subspecialties: Clinical Toxicology (diagnosis and treatment of human poisoning in emergency and critical care settings), Forensic Toxicology (post-mortem analysis and legal application of toxicological testing), Occupational/Environmental Toxicology (health effects of industrial chemicals and pollutants), Regulatory Toxicology (safety evaluation of drugs, cosmetics, and food additives), and Toxinology (specialized study of animal, plant, and microbial venoms and toxins).

\medterm{Toxidrome}-- Constellation of physical signs, symptoms, vital sign abnormalities, and laboratory findings associated with a specific class or group of toxic substances. Recognition of toxidromes allows rapid bedside identification of the likely toxic agent in an unknown overdose situation prior to formal laboratory confirmation. Major classic toxidromes include: Anticholinergic, Cholinergic, Opioid, Sedative-Hypnotic, Sympathomimetic, Salicylate, and Serotonergic toxidromes. Systematic toxidrome assessment evaluates mental status, vital signs (pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature), pupil size and reactivity, skin moisture and color, bowel motility, and presence of reflexes/neuromuscular excitability.

\medterm{Toxodrome}-- See Toxidrome.

\medterm{Tricyclic Antidepressant (TCA) Toxicity}-- Severe overdose of TCAs (amitriptyline, imipramine) causing inhibition of cardiac fast sodium channels, muscarinic receptors, and $\\alpha$-1 receptors. Triad: Coma, Convulsions, Cardiac arrhythmias (wide QRS complex, R wave in aVR). Antidote: IV Sodium Bicarbonate (alkalinizes serum, increases extracellular sodium to unblock sodium channels).

\medterm{Wasp Stings}-- See Bites and Stings.

\medterm{Whole Bowel Irrigation}-- Decontamination technique using large volumes of polyethylene glycol-electrolyte solution (PEG-ELS) via nasogastric tube to flush the entire GI tract; indicated for ingestions of sustained-release medications, heavy metals (iron, lead), or illicit drug packets ('body packers').
