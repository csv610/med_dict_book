\chapter{Pocket Dictionary: G Terms}

\medterm{G-Protein Coupled Receptors}-- The largest family of cell surface receptors with seven transmembrane alpha-helical
domains. Upon ligand binding, the receptor undergoes a conformational change that
activates an associated heterotrimeric G-protein (Galpha, Gbeta, Ggamma). The Galpha
subunit exchanges GDP for GTP and dissociates from the Gbeta-gamma complex.

\medterm{G6PD Deficiency}-- Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is an X-linked enzymopathy affecting
the pentose phosphate pathway, leading to impaired NADPH production and reduced
glutathione regeneration, rendering erythrocytes susceptible to oxidative hemolysis.
Common variants: African A- (mild), Mediterranean (severe), Mahidol (SE Asia).

\medterm{GABA (Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid)}-- The primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the mammalian central nervous system,
synthesised from glutamate via glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) with pyridoxal
phosphate (vitamin B6) as cofactor.

\medterm{Gadolinium-Based Contrast Agent}-- A paramagnetic contrast agent used in MRI to alter tissue signal and improve lesion
conspicuity or vascular assessment. Agent selection and dosing consider kidney function,
pregnancy, prior reactions, and the rare risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis.

\medterm{Gadolinium-Induced Nephrogenic Systemic Fibrosis}-- Rare but serious condition in patients with severe renal impairment receiving
gadolinium-based MRI contrast. Causes skin thickening, organ fibrosis. Risk reduced with
macrocyclic agents.

\medterm{Gagging}-- A protective retching response triggered by stimulation of the pharynx, posterior
tongue, or nearby structures. It may occur during swallowing, examination, aspiration,
reflux, infection, or emotional distress; persistent gagging with dysphagia, vomiting,
respiratory symptoms, or weight loss requires assessment.

\medterm{Gait}-- The pattern or style of walking. Gait is a complex motor function involving coordination
of the central and peripheral nervous systems, muscles, and joints. Gait assessment
evaluates stance, stride length, cadence, arm swing, truncal stability, and turning.

\medterm{Gait Abnormality}-- A deviation from normal walking caused by musculoskeletal, neurological, vestibular,
visual, cardiovascular, or systemic disease. Assessment considers speed, base, symmetry,
balance, stride, turning, assistive-device use, and associated weakness, sensory loss,
pain, or cognitive impairment.

\medterm{Gait Assessment}-- A crucial component of the neurological examination that provides significant diagnostic
information about the underlying neurological condition. Normal gait requires intact
motor, sensory, cerebellar, and vestibular systems, as well as intact cognition.

\medterm{Gait Disturbance}-- An abnormality of walking—a common presenting symptom with diverse causes.

\medterm{Gait Speed}-- Walking velocity measured over a standardized distance. It is a simple marker of
mobility, frailty, disability, and mortality risk and can track functional change over
time.

\medterm{Gait Training}-- Teaching or retraining walking pattern. Techniques: parallel bars, walker, cane, stair
training. Addresses abnormalities: limping, antalgic gait, hemiplegic gait, parkinsonian
gait. Goals: safety, efficiency, independence.

\medterm{Galactocele}-- A milk-filled retention cyst of the breast, occurring during lactation or after weaning.
Results from obstruction of a milk duct. Presents as a painless, fluctuant breast mass.
Diagnosis by ultrasound showing a well-defined, hypoechoic or anechoic mass. Treatment
includes aspiration. May become infected, requiring antibiotics.

\medterm{Galactogogue}-- A substance that promotes breast milk production.

hyperprolactinemia or nipple/breast stimulation. Causes: prolactinoma (pituitary
adenoma), medications (antipsychotics, metoclopramide, estrogens, opiates, verapamil,

\medterm{Galactorrhoea}-- The spontaneous flow of milk from the breast not associated with childbirth or
breastfeeding. Galactorrhoea can be unilateral or bilateral and is caused by
hyperprolactinaemia (elevated prolactin).

\medterm{Galactosaemia}-- A rare autosomal recessive disorder of galactose metabolism caused by deficiency of one
of three enzymes: galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase (GALT, classic galactosaemia,
type I), galactokinase (GALK, type II), or UDP-galactose epimerase (GALE, type III).

\medterm{Galactose Metabolism}-- Conversion of galactose to glucose-1-phosphate through the Leloir pathway involving
galactokinase, galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase, and UDP-galactose 4-epimerase.
Defects cause galactosemia, which can produce liver disease, cataracts, and
developmental toxicity.

\medterm{Galactosemia}-- An inherited disorder of galactose metabolism, most often caused by
galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase deficiency. Untreated classic disease can cause
jaundice, liver dysfunction, vomiting, cataracts, sepsis, and neurodevelopmental
impairment after milk feeding.

\medterm{Galant Reflex}-- A primitive reflex present from birth to 4 months, elicited by stroking the skin along
the side of the infant's back. The infant curves toward the stimulated side. Assessed by
running a finger from the lateral back toward the hip while the infant is in ventral
suspension. Absence may indicate neurologic abnormality.

\medterm{Gall-Bladder}-- A pear-shaped sac located beneath the liver on the visceral surface, in the gallbladder
fossa between the right and quadrate lobes of the liver. Capacity: approximately 30-50
mL.

\medterm{Gall-Bladder, Diseases of}-- Pathological conditions affecting the gallbladder. Types: (1) Cholelithiasis
(gallstones)--most common, affects 10-15\1 of adults in Western countries. (2) Acute
cholecystitis--inflammation of the gallbladder, usually due to stone obstruction of the
cystic duct (90\1).

\medterm{Gallavardin Phenomenon}-- The dissociation of the murmur of aortic stenosis into two components: a musical quality
heard at the apex and a harsh, rough quality heard at the right upper sternal border.
This occurs because high-frequency components are transmitted to the apex while
low-frequency components are heard at the base.

\medterm{Gallbladder}-- A small, pear-shaped sac under the liver that stores and concentrates bile before
releasing it into the small intestine to aid fat digestion.

\medterm{Gallbladder Cancer}-- Aggressive, often diagnosed incidentally after cholecystectomy for cholelithiasis (1-2\1
of cholecystectomies). Risk factors: gallstones (chronic inflammation), porcelain
gallbladder (calcified wall, 10-25\1 cancer risk), anomalous pancreaticobiliary
junction, gallbladder polyps >1cm, typhoid carrier state.

\medterm{Gallbladder volvulus}-- Axial torsion of the gallbladder around its mesentery and cystic duct, most commonly
affecting elderly women, leading to acute gallbladder ischemia. Presents with sudden
onset severe right upper quadrant pain, nausea, vomiting, and signs of peritoneal
irritation.

\medterm{Gallium Scan}-- Nuclear medicine imaging using Ga-67. Detects inflammation, infection, tumors. Largely
replaced by FDG-PET.

\medterm{Gallium-68}-- PET radionuclide, half-life 68 minutes. Produced from Ge-68/Ga-68 generator. Used in
DOTATATE, PSMA, and other theranostic applications.

\medterm{Gallstone Ileus}-- Large gallstone eroding through the gallbladder wall into the duodenum causing
mechanical small bowel obstruction. Diagnosed by CT showing Rigler triad of small bowel
obstruction, pneumobilia, and ectopic gallstone. The most common site of obstruction is
the ileum where the bowel caliber narrows.

\medterm{Galvanism}-- The application of a direct electric current for therapeutic or diagnostic purposes.
Named after Luigi Galvani (1737-1798). Historical uses: galvanic stimulation of muscles,
iontophoresis (introduction of ionised drugs through skin), electroconvulsive therapy
(modified).

\medterm{Gamma Camera}-- Imaging device using NaI(Tl) scintillation crystal, photomultiplier tubes, collimator.
Detects gamma rays from radiopharmaceuticals. Anger camera principle.

\medterm{Gamma-Globulin}-- A fraction of plasma proteins containing antibodies (immunoglobulins). Historically the
term referred to a group of serum proteins with the slowest electrophoretic mobility
(gamma region on serum protein electrophoresis). Currently synonymous with
immunoglobulin preparations used for passive immunisation and immunotherapy.

\medterm{Ganglion}-- A collection of neuronal cell bodies located outside the central nervous system (CNS).

\medterm{Ganglion Cysts}-- Ganglion cysts: mucin-filled cysts from joint capsule/tendon sheath. Dorsal wrist
(60-70\1, scapholunate interval), volar wrist (radial artery), flexor tendon sheath
(volar retinaculum, "seed ganglion"), DIP (mucous cyst, Heberden node association).

\medterm{Ganglioneuroblastoma}-- A pediatric tumor of the sympathetic nervous system containing both neuroblastic and
ganglion-cell elements. It most often arises in the adrenal or paraspinal region and may
present with an abdominal mass, pain, hypertension, or systemic symptoms.

\medterm{Ganglionic Blocker}-- A class of drugs that block nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at autonomic ganglia (both
sympathetic and parasympathetic), inhibiting neurotransmission in both divisions of the
autonomic nervous system.

\medterm{Gangrene}-- Death of tissue due to loss of blood supply, typically affecting the extremities and
bowel, and represents a form of coagulative necrosis. Dry gangrene occurs in tissues
with gradual arterial occlusion, where the dead tissue becomes dry, shrunken, and dark
brown or black.

\medterm{Gargalesthesia}-- The sensation of tickling. A complex sensory experience mediated by light tactile
stimulation of sensitive skin areas (axillae, soles, flanks, ribcage). Involves both
spinal reflexes (goosebumps, withdrawal) and emotional processing in the anterior
cingulate cortex and somatosensory cortex.

\medterm{Gargle}-- The act of rinsing the mouth and throat with a liquid, holding it at the back of the
throat while exhaling to create a bubbling action. Used for relief of sore throat or as
a delivery method for antiseptics, anaesthetics, and anti-inflammatory agents.

\medterm{Gartner duct cyst}-- A benign cyst arising from remnants of the mesonephric (Wolffian) duct along the lateral
wall of the vagina or uterus. They are typically asymptomatic and discovered
incidentally on pelvic examination or imaging. Most require no treatment unless
symptomatic, in which case surgical excision is performed.

\medterm{Gas Gangrene}-- A life-threatening bacterial infection characterized by the presence of gas in tissues
(subcutaneous emphysema) and rapid myonecrosis. Caused by Clostridium perfringens (type
A, 80\1 of cases), C. novyi, C. septicum, and C.

\medterm{Gaskin Maneuver (All-Fours Position)}-- A maneuver for shoulder dystocia resolution: the mother is rolled onto her hands and
knees (all-fours position). Gravity and the change in pelvic dimensions (increased
anteroposterior diameter of the pelvic outlet) may dislodge the impacted shoulder. The
posterior arm may also be delivered more easily.

\medterm{Gastralgia}-- Pain in the stomach or epigastric region.

\medterm{Gastrectomy}-- Surgical removal of all (total gastrectomy) or part (partial/subtotal gastrectomy) of
the stomach. Indications: gastric cancer (most common), severe peptic ulcer disease
(rarely needed now due to PPIs and H. pylori treatment), gastric lymphoma,
gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), perforation, bleeding.

\medterm{Gastric Acid}-- Hydrochloric acid secreted by gastric parietal cells, producing an intragastric pH of
1-3. Secretion is stimulated by histamine (H2 receptors), gastrin (CCK-B/gastrin
receptors), and acetylcholine (M3 muscarinic receptors), and inhibited by somatostatin,
prostaglandins, and luminal acid (negative feedback).

\medterm{Gastric Bezoar}-- Accumulation of indigestible material in the stomach. Phytobezoars consist of vegetable
fiber, trichobezoars of hair, and pharmacobezoars of medications. Associated with
gastroparesis, prior surgery, and impaired gastric motility. Diagnosed by endoscopy.

\medterm{Gastric Bypass}-- Roux-en-Y gastric bypass creating a small proximal pouch drained by a Roux limb,
combining restrictive and mildly malabsorptive mechanisms. Indicated for BMI over 40, or
over 35 with obesity-related comorbidity. Mean excess weight loss approaches 60-70
percent.

\medterm{Gastric cancer}-- See Stomach Cancer.

\medterm{Gastric Carcinoid Tumor}-- Neuroendocrine tumor arising from enterochromaffin-like cells in the gastric fundus.
Type I gastric carcinoids are associated with autoimmune atrophic gastritis and
hypergastrinemia, are typically small and multifocal, and carry a good prognosis.

\medterm{Gastric Emptying}-- See physiology.

\medterm{Gastric Emptying Study}-- Nuclear medicine diagnostic test measuring the rate of gastric emptying. The protocol
involves ingestion of a radiolabeled meal followed by serial gamma camera images
obtained at one, two, and four hours. Gastric emptying is considered delayed if more
than ten percent of the meal remains at four hours.

\medterm{Gastric Lymphoma}-- Malignant proliferation of lymphoid tissue in the stomach most commonly
mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma. Strongly associated with H. pylori
infection. Early disease may regress completely with H. pylori eradication therapy
alone.

\medterm{Gastric Malignancy}-- See stomach cancer.

\medterm{Gastric Outlet Obstruction}-- Blockage of gastric outflow at the pylorus or duodenum. Causes include peptic ulcer
scarring, gastric cancer, and pyloric stenosis in infants. Presents with projectile
vomiting, dehydration, and metabolic alkalosis. Diagnosis is by upper endoscopy and CT
scan.

\medterm{Gastric pneumatosis}-- Presence of gas within the gastric wall, also called gastric emphysema, which may be
benign (gastric emphysema) or life-threatening (emphysematous gastritis). Benign forms
result from barotrauma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or mechanical causes.

\medterm{Gastric Polyps}-- Protruding lesions of the gastric mucosa classified by histological type. Fundic gland
polyps are the most common type associated with long-term proton pump inhibitor use and
familial adenomatous polyposis. Hyperplastic polyps are associated with chronic
gastritis and H. pylori infection and carry a small risk of dysplasia.

\medterm{Gastric Surgery}-- Gastric cancer: D2 lymphadenectomy standard (stations 1-12a, 12b). Gastrectomy types:
total, distal subtotal (Billroth I gastroduodenostomy, Billroth II gastrojejunostomy,
Roux-en-Y), proximal, pylorus-preserving. Reconstruction: Roux-en-Y preferred (prevents
bile reflux).

\medterm{Gastric Ulcer}-- Peptic ulcer in the gastric mucosa associated with H. pylori, NSAIDs, and smoking. Pain
is typically worsened by food. There is a higher risk of malignancy than duodenal
ulcers. Diagnosis is by upper endoscopy with biopsy to exclude malignancy. Treatment
involves proton pump inhibitors and H. pylori eradication.

\medterm{Gastric Ulcer (see Peptic Ulcer)}-- See Peptic Ulcer.

\medterm{Gastric Volvulus}-- Rotation of the stomach along its axis causing obstruction. It may present with
Borchardt triad of epigastric pain, retching, and inability to pass a nasogastric tube.
It is a surgical emergency requiring decompression and gastropexy. Organoaxial volvulus
is the most common type where the stomach rotates along its longitudinal axis.

\medterm{Gastrin}-- A peptide hormone produced by G cells in the gastric antrum and duodenum. Secreted in
response to: (1) gastric distension, (2) amino acids and peptides (especially from
protein-rich meals), (3) vagal stimulation (via gastrin-releasing peptide, GRP), (4)
hypercalcaemia.

\medterm{Gastrin Secretion}-- Gastrin is a peptide hormone produced by G-cells in the gastric antrum and duodenum,
stimulating hydrochloric acid secretion by parietal cells, pepsinogen secretion by chief
cells, and growth of the gastric mucosa.

\medterm{Gastrinoma}-- Gastrin-secreting neuroendocrine tumor causing Zollinger-Ellison syndrome characterized
by gastric acid hypersecretion leading to severe peptic ulcer disease, diarrhea, and
esophagitis. Approximately sixty percent are malignant with liver metastases. Multiple
endocrine neoplasia type 1 association in approximately twenty percent.

\medterm{Gastritis}-- Inflammation of the gastric mucosa, classified by duration (acute or chronic), etiology,
and histologic pattern. Acute gastritis is most commonly caused by Helicobacter pylori
infection (acute H.

\medterm{Gastrocnemius}-- The large superficial muscle of the posterior calf, forming the bulk of the calf
contour. Origin: medial and lateral femoral condyles. Insertion: calcaneus via the
Achilles tendon. Nerve: tibial nerve (S1-S2). Action: plantar flexion of the ankle
(standing, walking, running, jumping); also flexes the knee.

\medterm{Gastrocnemius Muscle}-- Most superficial posterior leg muscle forming the calf prominence, with medial/lateral
femoral condyle heads merging with soleus to form the Achilles tendon on the calcaneus.
Innervated by tibial nerve (S1-S2). Plantarflexes ankle and flexes knee (two-joint
muscle). Active in walking/running/jumping.

\medterm{Gastroduodenal Crohn Disease}-- Involvement of the stomach and duodenum by Crohn disease occurring in approximately five
percent of patients with Crohn. Gastric Crohn typically involves the antrum and pylorus
and can cause gastric outlet obstruction from edema, fibrosis, or stricture. Duodenal
Crohn presents with epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting, and weight loss.

\medterm{Gastroenteritis}-- Inflammation of the stomach and intestinal mucosa, most commonly caused by viral
infections (norovirus, rotavirus, adenovirus), bacterial pathogens (E. coli, Salmonella,
Campylobacter), or parasites. Presents with diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and
dehydration. Self-limited in most cases.

\medterm{Gastroenterology}-- The medical specialty focused on the diagnosis and management of diseases of the
gastrointestinal tract (esophagus, stomach, small intestine, colon, rectum, pancreas,
gallbladder, bile ducts, liver). Subspecialties: hepatology (liver), pancreatology,
inflammatory bowel disease, gastrointestinal oncology, neurogastroenterology.

\medterm{Gastroesophageal Reflux}-- Backward flow of gastric contents into the oesophagus, caused by transient or persistent
relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter or other mechanical factors.

\medterm{Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)}-- A chronic condition in which gastric contents reflux backward into the esophagus,
causing troublesome symptoms and/or complications. Epidemiology: affects 10--20\1 of
adults in Western countries.

\medterm{Gastrogastric intussusception}-- Rare form of intussusception where one segment of the stomach telescopes into an
adjacent gastric segment, typically associated with a lead point such as a gastric
polyp, leiomyoma, or carcinoma. Presents with abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and
occasionally upper gastrointestinal bleeding.

\medterm{Gastrografin Study}-- X-ray imaging using water-soluble contrast. Used when barium contraindicated
(perforation risk). Less detailed than barium.

\medterm{Gastrointestinal amyloidosis}-- Deposition of misfolded amyloid fibrils in the gastrointestinal tract, either as part of
systemic amyloidosis (AL, AA, or ATTR types) or rarely as primary localized disease.

\medterm{Gastrointestinal Bleeding}-- Hemorrhage from any part of the gastrointestinal tract classified as upper or lower
based on location relative to the ligament of Treitz. Upper gastrointestinal bleeding
originating proximal to this landmark typically presents with hematemesis or melena.

\medterm{Gastrointestinal Bleeding Scan}-- Detection of GI bleeding using Tc-99m labeled RBCs. Sensitive for active bleeding (>0.1
mL/min). Localizes bleeding site. Alternative: tagged RBC scan, CT angiography.

\medterm{Gastrointestinal Decontamination}-- Methods: activated charcoal, gastric lavage (rare), whole bowel irrigation (sorbitol,
PEG). Timing critical for charcoal (1-2 hours post-ingestion).

\medterm{Gastrointestinal Obstruction}-- Partial or complete blockage of the passage of intestinal contents through the
gastrointestinal tract. It may be mechanical or functional and can cause colicky pain,
vomiting, distention, constipation, obstipation, dehydration, bowel ischaemia,
perforation, or sepsis.

\medterm{Gastrointestinal Perforation}-- A full-thickness defect in the gastrointestinal wall allowing luminal contents to enter
the peritoneal or adjacent space. It can cause peritonitis, sepsis, and shock and
usually requires urgent imaging, antimicrobial therapy, resuscitation, and source
control.

\medterm{Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor}-- Mesenchymal tumor arising from interstitial cells of Cajal in the gastrointestinal wall
most commonly in the stomach. Often driven by gain-of-function mutations in the KIT
proto-oncogene or PDGFRA gene. The majority arebut approximately twenty to thirty
percent are malignant.

\medterm{Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor (GIST)}-- Mesenchymal tumor of GI tract. CT diagnosis. KIT mutation common.

\medterm{Gastrointestinal Villi}-- Finger-like projections of the small-intestinal mucosa that greatly increase absorptive
surface area. Each villus contains blood capillaries and a central lacteal for transport
of absorbed nutrients and lipids.

\medterm{Gastroparesis}-- Delayed gastric emptying without mechanical obstruction most commonly caused by diabetes
mellitus, post-surgical changes, or idiopathic causes. Presents with nausea, vomiting of
undigested food, early satiety, bloating, and abdominal pain. Diagnosed by gastric
emptying scintigraphy showing more than ten percent retention at four hours.

\medterm{Gastroschisis}-- A full-thickness abdominal wall defect, usually to the right of the umbilicus, through
which bowel herniates without a covering membrane. Not associated with chromosomal
abnormalities. Presents at birth with exposed bowel loops that may be edematous,
thickened, and discolored from amniotic fluid exposure.

\medterm{Gastrospenic fistulization}-- Abnormal communication between the stomach and spleen, most commonly arising from
gastric ulcer erosion into the splenic hilum or as a complication of splenic abscess.
Presents with abdominal pain, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and sepsis.

\medterm{Gastrostomy}-- Surgical creation of an opening (stoma) through the abdominal wall into the stomach for
enteral feeding or decompression.

\medterm{Gastrostomy Tube (PEG)}-- Tube through abdominal wall into stomach for feeding. Percutaneous endoscopic
gastrostomy. Indications: prolonged enteral feeding, dysphagia, neurological disorders.
Complications: infection, leakage, granulation tissue, burial bumper.

\medterm{Gastrulation}-- A crucial early phase in embryonic development where the single-layered blastula
reorganizes into a multi-layered structure with three germ layers.

\medterm{Gaucher Disease}-- See genetics.

\medterm{Gaucher's disease}-- See Storage Diseases.

\medterm{Gauze}-- Sterile cloth for wound care. Types: 4x4 pads, roller gauze, lap pads.

\medterm{Gavage}-- Feeding through a tube passed into the stomach (nasogastric, orogastric, or gastrostomy
tube). Used when oral feeding is impossible or inadequate. In neonates: gavage feeding
is used for premature infants unable to coordinate suck-swallow-breathe.

\medterm{Gel}-- A semisolid pharmaceutical formulation in which a liquid (usually water) is immobilized
within a three-dimensional network of a gelling agent (e.g., carbomer, cellulose,
alginate).

\medterm{Gelfoam}-- An absorbable hemostatic agent made from purified porcine gelatin. Used in surgical
settings to control bleeding where conventional hemostasis is difficult (liver, bone,
neurosurgery). Mechanism: provides a physical matrix that promotes platelet aggregation
and clot formation. Absorbed within 4-6 weeks.

\medterm{Gemfibrozil}-- A fibrate (PPAR-alpha agonist) used primarily for severe hypertriglyceridemia (reducing
pancreatitis risk) and mixed hyperlipidemia. Raises HDL and lowers triglycerides
markedly; modest LDL lowering.

\medterm{Gemination}-- Attempted division of single tooth bud. Results in large tooth with bifid crown, normal
tooth count.

\medterm{Gender Dysphoria}-- Psychological distress resulting from incongruence between one's experienced gender and
assigned gender at birth. Persists for \(\)6 months and causes significant impairment.
Onset in childhood (may resolve) or adolescence/adulthood (persistent).

\medterm{Gene}-- Basic unit of heredity, segment of DNA encoding a functional product (protein or RNA).
Structure: promoter, coding sequence (exons), introns, terminator. Genes are located at
specific positions (loci) on chromosomes.

\medterm{Gene Expression}-- Process by which information from a gene is used to synthesize functional product
(protein or RNA). Steps: transcription, RNA processing, translation, post-translational
modification. Regulated at multiple levels.

\medterm{Gene Panel}-- A laboratory test that sequences a selected group of genes associated with a clinical
phenotype. Panels can provide efficient, focused analysis but may miss variants outside
the selected genes or changes poorly detected by the platform.

\medterm{Gene Therapy}-- Treatment by introducing, removing, or changing genetic material. Approaches: gene
addition (functional copy), gene silencing (siRNA, antisense), gene editing
(CRISPR-Cas9). Examples: Luxturna (RPE65 mutation), Zolgensma (SMN1 in spinal muscular
atrophy).

\medterm{General Anesthesia}-- A controlled, reversible state of unconsciousness, analgesia, amnesia, and muscle
relaxation induced by pharmacologic agents, enabling surgical procedures without patient
awareness or pain.

\medterm{General Anesthetic}-- An agent producing reversible loss of consciousness, amnesia, analgesia, and muscle
relaxation sufficient for surgery.

\medterm{Generalized Anxiety Disorder}-- Characterized by excessive, difficult-to-control anxiety and worry about multiple events
or activities occurring more days than not for at least six months. Associated symptoms
include restlessness, being easily fatigued, difficulty concentrating, irritability,
muscle tension, and sleep disturbance.

\medterm{Generalized granuloma annulare}-- A widespread variant of granuloma annulare presenting with numerous small,
flesh-coloured to erythematous papules distributed over the trunk and extremities,
lacking the classic annular arrangement of localized disease. It is often associated
with diabetes mellitus and may be more persistent than localized forms.

\medterm{Generalized Tonic-Clonic Seizure}-- A generalized tonic-clonic seizure (formerly grand mal seizure) is a seizure involving
bilateral tonic stiffening followed by rhythmic clonic jerking of the extremities, with
loss of consciousness.

\medterm{Generalized-Onset Seizure}-- A seizure that begins in bilaterally distributed networks and rapidly engages both
cerebral hemispheres. Generalized absence, myoclonic, tonic, clonic, atonic, and
tonic-clonic seizures are recognized clinical types.

\medterm{Generator}-- System for producing short-lived radionuclide from longer-lived parent. Tc-99m from
Mo-99. Elution with saline. Quality control: Mo-99 breakthrough, Al-3+ breakthrough,
sterility.

\medterm{Generic Medicine}-- A medicine containing the same active ingredient, strength, dosage form, route, and
intended clinical effect as an approved reference product. Regulatory requirements
generally include demonstration of pharmaceutical equivalence and bioequivalence.

\medterm{Genetic Counseling}-- Process of helping individuals understand and adapt to genetic aspects of disease.
Includes risk assessment, education, psychosocial support, informed decision-making.

\medterm{Genetic Counselor}-- A health professional who evaluates inherited cancer risk, arranges and interprets
genetic testing, and provides counseling to patients and families, including cascade
testing of relatives.

\medterm{Genetic Heterogeneity}-- The occurrence of similar or related phenotypes caused by different genetic mechanisms.
Locus heterogeneity refers to variants in different genes causing the same phenotype;
allelic heterogeneity refers to different variants in the same gene.

\medterm{Genetic Screening}-- Testing of asymptomatic individuals for genetic disorders. Newborn screening: metabolic
disorders, hemoglobinopathies. Carrier screening: before pregnancy or during pregnancy.
Prenatal screening: non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), maternal serum screening.

\medterm{Genetic Sonogram}-- A targeted ultrasound performed at 18-22 weeks that combines standard anatomic survey
with specific soft markers for aneuploidy.

\medterm{Genetic Testing}-- Analysis of DNA for disease risk or diagnosis. Examples: BRCA testing, carrier
screening.

\medterm{Genetic Testing (Sequencing)}-- Analysis of DNA to identify pathogenic variants associated with genetic disorders.
Sanger sequencing: gold standard for single-gene testing (CFTR for cystic fibrosis,
BRCA1/2 for hereditary breast/ovarian cancer).

\medterm{Genetic Variant of Uncertain Significance}-- A DNA variant for which available evidence is insufficient to classify it as benign or
pathogenic. It should not generally be used alone to make predictive or treatment
decisions and may be reclassified as evidence evolves.

\medterm{Genital Herpes}-- Herpes simplex virus infection of the genital region (HSV-2 most commonly; HSV-1
increasingly), transmitted sexually, causing painful grouped vesicles/ulcers,
urethritis, dysuria, and lymphadenopathy; primary infection may cause fever and systemic
symptoms. Recurrent episodes are shorter and milder.

\medterm{Genital Injury}-- Trauma to the genital or anogenital tissues caused by sexual contact, assault,
childbirth, accident, or disease. Examination should document location, morphology,
healing stage, pain, bleeding, and associated injuries without assuming mechanism from
appearance alone.

\medterm{Genital Warts}-- See Genital Warts (Condylomata Acuminata).

\medterm{Genital Warts (Condylomata Acuminata)}-- Anogenital warts caused by human papillomavirus (HPV), most commonly types 6 and 11
(low-risk, >90\1 of genital warts, not associated with malignancy). Transmission: direct
skin-to-skin contact during vaginal, anal, or oral sex; highly infectious (65\1
transmission rate per sexual encounter).

\medterm{Genitalia}-- The external and internal reproductive organs. The term includes the vulva and vagina,
or the penis, scrotum, and associated structures, together with the gonads and
reproductive ducts when used broadly; anatomy varies with sex, age, and developmental
stage.

\medterm{Genitals}-- The external and internal reproductive organs. Female genitals: external (vulva — mons
pubis, labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, vaginal opening, Bartholin glands) and
internal (vagina, cervix, uterus, fallopian tubes, ovaries).

\medterm{Genito-Pelvic Pain/Penetration Disorder}-- A sexual dysfunction characterized by persistent or recurrent difficulties with vaginal
penetration, marked vulvovaginal or pelvic pain during intercourse, fear or anxiety
about penetration, and/or tensing of the pelvic floor muscles, lasting \(\)6 months and
causing marked distress.

\medterm{Genodermatoses}-- Genetic skin disorders. Inherited ichthyoses (lamellar, X-linked, congenital
ichthyosiform erythroderma). Epidermolysis bullosa (simplex, junctional, dystrophic).
Neurocutaneous syndromes (neurofibromatosis, tuberous sclerosis, Sturge-Weber).
Ectodermal dysplasias (hypohidrotic, hypotrichosis, nail dystrophy).

\medterm{Genomic Imprinting}-- Parent-of-origin-specific gene expression. Only one allele (maternal or paternal) is
expressed. Examples: Prader-Willi (paternal deletion 15q11-q13), Angelman (maternal
deletion 15q11-q13).

\medterm{Genotoxicity}-- Ability of substance to damage DNA. Tests: Ames test, chromosomal aberration,
micronucleus. Predicts carcinogenicity.

\medterm{Genotype}-- Genetic makeup of an individual, the specific alleles present at one or more loci.
Determines phenotype along with environmental factors.

\medterm{Geographic Atrophy}-- The advanced form of dry age-related macular degeneration, characterized by
well-demarcated, progressively enlarging areas of complete loss of the retinal pigment
epithelium, choriocapillaris, and overlying photoreceptors in the macula, resulting in
irreversible central vision loss.

\medterm{Geographic tongue}-- A benign inflammatory condition of the tongue featuring migratory red patches with white
or yellow borders, alternating with areas of depapillation, often asymptomatic and of
unknown cause.

\medterm{Geriatric Assessment}-- Comprehensive multidimensional evaluation of elderly person. Domains: medical,
functional, cognitive, psychological, social, environmental, pharmacological. Performed
by interdisciplinary team. Guides care planning, identifies problems, predicts outcomes.

\medterm{Geriatric Dentistry}-- Dental care for elderly. Management of age-related changes, polypharmacy effects,
xerostomia, root caries, periodontal disease, oral cancer screening.

\medterm{Geriatric Depression Scale}-- A screening questionnaire designed to detect depressive symptoms in older adults while
minimizing emphasis on somatic symptoms that may overlap with medical illness. A
positive screen requires clinical diagnostic assessment.

\medterm{Geriatric Medical Ethics}-- Ethical principles applied to care of older adults, including autonomy, capacity,
beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice.

\medterm{Geriatric Nutrition}-- Nutrition care addressing the effects of aging, chronic disease, medications, frailty,
oral and swallowing problems, sensory changes, and social factors on intake and
nutritional status. Goals include preserving function, muscle mass, hydration, and
quality of life.

\medterm{Geriatric Rehabilitation}-- See geriatrics.

\medterm{Geriatric Syndrome}-- Multifactorial condition common in elderly, not attributable to single disease.
Examples: falls, incontinence, frailty, delirium, immobility, pressure ulcers. Require
comprehensive assessment and multidisciplinary management.

\medterm{Geriatrician}-- Physician specializing in geriatrics.

\medterm{Geriatrics}-- Medical care of elderly.

\medterm{Geriatrix}-- Outdated term for elderly female patient. Prefer "older woman" or "elderly patient."

\medterm{Germ}-- A colloquial term for a microorganism that causes disease.

\medterm{Germ Cell Tumor}-- A tumor arising from germ cells (precursors of sperm and eggs), including seminoma,
dysgerminoma, teratoma, yolk sac tumor, embryonal carcinoma, and choriocarcinoma. Occurs
in the testis, ovary, and extragonadal sites (mediastinum, retroperitoneum, pineal);
many are highly curable with platinum-based chemotherapy.

\medterm{Germ Theory of Disease}-- The scientific principle that specific microorganisms cause specific infectious
diseases; established in the late 19th century by Koch (postulates—organism present in
all cases, isolated in pure culture, reproduces disease when inoculated, re-isolated)
and promoted by Pasteur and Lister.

\medterm{German Measles (Rubella)}-- German measles caused by Rubivirus. See Rubella.

\medterm{Germinoma}-- A germ-cell tumor most commonly arising in the pineal or suprasellar region, highly
sensitive to radiation and chemotherapy. Ovarian (dysgerminoma) and testicular
(seminoma) counterparts exist.

\medterm{Germline Mutation}-- A genetic variant present in the egg or sperm and therefore present in essentially every
cell of the individual. Pathogenic germline variants may confer inherited cancer
susceptibility and can have implications for relatives and treatment.

\medterm{Germline Testing}-- Genetic testing performed to identify inherited or potentially heritable variants,
usually from blood or saliva. Results may affect the patient, biological relatives,
cancer surveillance, reproductive planning, and genetic counseling.

\medterm{Germline Variant}-- A DNA variant present in the cells that give rise to eggs or sperm and therefore
potentially transmissible to offspring. It may also be present in most cells of the
individual, unlike a variant restricted to a somatic tissue.

\medterm{Gerontologist}-- Specialist in gerontology.

\medterm{Gerontology}-- Study of aging. Biological, psychological, social aspects.

\medterm{Gestation}-- The period of development of a fetus from fertilization to birth. Human gestation
averages 280 days (40 weeks) from the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP) or
266 days (38 weeks) from conception.

\medterm{Gestational Age}-- The age of pregnancy measured in weeks from the first day of the last menstrual period
(LMP). Also called menstrual age. Typically 2 weeks longer than fertilization age.
Calculated using LMP or firsttrimester ultrasound. Used to estimate due date (40 weeks
from LMP). Preterm is less than 37 weeks; post-term is beyond 42 weeks.

\medterm{Gestational Diabetes}-- Glucose intolerance first diagnosed during pregnancy, affecting 6-9\1 of pregnancies.
Risk factors include obesity, advanced maternal age, family history, and prior
gestational diabetes. Complications include macrosomia, birth trauma, neonatal
hypoglycemia, and preeclampsia.

\medterm{Gestational Diabetes Mellitus}-- Glucose intolerance first recognized during pregnancy affecting approximately six to
nine percent of pregnancies. Results from insulin resistance induced by placental
hormones including human placental lactogen, cortisol, and progesterone combined with
insufficient beta cell compensation.

\medterm{Gestational Hypertension}-- New-onset hypertension (BP \(\)140/90 mmHg) after 20 weeks of gestation in a woman with
previously normal blood pressure, without proteinuria or other features of preeclampsia.
Affects 6-8\1 of pregnancies. Management: monitoring blood pressure, screening for
preeclampsia progression (proteinuria, labs, symptoms).

\medterm{Gestational Sac}-- The first sonographic sign of an intrauterine pregnancy, visible on transvaginal
ultrasound at approximately 4.5-5 weeks gestational age. A fluid-filled structure within
the decidua, surrounded by the chorionic ring. Mean sac diameter (MSD) correlates with
gestational age.

\medterm{Gestational Trophoblastic Disease}-- A spectrum of conditions arising from abnormal trophoblast proliferation, including
complete and partial hydatidiform moles and gestational choriocarcinoma. Complete mole
has 46,XX karyotype (all paternal origin); partial mole is triploid (69 chromosomes).
Presents with elevated hCG, uterus large for dates, and vaginal bleeding.

\medterm{Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia}-- Persistent or malignant trophoblastic disease after a pregnancy event, identified by
abnormal or rising beta-hCG, metastases, or histologic criteria. It includes invasive
mole and choriocarcinoma and is highly responsive to risk-adapted chemotherapy.

\medterm{Gettler Test}-- Historical quantitative chemical test for drowning, comparing chloride concentration in
blood harvested from the left and right heart ventricles.

\medterm{Ghrelin}-- Hunger hormone from stomach. Stimulates appetite.

\medterm{Giant Cell Arteritis}-- A large-vessel vasculitis of adults >50 (peak 70-80) affecting cranial branches of
carotid arteries, with risk of permanent vision loss from anterior ischemic optic
neuropathy. Clinical: new-onset headache (temporal), scalp tenderness, jaw claudication,
visual disturbances (amaurosis fugax, diplopia), polymyalgia rheumatica symptoms.

\medterm{Giant Cell Arteritis Emergency}-- Suspected giant cell arteritis with visual symptoms, jaw or tongue claudication, or
neurologic ischemia requires immediate glucocorticoid treatment to reduce risk of
irreversible visual loss while diagnostic evaluation proceeds.

\medterm{Giant Coronary Aneurysm}-- See Coronary Artery Aneurysm.

\medterm{Giant right atrium}-- Marked dilatation of the right atrium, most commonly caused by chronic severe tricuspid
regurgitation, Ebstein anomaly, or long-standing atrial fibrillation with right heart
failure. The enlarged atrium may cause compression of adjacent structures, arrhythmias,
and thrombus formation. Diagnosis is by echocardiography or cardiac imaging.

\medterm{Giardia Infection}-- Intestinal infection caused by the protozoan Giardia duodenalis, usually acquired from
contaminated water, food, or person-to-person spread. It commonly causes foul-smelling
diarrhea, bloating, abdominal cramps, and malabsorption.

\medterm{Giardia lamblia}-- A protozoan parasite that causes giardiasis, a diarrhoeal illness transmitted through
the fecal-oral route. The organism has a trophozoite and cyst stage. Infection occurs
through ingestion of cysts in contaminated water. Symptoms include foul-smelling
diarrhea, abdominal cramps, and bloating.

\medterm{Giardiasis}-- Infection with Giardia lamblia (G. intestinalis), a protozoan parasite causing diarrheal
illness. Transmission via contaminated water (fecal-oral). Presents with foul-smelling,
greasy diarrhea, bloating, abdominal cramps, weight loss, and steatorrhea. Diagnosis:
stool antigen testing or microscopy. Treatment: metronidazole or tinidazole.

\medterm{Giemsa Stain}-- A Romanowsky-type stain of methylene blue and eosin dyes used to visualise blood cells
and microorganisms in smears and tissue sections. In microbiology it stains Plasmodium,
Babesia, Trypanosoma, Leishmania, Borrelia, and intracellular cytomegalovirus
inclusions, supporting rapid diagnosis of blood-borne parasitic and other infections.

\medterm{Gigantism}-- A rare condition of excessive growth and tall stature caused by growth hormone (GH)
hypersecretion that begins before epiphyseal plate closure in childhood, before the
onset of puberty.

\medterm{Gilbert Syndrome}-- A common, benign autosomal recessive hereditary hyperbilirubinemia caused by a promoter
mutation in the UGT1A1 gene leading to reduced activity ( 30\1 of normal) of
UDP-glucuronosyltransferase.

\medterm{Gingival Hyperplasia}-- An abnormal overgrowth of gingival tissue around the teeth, which can impair plaque
control, increase the risk of periodontal disease, and affect aesthetics. It can be
caused by chronic inflammatory conditions (associated with poor oral hygiene) or
systemic factors.

\medterm{Gingival melanoma}-- A rare and aggressive mucosal melanoma arising from melanocytes in the gingival mucosa,
presenting as a pigmented or amelanotic nodule or ulcer on the gums. Mucosal melanomas
of the head and neck carry a poor prognosis compared to cutaneous melanoma, often
presenting at an advanced stage.

\medterm{Gingival Recession}-- Apical displacement of the gingival margin from the cemento-enamel junction, exposing
the root surface. Causes include periodontal inflammation, traumatic toothbrushing, thin
periodontal tissue, tooth position, and iatrogenic factors; it may produce sensitivity
and root-caries risk.

\medterm{Gingivectomy}-- Surgical removal of gingival tissue. Indications: gingival hyperplasia, cosmetic
recontouring, access to caries.

\medterm{Gingivitis}-- Inflammation of gums without bone loss. Caused by dental plaque accumulation. Signs:
redness, swelling, bleeding on probing. Reversible with improved oral hygiene and
professional cleaning. If untreated, progresses to periodontitis.

\medterm{Gingivoplasty}-- Surgical reshaping of gingiva. Often combined with gingivectomy.

\medterm{Gingivostomatitis}-- Inflammation of the gums and oral mucosa, commonly caused by primary herpes simplex
infection but also associated with other infections, trauma, or systemic disease. It may
cause painful ulcers, swollen bleeding gums, fever, and difficulty eating or drinking.

\medterm{Gitelman Syndrome}-- Inherited autosomal recessive disorder of the distal convoluted tubule caused by
mutations in the NCC sodium-chloride cotransporter gene. Milder than Bartter syndrome
typically presenting in adolescence or adulthood.

\medterm{Gland}-- An organized group of epithelial cells specialized for secretion.

\medterm{Glandular Fever (Infectious Mononucleosis)}-- An acute viral syndrome caused by Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), primarily affecting
adolescents and young adults. See Mononucleosis.

\medterm{Glanzmann thrombasthenia}-- An inherited platelet aggregation disorder caused by deficient or dysfunctional
glycoprotein IIb/IIIa, producing mucocutaneous bleeding despite a normal platelet count.

\medterm{Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)}-- A standardized assessment of consciousness based on eye opening, verbal response, and
motor response, with a total score from 3 to 15. It supports serial neurologic
assessment but should be recorded with the individual component scores and confounders.

\medterm{Glass Ionomer}-- Restorative material releasing fluoride. Bonds to tooth structure. Used for pediatric
dentistry, cervical lesions, bases.

\medterm{Glaucoma}-- A group of progressive optic neuropathies characterized by the degeneration of retinal
ganglion cells and their axons, resulting in characteristic optic nerve head changes and
visual field loss, with elevated intraocular pressure being the most important
modifiable risk factor.

\medterm{Glenohumeral Joint}-- Ball-and-socket synovial joint between humeral head and glenoid cavity. Most mobile body
joint allowing flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, medial/lateral rotation,
circumduction. Mobility trades for stability, making it the most commonly dislocated
joint. Stabilized by glenoid labrum, glenohemoral ligaments, and rotator cuff.

\medterm{Glenoid Cavity}-- Shallow pear-shaped depression on the lateral scapular angle articulating with the
humeral head. The glenoid labrum deepens the socket by approximately 50 percent. Due to
its shallowness, the glenohumeral joint is the most commonly dislocated joint.

\medterm{GLIM Criteria}-- A consensus framework for diagnosing malnutrition using at least one phenotypic
criterion, such as weight loss, low body-mass index, or reduced muscle mass, and at
least one etiologic criterion, such as reduced intake or assimilation or disease-related
inflammation. Severity is graded using phenotypic thresholds.

\medterm{Glioblastoma}-- Grade IV astrocytoma (WHO), the most common and aggressive primary malignant brain tumor
in adults. Arises de novo (primary GBM, IDH-wildtype) or from low-grade astrocytoma
(secondary, IDH-mutant—better prognosis).

\medterm{Glioma}-- A tumor arising from glial cells (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, ependymal cells) in the
brain or spinal cord. Includes astrocytomas, oligodendrogliomas, and ependymomas;
behavior ranges from low-grade to highly aggressive (glioblastoma).

\medterm{Glisson's Capsule}-- Thin fibrous connective tissue envelope surrounding the liver, sending septa inward
dividing it into lobules. Richly innervated by phrenic and intercostal nerve pain
fibers, sensitive to distension and inflammation. Covered by visceral peritoneum except
at the bare area.

\medterm{Global Health}-- Health issues transcending national boundaries. Examples: pandemics, health disparities,
climate change.

\medterm{Globin}-- A family of heme-binding proteins involved in oxygen transport and storage. Structure:
globin fold (8 alpha-helices arranged in a globular structure), binds one heme molecule
(iron-protoporphyrin IX) per subunit.

\medterm{Globulin}-- A group of serum proteins (globular proteins) that are less soluble in water but soluble
in dilute salt solutions. On serum protein electrophoresis, globulins migrate in the
alpha-1, alpha-2, beta, and gamma regions. Alpha-1 globulins: alpha-1-antitrypsin,
alpha-fetoprotein.

\medterm{Globus Sensation}-- The persistent or intermittent sensation of a lump or foreign body in the throat without
an anatomic lesion or dysphagia, also termed globus pharyngeus, typically noticed
between meals. There is no impedance to swallowing and no weight loss; the feeling
worsens with stress, anxiety, or laryngopharyngeal reflux.

\medterm{Glomerular Filtration Rate}-- Volume of plasma filtered by the glomeruli per unit time normally ninety to one hundred
twenty milliliters per minute per one point seven three square meters body surface area.
Estimated by the CKD-EPI equation using serum creatinine, age, sex, and race.

\medterm{Glomerulonephritis}-- Inflammation of the glomeruli, classified by clinical presentation (nephritic vs.
nephrotic syndrome) and histopathology (e.g., IgA nephropathy, post-streptococcal,
membranoproliferative, crescentic). Presents with hematuria, proteinuria, hypertension,
and reduced GFR.

\medterm{Glomerulopathy}-- Any disease affecting the renal glomeruli, the capillary structures responsible for
blood filtration. It may produce albuminuria or proteinuria, haematuria, reduced
filtration, oedema, hypertension, or kidney failure and may be primary or secondary to
systemic, metabolic, infectious, or immune disease.

\medterm{Glomerulus}-- The tuft of capillaries in the renal corpuscle (Bowman's capsule) where blood is
filtered to form urine.

\medterm{Glomus Tumor}-- A rare benign neoplasm arising from glomus bodies (thermoregulatory shunts), presenting
as a small, blue-red, exquisitely tender subungual or digital nodule. The classic triad
is severe pain, point tenderness, and cold sensitivity. Diagnosis is clinical with
positive Love pin test and Hildreth sign (tumor disappears with ischemia).

\medterm{Glomus tympanicum}-- A benign vascular tumor arising from the glomus bodies on the promontory of the middle
ear, presenting with pulsatile tinnitus, hearing loss, and a reddish mass behind the
tympanic membrane.

\medterm{Glossitis}-- Inflammation of the tongue. Causes: (1) Infection: bacterial (Streptococcus,
Actinomyces), viral (HSV, Coxsackie--herpangina, HIV), fungal (Candida--oral thrush).

\medterm{Glossopharyngeal Neuralgia}-- Recurrent, brief attacks of severe stabbing or electric-shock-like pain in the throat,
tonsillar region, base of the tongue, or ear, caused by irritation of the
glossopharyngeal nerve. Swallowing, coughing, or talking may trigger attacks.

\medterm{Glottis}-- The opening between the vocal folds (true vocal cords) in the larynx, through which air
passes during respiration and phonation. Boundaries: anterior (anterior commissure,
attachment of vocal folds to thyroid cartilage), posterior (arytenoid cartilages and
posterior commissure).

\medterm{Gloves}-- Protective hand covering. Types: examination (nitrile, latex, vinyl), surgical. Sterile
vs. non-sterile.

\medterm{GLP-1 Receptor Agonist}-- A glucose-lowering drug that activates the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor, enhancing
glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppressing inappropriate glucagon secretion,
slowing gastric emptying, and increasing satiety.

\medterm{GLP-1 Receptor Agonists}-- Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists are a class of injectable antidiabetic agents
that mimic the effects of the incretin hormone GLP-1, which is secreted by intestinal
L-cells in response to food intake.

\medterm{Glucagon}-- A peptide hormone produced by the alpha cells of the pancreatic islets (Langerhans).

\medterm{Glucagon Signaling}-- Glucagon binds GPCR, activates adenylate cyclase, increases cAMP, activates PKA.
Promotes glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis.

\medterm{Glucagonoma}-- A rare pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor secreting glucagon. Classic presentation: 4 Ds —
diabetes mellitus (mild, new onset), dermatitis (necrolytic migratory erythema,
characteristic painful, erythematous, blistering rash on groin, perineum, extremities),
diarrhea, and deep vein thrombosis.

\medterm{Glucocorticoid Replacement Therapy}-- Replacement of cortisol deficiency in adrenal insufficiency using hydrocortisone in
divided doses. Typical regimen is ten to fifteen milligrams in the morning and five to
ten milligrams in the early afternoon mimicking the physiological cortisol diurnal
rhythm.

\medterm{Glucocorticoid-Sparing Therapy}-- Use of a non-glucocorticoid treatment strategy to control inflammation while reducing
cumulative corticosteroid exposure. It aims to limit osteoporosis, infection, diabetes,
hypertension, adrenal suppression, and other glucocorticoid complications.

\medterm{Glucocorticoids}-- A class of steroid hormones produced by the zona fasciculata of the adrenal cortex,
regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.

\medterm{Glucometer}-- Device measuring blood glucose. Capillary blood from fingerstick.

\medterm{Gluconeogenesis}-- Metabolic pathway generating glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors (lactate,
glycerol, glucogenic amino acids). Occurs primarily in liver (90\1) and kidney (10\1).

\medterm{Glucose}-- A monosaccharide (C6H12O6) that is the primary energy source for cells. Types: D-glucose
(dextrose, naturally occurring enantiomer) and L-glucose (synthetic). Routes of entry:
dietary carbohydrates (digested to glucose), gluconeogenesis (synthesis from
non-carbohydrate precursors), glycogenolysis (breakdown of stored glycogen).

\medterm{Glucose Intolerance}-- Impaired handling of glucose resulting in blood-glucose concentrations above normal but
below the diagnostic threshold for diabetes in a defined testing context. It often
reflects insulin resistance or inadequate insulin secretion and is associated with
increased risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease.

\medterm{Glucose Management Indicator}-- Derived metric from continuous glucose monitoring that estimates the hemoglobin A1c
equivalent based on mean sensor glucose values. Formula converts mean glucose to an
A1c-equivalent value allowing comparison between CGM data and traditional A1c.

\medterm{Glucose Sensor}-- Continuous glucose monitor (CGM) for diabetes. Subcutaneous sensor measures interstitial
glucose. Transmitter sends data to receiver/phone. Examples: Dexcom, Freestyle Libre,
Medtronic. Reduces hypoglycemia, improves A1c.

\medterm{Glucose Tolerance Test}-- Testing for diabetes mellitus and gestational diabetes. 75g OGTT (oral glucose tolerance
test): measure plasma glucose at 0, 60, and 120 minutes. Diagnostic: fasting \(\)126
mg/dL, 2-hour \(\)200 mg/dL. Gestational diabetes screening: 50g glucose challenge test
(GCT) at 24-28 weeks; if >140 mg/dL at 1 hour, proceed to 100g 3-hour OGTT.

\medterm{Glucose-6-Phosphatase}-- Enzyme converting glucose-6-phosphate to glucose. Located in liver and kidney.
Deficiency causes Von Gierke disease.

\medterm{Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency}-- An X-linked enzymopathy affecting approximately 400 million people worldwide, causing
episodes of hemolytic anemia triggered by oxidative stress from infections, certain
drugs including primaquine and sulfonamides, and fava beans. G6PD is essential for
generating NADPH to protect red blood cells from oxidative damage.

\medterm{Glue (Vascular)}-- A vascular embolization material used to occlude selected vessels under image guidance.

\medterm{Glutamate}-- The principal excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system. Synthesized
from glutamine or alpha-ketoglutarate and acting on ionotropic receptors (AMPA, kainate,
NMDA) for fast synaptic transmission and metabotropic receptors (mGluR1-8) for
modulation.

\medterm{Glutathione}-- Antioxidant tripeptide (Glu-Cys-Gly). Detoxifies reactive metabolites (e.g., NAPQI from
acetaminophen). Depletion causes toxicity. N-acetylcysteine replenishes glutathione.

\medterm{Gluten}-- A complex of storage proteins (prolamins and glutelins) found in wheat (gliadin), barley
(hordein), and rye (secalin). Gluten provides elasticity to dough. In susceptible
individuals, gluten triggers an autoimmune response (celiac disease) or non-autoimmune
sensitivity (non-celiac gluten sensitivity, NCGS).

\medterm{Gluten-Free Diet}-- Eliminates gluten (wheat, barley, rye). Essential for celiac disease, non-celiac gluten
sensitivity. Risk of nutrient deficiency if not supplemented. May be unnecessarily
restrictive for others.

\medterm{Gluten-Sensitive Enteropathy (Celiac Disease)}-- An autoimmune enteropathy triggered by ingestion of gluten (wheat, barley, rye) in
genetically predisposed individuals. Incidence: 1\1 worldwide, higher in HLA-DQ2 (90\1)
and HLA-DQ8 carriers.

\medterm{Gluteus Maximus Muscle}-- Largest, most superficial gluteal muscle forming the buttock bulk, from posterior iliac
gluteal line, sacrum, coccyx, and sacrotuberous ligament to the iliotibial tract and
gluteal tuberosity. Innervated by inferior gluteal nerve (L5-S2). Primary hip extensor
and lateral rotator.

\medterm{Gluteus Medius Muscle}-- Thick fan-shaped muscle between maximus and minimus, from external ilium to the greater
trochanter. Innervated by superior gluteal nerve (L4-S1). Primary hip abductor essential
for pelvic stability during single-leg stance. Superior gluteal nerve injury causes
Trendelenburg sign with contralateral pelvic drop.

\medterm{Gluteus Minimus Muscle}-- Smallest, deepest gluteal muscle from external ilium to the greater trochanter.
Innervated by superior gluteal nerve (L4-S1). Hip abductor and medial rotator
stabilizing the hip during walking. Works synergistically with medius for pelvic
stability. Tendinopathy commonly causes lateral hip pain mimicking trochanteric

\medterm{Glycaemia}-- The concentration of glucose in the blood, normally maintained within a narrow range
(fasting: 3.9-5.5 mmol/L or 70-100 mg/dL) through the coordinated action of insulin
(lowers glucose) and counter-regulatory hormones (glucagon, cortisol, catecholamines,
growth hormone).

\medterm{Glycemic Control}-- Maintenance of blood glucose within target range. In elderly, goals less stringent to
avoid hypoglycemia. HbA1c target: <7.5-8.0\1 depending on health status. Monitoring:
self-monitoring, continuous glucose monitoring. Medications: metformin first-line, avoid
sulfonylureas if hypoglycemia risk.

\medterm{Glycemic Index}-- Ranking of carbohydrates by effect on blood glucose. High GI foods spike glucose. Low GI
foods provide sustained energy. Useful for diabetes management.

\medterm{Glycemic Load}-- Combines glycemic index with carbohydrate amount. More practical measure of blood
glucose impact.

\medterm{Glycerin}-- A colorless, odorless, sweet-tasting trihydroxy alcohol (glycerol) with humectant,
osmotic, and solvent properties.

\medterm{Glycerol (Glycerine)}-- A triol compound (CH\12OH-CHOH-CH\12OH), a colourless, odourless, viscous liquid with a
sweet taste.

\medterm{Glycogen}-- A branched polysaccharide composed of glucose units linked by alpha-1,4 glycosidic bonds
(linear) and alpha-1,6 bonds (branching points, every 8-10 residues). Glycogen serves as
the main storage form of glucose in animals.

\medterm{Glycogen Storage Disease}-- A group of inherited metabolic disorders caused by defects in glycogen synthesis,
breakdown, or regulation, resulting in abnormal glycogen accumulation in tissues. Major
types: Type I (von Gierke, G6PC deficiency) — fasting hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis,
hepatomegaly, hyperuricemia, and hypertriglyceridemia.

\medterm{Glycogen Storage Diseases}-- A group of inherited disorders caused by deficiencies in enzymes involved in glycogen
metabolism, affecting the liver, muscle, or both.

\medterm{Glycogenesis}-- The synthesis of glycogen from glucose, primarily in liver and skeletal muscle.
Glucose-1-phosphate is converted to UDP-glucose, and glycogen synthase and the branching
enzyme extend and branch the polymer; insulin promotes the pathway.

\medterm{Glycogenolysis}-- Breakdown of glycogen to glucose-1-phosphate (converted to glucose-6-phosphate). Liver
glycogenolysis maintains blood glucose during fasting; muscle glycogenolysis provides
energy for contraction. Regulated by hormonal signals (glucagon, epinephrine) and
allosteric effectors (AMP activates phosphorylase).

\medterm{Glycolysis}-- Metabolic pathway breaking down glucose (6C) to pyruvate (3C), generating 2 ATP (net)
and 2 NADH. Occurs in cytoplasm. Ten enzymatic steps: investment phase (uses 2 ATP) and
payoff phase (produces 4 ATP, 2 NADH). Key regulatory enzymes: hexokinase,
phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1, rate-limiting), pyruvate kinase.

\medterm{Glycosuria}-- The presence of glucose in the urine. Normally, the renal tubules reabsorb filtered
glucose via sodium-glucose co-transporters (SGLT2 in proximal tubule). Glycosuria occurs
when blood glucose exceeds the renal threshold (approximately 10 mmol/L or 180 mg/dL),
overwhelming reabsorptive capacity.

\medterm{GnRH Agonist}-- Gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists are a class of medications that initially
stimulate (flare effect, 1-3 weeks) and then suppress pituitary gonadotropin (LH and
FSH) secretion through receptor downregulation, leading to profound suppression of sex
steroid production (testosterone in men, estradiol in women).

\medterm{GnRH Antagonist}-- A class of medications that competitively block GnRH receptors on pituitary
gonadotrophs, producing immediate, reversible suppression of LH and FSH secretion
without the initial flare effect seen with GnRH agonists.

\medterm{Goal Attainment Scaling}-- An individualized outcome method in which patient-specific goals are defined with graded
levels of expected attainment and scored after intervention. It captures meaningful
changes that standardized measures may miss.

\medterm{Goal Setting}-- SMART goals: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. Patient-centered
goals improve adherence.

\medterm{Goals-of-Care Discussion}-- A clinical conversation that aligns treatment recommendations with a patient's values,
priorities, prognosis, acceptable burdens, and preferred outcomes. It may include
decisions about hospitalization, resuscitation, artificial nutrition, and
comfort-focused care.

\medterm{Goiter}-- Enlargement of the thyroid gland, which can be diffuse or nodular. Causes: iodine
deficiency (most common worldwide), Hashimoto thyroiditis, Graves disease, multinodular
goiter, and thyroid cancer. May be associated with normal, hyperthyroid, or hypothyroid
function.

\medterm{Gold Alloy}-- Noble metal alloy for dental restorations. Excellent biocompatibility, durability,
marginal adaptation. Expensive.

\medterm{Goldenhar Syndrome}-- A variant of hemifacial microsomia characterized by the triad: epibulbar dermoids
(benign conjunctival tumors), preauricular skin tags or sinuses, and vertebral anomalies
(hemivertebrae, fused vertebrae, scoliosis). Also involves facial asymmetry, microtia,
conductive hearing loss, and macrostomia.

\medterm{Goldmann Tonometry}-- Goldmann applanation tonometry is the gold standard method for measuring intraocular
pressure, the most important modifiable risk factor for glaucoma, and it involves
applanating (flattening) a fixed area of the cornea using a biprism attached to a slit
lamp, measuring the force required to achieve this applanation.

\medterm{Golfer Elbow}-- Medial epicondylitis, a tendinopathy of the common flexor/pronator tendon at the medial
epicondyle. Less common than tennis elbow. Caused by repetitive wrist flexion and
forearm pronation. Presents with medial elbow pain worsened by wrist flexion and
gripping. Tenderness over the medial epicondyle. Positive Golfer elbow test.

\medterm{Golgi Apparatus}-- Membrane-bound organelle (Golgi complex) consisting of flattened cisternae (cis, medial,
trans) that modifies, sorts, and packages proteins and lipids from the ER for secretion
or delivery to other organelles.

\medterm{Golgi Tendon Organ}-- A mechanoreceptor located at the junction between a skeletal muscle and its tendon,
innervated by Ib afferent fibers. The Golgi tendon organ detects tension generated by
muscle contraction or passive stretch.

\medterm{Gonadal Dysgenesis}-- Incomplete or abnormal development of the ovaries or testes, often resulting from
chromosomal abnormalities, gene variants, or disordered sex development. It may cause
atypical genital development, delayed or absent puberty, infertility, and abnormal
gonadal hormone production.

\medterm{Gonadotrophins}-- Hormones that stimulate the gonads. The anterior pituitary secretes follicle-stimulating
hormone (FSH) and luteinising hormone (LH), both glycoprotein hormones sharing a common
alpha subunit (identical to TSH and hCG alpha) with a unique beta subunit.

\medterm{Gonads}-- The primary reproductive organs: testes in males and ovaries in females. Functions: (1)
gamete production (spermatogenesis in testes, oogenesis in ovaries); (2) sex steroid
hormone secretion (testosterone from testicular Leydig cells; estrogen and progesterone
from ovarian follicular cells and corpus luteum).

\medterm{Gonioscopy}-- An ophthalmological examination technique that allows direct visualization of the
anterior chamber angle, the anatomical region where the cornea and iris meet and where
the trabecular meshwork is located, and it is essential for distinguishing between
open-angle and angle-closure forms of glaucoma.

\medterm{Gonococcal Arthritis}-- A disseminated infection of joints caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae, often with fever,
migratory joint or tendon pain, skin pustules, and tenosynovitis. It requires prompt
antimicrobial treatment and evaluation for concomitant sexually transmitted infection.

\medterm{Gonococcal pharyngitis}-- See Gonorrhea.

\medterm{Gonorrhea}-- A sexually transmitted infection caused by the Gram-negative diplococcus Neisseria
gonorrhoeae. Epidemiology: second most common bacterial STI (after Chlamydia), highest
incidence in 15-24 year olds.

\medterm{Goodpasture Syndrome}-- Anti-GBM disease affecting both kidneys and lungs. Pulmonary hemorrhage with hemoptysis
and crescentic GN. Linear IgG deposits on renal biopsy. Emergent plasmapheresis,
cyclophosphamide, and steroids. Early treatment essential to prevent irreversible renal
and pulmonary damage.

\medterm{Gottron Papules}-- Pathognomonic dermatological sign of dermatomyositis, consisting of erythematous to
violaceous lichenoid papules located over the dorsal aspects of the interphalangeal
(PIP/DIP) and metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints. May undergo necrosis or ulceration.

\medterm{Gout}-- A crystal deposition disease caused by monosodium urate (MSU) crystal formation in
joints and tissues due to chronic hyperuricemia (serum urate >6.8 mg/dL).
Pathophysiology: urate overproduction (10\1) or underexcretion (90\1); crystals activate
NLRP3 inflammasome -> IL-1beta release -> neutrophilic inflammation.

\medterm{Gout Flare}-- An acute inflammatory response to monosodium urate crystal deposition in or around a
joint. It commonly causes abrupt severe pain, swelling, warmth, and erythema, often
involving the first metatarsophalangeal joint; aspiration can confirm crystals and
exclude infection.

\medterm{Gout nodulosis}-- The formation of tophi—large, firm, subcutaneous nodules composed of monosodium urate
crystals—resulting from chronic, poorly controlled hyperuricemia. Tophi most commonly
occur at the ears, fingers, toes, olecranon bursae, and Achilles tendon, and may
ulcerate and discharge chalky white material.

\medterm{Gown}-- Protective clothing. Types: examination, surgical, isolation.

\medterm{Grade}-- Degree of cellular differentiation. Grade 1: well-differentiated. Grade 2: moderately
differentiated. Grade 3: poorly differentiated. Grade 4: undifferentiated. Higher grade
= more aggressive.

\medterm{Graded Potentials}-- Local changes in membrane potential that vary in magnitude proportional to stimulus
strength, unlike all-or-none action potentials. They include receptor potentials (in
sensory receptors), postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs and IPSPs at synapses), and pacemaker
potentials (in cardiac/specialized cells).

\medterm{Graft}-- Surgical procedure creating detour around blocked vessel using autologous vein
(saphenous) or prosthetic graft (PTFE, Dacron). See bypass graft.

\medterm{Graft (Periodontal)}-- Tissue used to cover exposed root or augment gingiva. Connective tissue graft (gold
standard), free gingival graft, allograft, xenograft.

\medterm{Graft-Versus-Host Disease}-- Immunological reaction where donor T cells attack recipient tissues after allogeneic
bone marrow transplant. Affects the skin, liver, and gastrointestinal tract. GI
manifestations include diarrhea, abdominal pain, nausea, anorexia, and gastrointestinal
bleeding.

\medterm{Graft-Versus-Host Disease (GVHD)}-- An immune-mediated complication of allogeneic hematopoietic transplantation in which
donor-derived immunocompetent T cells attack recipient tissues. Acute or chronic GVHD
commonly affects skin, liver, and gastrointestinal tract and requires prevention and
immunosuppressive treatment.

\medterm{Grafting}-- The surgical transfer of tissue from one site to another, or from one individual to
another. Types: (1) Autograft: same individual (gold standard, e.g., skin graft for
burns, bone graft for non-union, saphenous vein graft for CABG). (2) Allograft: same
species, different individual (cadaveric bone, cornea, heart valve, kidney).

\medterm{Gram Negative Bacilli}-- Rod-shaped gram-negative bacteria appearing pink on Gram stain owing to a thin
peptidoglycan layer and lipopolysaccharide outer membrane.

\medterm{Gram Positive Cocci}-- Spherical gram-positive bacteria staining purple owing to a thick peptidoglycan layer
and absent outer membrane. Clinically important genera include Staphylococcus,
Streptococcus, and Enterococcus, identifiable by arrangement (clusters, chains, pairs)
and hemolysis, and causing skin, pharyngeal, respiratory, and bloodstream infections.

\medterm{Gram stain}-- A differential staining technique that classifies bacteria into two major groups based
on cell wall structure. The procedure involves crystal violet staining, iodine mordant,
alcohol decolorisation, and safranin counterstain.

\medterm{Gram-Negative Bacteria}-- Bacteria that do not retain crystal violet in the Gram stain (appear pink/red),
characterized by a thin peptidoglycan layer and an outer membrane containing
lipopolysaccharide (endotoxin). Examples: Enterobacteriaceae (E.

\medterm{Gram-Negative Meningitis}-- Meningitis caused by gram-negative bacteria, including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella, and
Neisseria species. It can progress rapidly to sepsis, neurologic injury, and death and
requires urgent cerebrospinal-fluid evaluation and antimicrobial treatment.

\medterm{Gram-Positive Bacteria}-- Bacteria that retain crystal violet in the Gram stain (appear purple/blue),
characterized by a thick peptidoglycan cell wall and no outer membrane.

\medterm{Grand Mal}-- A type of generalised seizure characterized by bilateral synchronous epileptic activity
involving the entire brain. Formerly called grand mal, now classified as bilateral
tonic-clonic seizure according to the ILAE 2017 classification.

\medterm{Granular Casts}-- Degenerated cellular debris within tubular lumen appearing as coarse or fine-grained
structures. Coarse casts represent more acute process, fine casts indicate chronic
degenerative process. Common in acute tubular necrosis reflecting tubular epithelial
cell damage. Also seen in chronic kidney disease, nephrotic syndrome, and hypertension.

\medterm{Granulocyte}-- A type of white blood cell (leukocyte) containing cytoplasmic granules that are visible
on light microscopy. Granulocytes are produced in the bone marrow (myelopoiesis, 3-4
days maturation) and are part of the innate immune system.

\medterm{Granuloma}-- A distinctive pattern of chronic inflammation characterized by the aggregation of
modified macrophages (epithelioid histiocytes), often with multinucleated giant cells
(Langhans or foreign-body type), surrounded by lymphocytes. Granulomas form when
macrophages cannot eliminate a persistent antigen or pathogen.

\medterm{Granuloma Annulare}-- A benign inflammatory condition characterized by annular, erythematous to skin-colored
plaques with raised borders and central clearing. It most commonly affects the hands,
feet, and elbows. Localized form is most common; generalized form is associated with
diabetes mellitus.

\medterm{Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis}-- GPA (formerly Wegener granulomatosis): an ANCA-associated small-vessel necrotizing
vasculitis affecting the upper and lower respiratory tract and kidneys.

\medterm{Granulomatous amebic encephalitis}-- A rare, usually fatal central nervous system infection caused by free-living amoebae
such as Acanthamoeba species, typically affecting immunocompromised individuals. It
presents with a subacute to chronic meningoencephalitis and forms granulomatous lesions
in the brain.

\medterm{Granulomatous Inflammation}-- A distinctive pattern of chronic inflammation characterized by organized collections of
activated macrophages that have undergone morphological transformation into epithelioid
cells, often surrounded by a rim of lymphocytes and occasionally fibroblasts.

\medterm{Granulosa Cell Tumor}-- An estrogen-secreting ovarian sex cord-stromal tumor that can cause precocious puberty,
endometrial hyperplasia, or carcinoma. Generally low-grade with a tendency to late
recurrence.

\medterm{Graves Disease}-- Autoimmune disorder and most common cause of hyperthyroidism characterized by
thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins that bind and activate the thyroid-stimulating
hormone receptor causing unregulated thyroid hormone synthesis and secretion. The
diffuse goiter reflects TSH receptor stimulation.

\medterm{Gray (Gy)}-- The SI unit of absorbed radiation dose, equal to one joule of ionizing radiation energy
deposited per kilogram of matter. It describes physical energy absorption and differs
from the sievert, which incorporates biologic weighting.

\medterm{Gray Matter}-- Regions of the brain and spinal cord composed primarily of nerve cell bodies, dendrites,
and synapses, responsible for processing information.

\medterm{Greater Curvature}-- Convex lateral stomach border from gastroesophageal junction to pylorus, approximately
four times longer than the lesser curvature. Attachment site for the greater omentum.
Blood supply from short gastric and left gastroepicolic vessels. Common site for gastric
ulcers and cancer.

\medterm{Greater Trochanter}-- Large quadrilateral lateral proximal femoral prominence. Insertion for gluteus
medius/minimus and piriformis. Palpable landmark and site of trochanteric bursa.
Intertrochanteric fractures between trochanters are extracapsular with lower avascular
necrosis risk because periosteal blood supply is preserved.

\medterm{Grey Turner's Sign}-- Bluish or violaceous ecchymosis of the flanks caused by retroperitoneal bleeding
tracking through the abdominal wall. It is classically associated with severe
haemorrhagic pancreatitis but may occur with other retroperitoneal haemorrhage.

\medterm{Grid Implant}-- See mesh implant.

\medterm{Grief}-- A natural emotional response to loss, particularly the death of a loved one, but also
following other significant losses such as divorce, job loss, or declining health. It
encompasses a range of feelings including sadness, anger, guilt, and numbness, often
occurring in waves rather than linear stages.

\medterm{Griping (see Colic)}-- Severe abdominal pain, typically spasmodic in nature. See Colic.

\medterm{Grippe (see Influenza)}-- An older term for influenza. See Influenza.

\medterm{Griscelli Syndrome}-- Three types: Type 1 (MYO5A mutation): primary neurologic impairment, no
immunodeficiency; Type 2 (RAB27A mutation): immunodeficiency + partial albinism, HLH
risk; Type 3 (MLPH mutation): only partial albinism. Type 2: silvery hair, large
melanosomes in melanocytes, defective cytotoxic granule release -> HLH.

\medterm{Gross Examination}-- The macroscopic, naked-eye inspection of a specimen in the pathology laboratory,
performed before microscopic sectioning and the essential first step in pathologic
diagnosis of resected tissues and organs.

\medterm{Gross Motor Skills}-- The ability to use large muscle groups for movement and posture, developing in a
predictable sequence in infancy/childhood: head control (3 months), sitting (6 months),
crawling (9 months), standing (10-12 months), walking (12-18 months), running/jumping
(2-3 years), hopping and balance (3-5 years).

\medterm{Ground-Glass Opacity}-- Hazy lung opacity on CT without obscuring vessels. Non-specific, may represent
inflammation, edema, fibrosis, malignancy.

\medterm{Group A Strep}-- Group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes), a gram-positive coccus in chains,
beta-hemolytic. Infections: pharyngitis/tonsillitis, impetigo, erysipelas, cellulitis,
scarlet fever, necrotizing fasciitis, streptococcal toxic shock syndrome, pneumonia, and
puerperal sepsis.

\medterm{Group A streptococcus}-- See Group A Strep.

\medterm{Group B Strep}-- Group B Streptococcus (GBS, Streptococcus agalactiae): a gram-positive coccus colonizing
the GI and genitourinary tract in 10-30 percent of women; major cause of neonatal sepsis
and meningitis.

\medterm{Group B Streptococcus}-- A gram-positive bacterium (Streptococcus agalactiae) colonizing the genitourinary and
gastrointestinal tracts. Asymptomatic vaginal colonization occurs in 20-30\1 of pregnant
women. Screening is performed at 36-37 weeks gestation via rectovaginal swab culture.

\medterm{Growing Pains}-- Recurrent, bilateral leg pain in children ages 3-12, typically occurring in the late
afternoon or evening and resolving by morning. Pain is deep, aching in the calves,
shins, and thighs (not joints). No limping, fever, swelling, or functional impairment.

\medterm{Growth Chart}-- A graphical representation of growth (weight, height/length, head circumference, BMI)
plotted against age, standardized by population (WHO growth standards 0-5 years; CDC
growth charts 2-20 years; percentiles/z-scores).

\medterm{Growth Factor}-- Proteins that stimulate cell growth, proliferation, differentiation, and survival. Major
families: EGF (epidermal growth factor), FGF (fibroblast growth factor), PDGF
(platelet-derived), VEGF (vascular endothelial), TGF-beta (transforming), NGF (nerve),
IGF (insulin-like), HGF (hepatocyte).

\medterm{Growth Hormone}-- Pituitary hormone. Stimulates IGF-1 production. Lipolytic, anti-insulin effects.

\medterm{Growth Hormone Deficiency}-- Insufficient secretion of growth hormone from the anterior pituitary, causing short
stature in children and metabolic effects in adults. In children: delayed growth, short
stature, delayed bone age, and increased fat mass. In adults: decreased muscle mass,
increased fat mass, reduced bone density, fatigue, and dyslipidemia.

\medterm{Growth Hormone Physiology}-- Growth hormone is secreted in a pulsatile pattern by pituitary somatotroph cells
regulated by hypothalamic GHRH which stimulates release and somatostatin which inhibits
release. GH acts directly on tissues and indirectly through IGF-1 produced primarily by
the liver.

\medterm{Growth Medium}-- A nutrient preparation supporting microbial multiplication in vitro, supplying carbon,
nitrogen, minerals, and growth factors in liquid or solid form.

\medterm{Growth Retardation}-- Abnormally slow physical growth or failure to reach expected height or weight for age
and developmental stage. Causes include inadequate nutrition, chronic systemic disease,
endocrine or genetic disorders, malabsorption, psychosocial deprivation, and
constitutional variation; assessment uses growth charts and clinical context.

\medterm{Growth Scan}-- An ultrasound performed in the third trimester (usually 28-32 weeks and/or 36-38 weeks)
to assess fetal growth. Measurements: BPD, HC, AC, FL, and estimated fetal weight (EFW).
Growth percentiles plotted on a growth curve. Abnormal: <10th percentile (SGA/IUGR) or
>90th percentile (LGA/macrosomia.

\medterm{Guaifenesin}-- An expectorant that thins and loosens bronchial mucus by increasing respiratory tract
fluid and reducing mucus viscosity, facilitating productive cough. Used for symptomatic
relief of chest congestion and productive cough in upper respiratory infections and
COPD. Typical adult dose 200-400 mg every 4 hours.

\medterm{Guided Tissue Regeneration}-- Periodontal procedure using barrier membrane to promote regeneration of bone, cementum,
periodontal ligament.

\medterm{Guillain-Barre Syndrome}-- An acute, immune-mediated polyneuropathy typically presenting with ascending paralysis
following an antecedent infection (Campylobacter jejuni, CMV, EBV, Zika). Variants:
acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (AIDP, most common in North America),
acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN, more common in Asia).

\medterm{Gummy Smile}-- Excessive gingival display when smiling. Caused by hypermobile lip, vertical maxillary
excess, altered passive eruption. Treatment: surgery, Botox, orthodontics.

\medterm{Gunshot Wound}-- An injury produced by a projectile discharged from a firearm; forensic classification by
range and direction. Entrance wound: rounded/ovoid defect with an abraded ring and often
a zone of soot (close range) or stippling/tattooing (intermediate range); contact wounds
show muzzle imprint.

\medterm{Gut Microbiome}-- The complex community of trillions of microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, and
fungi, residing in the gastrointestinal tract. The gut microbiome plays a crucial role
in digestion, immune function, vitamin synthesis, and protection against pathogens.

\medterm{Guttate Psoriasis}-- An acute eruption of numerous small, scaly, drop-like psoriatic papules and plaques,
often following streptococcal pharyngitis in children or young adults. Diagnosis is
clinical; the eruption may resolve spontaneously or evolve into chronic plaque
psoriasis.

\medterm{Gynaecologist}-- A medical doctor who specializes in the health of the female reproductive system
(vagina, cervix, uterus, fallopian tubes, ovaries) and breasts.

\medterm{Gynaecomastia}-- The benign enlargement of breast tissue in males. Pathophysiology: an imbalance between
estrogen (stimulates breast tissue growth) and androgen (suppresses) activity, resulting
in increased estrogen-to-testosterone ratio.

\medterm{gynecology}-- The medical specialty concerned with the health of the female reproductive system
(uterus, ovaries, fallopian tubes, cervix, vagina, and vulva).

\medterm{Gynecomastia}-- Enlargement of breast tissue in males, often bilateral, occurring physiologically in
neonates, during puberty (40-60\1 of boys), and in aging men. Pathologic causes include
Klinefelter syndrome, testicular tumors, medications (spironolactone, cannabis), and
liver disease. Persistent or asymmetric gynecomastia warrants evaluation.

\medterm{Gyrus}-- A ridge or convolution on the surface of the cerebral cortex, separated by sulci
(grooves). The folded structure triples the surface area of the brain (approximately
$2500\,\mathrm{cm^2}$) while fitting within the cranial vault.

\medterm{Gastrointestinal Pain}-- Pain arising from the digestive tract or related organs. Causes include indigestion, infection,
ulcer disease, inflammation, obstruction, gallstones, and disorders of the liver or pancreas.
Severe, persistent, or worsening pain requires medical assessment.
