
\medterm{B And T Cell Screen} B and T cell screen uses immunophenotyping to measure and characterize circulating B lymphocytes and T lymphocytes, supporting evaluation of immune deficiency, leukemia, and lymphoma.

\medterm{B And T Lymphocytes} B lymphocytes produce antibodies, while T lymphocytes coordinate immune responses or kill infected and abnormal cells; both are adaptive immune cells.
\medterm{B Cell} A lymphocyte that develops in the bone marrow and can differentiate into antibody-producing plasma cells or memory B cells after activation.

\synonyms
B lymphocyte; B-cell.

\medterm{B Cell (B Lymphocyte)} A key cellular component of adaptive humoral immunity that matures in the bone marrow.
Upon antigen recognition via B-cell receptor (BCR/surface IgM) and T-cell help
(CD40/CD40L interaction), B cells proliferate and differentiate into antibody-secreting
plasma cells and long-lived memory B cells.

\synonyms
B Lymphocyte

\medterm{B Chromosome} A supernumerary chromosome that is not part of the standard chromosome complement and is
not required for normal development. It may occur in certain plants, animals, or tumors
and varies in number between cells.

\medterm{B Hemophilia (Hemophilia)} Alternate index label for Hemophilia.

\medterm{B Virus} Herpes B virus, also called Macacine alphaherpesvirus 1, a macaque virus that can cause severe and sometimes fatal encephalomyelitis in humans after exposure to infected animals or tissues.

\medterm{B-Cell Activation} The process by which a B lymphocyte is stimulated by antigen and other immune signals to multiply and develop into antibody-producing plasma cells or memory B cells.

\medterm{B-Cell Leukemia/Lymphoma Panel} B-cell leukemia/lymphoma panel uses flow cytometry and antibody markers to identify abnormal B-cell populations in blood, marrow, or tissue.

\medterm{B-Cell Lymphoma} A group of lymphatic cancers arising from B lymphocytes.

\medterm{B-Cell Receptor} The membrane-bound immunoglobulin complex on a B lymphocyte that recognizes native
antigen. BCR signaling, with appropriate co-stimulation, promotes B-cell activation,
proliferation, differentiation, and antibody production.

\medterm{B-Lymphocyte} A B lymphocyte is an immune cell that can mature into an antibody-producing plasma cell or a memory B cell.

\medterm{B-Lynch Suture} A surgical technique for controlling postpartum hemorrhage due to uterine atony
unresponsive to medical management. A compressive suture placed around the uterus to
mechanically compress the uterine walls and reduce blood flow. The suture creates a
brace-like effect. Can be combined with other compression sutures (Cho, Hayman).

\medterm{B-Mode Ultrasound} Brightness-mode ultrasonography, which produces a two-dimensional image from echoes returning from tissues. It is the standard imaging mode for many organs and soft tissues.

\medterm{Ba} The chemical symbol for barium, a metal used in compounds such as barium sulfate for contrast studies of the digestive tract.

\medterm{Babcock Clamp} An atraumatic surgical clamp with fenestrated jaws used to hold delicate tubular tissue.

\medterm{Babesia} A genus of intraerythrocytic protozoan parasites transmitted mainly by Ixodes ticks; infection (babesiosis) can cause fever, haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and severe disease in people without a spleen or with impaired immunity.

\medterm{Babesia Divergens} A species of Babesia, an intraerythrocytic protozoan parasite transmitted by Ixodes ticks, primarily causing babesiosis in Europe. It can cause severe hemolytic anemia, particularly in asplenic or immunocompromised individuals.

\medterm{Babesia Microti} The most common cause of babesiosis in North America, an intraerythrocytic protozoan parasite transmitted by Ixodes scapularis ticks. Infection can range from asymptomatic to severe hemolytic anemia with fever, jaundice, and organ failure in immunocompromised patients.

\medterm{Babesiosis} A tick-borne infection caused by Babesia parasites, commonly B. microti in the United States and B. divergens in Europe. The parasites infect red blood cells and can cause fever, fatigue, haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, jaundice, and haemoglobinuria; severe disease is more likely in people without a spleen or with impaired immunity.

\medterm{Babinski Reflex} An extensor plantar response present from birth to approximately 2 years. When the
lateral sole is stroked, the great toe dorsiflexes and other toes fan outward.
Disappears as the corticospinal tract myelinates. Persistence beyond age 2 may indicate
neurologic abnormality.

\medterm{Babinski Response} Extension of the great toe and fanning of the other toes when the sole is stimulated; it is normal in infants but may indicate corticospinal-tract dysfunction in older children or adults.

\medterm{Babinski Sign} The Babinski sign is a pathological reflex elicited by stroking the lateral aspect of
the sole of the foot from the heel to the metatarsal heads with a blunt object. A normal
response (flexor plantar response) is plantar flexion (curling downward) of all toes.

\medterm{Baby Acne} Small red or white bumps on an infant's skin, usually caused by temporary hormonal effects and often resolving without treatment.

\medterm{Baby Blues} A common, short-lived period of tearfulness, irritability, anxiety, or mood lability during the first days after childbirth, usually resolving within about two weeks.

\medterm{Babyhood} Babyhood is the period of infancy, when rapid growth, brain development, motor skills, feeding, and immune maturation occur.
\medterm{Bacillaceae} A family of Gram-positive, spore-forming rod-shaped bacteria in the order Bacillales. It includes medically important genera such as Bacillus and Clostridium, some of which cause anthrax, botulism, or gas gangrene.

\medterm{Bacillary Angiomatosis} A vascular proliferative disease caused by Bartonella henselae or B. quintana, occurring
in immunocompromised patients (HIV/AIDS, transplant). Presents with erythematous
papules, nodules, or pedunculated lesions resembling Kaposi sarcoma or pyogenic
granuloma. Can involve skin, liver (peliosis hepatis), spleen, bone, and lymph nodes.

\medterm{Bacillary Dysentery} Acute inflammatory diarrhea caused by Shigella species, typically producing fever, abdominal cramps, and frequent stools that may contain blood or mucus.

\medterm{Bacille Calmette-GuéRin (BCG)} Bacille Calmette-Guérin is a live attenuated mycobacterial vaccine used mainly against tuberculosis and, in some settings, as bladder-cancer immunotherapy.
\medterm{Bacillophobia} An excessive fear of bacteria or germs; it may lead to avoidance, repeated cleaning, or other anxiety-driven behaviors.

\medterm{Bacilluria} The presence of bacilli in the urine, usually referring to rod-shaped bacteria detected
by microscopy or culture. Its clinical significance depends on symptoms, quantitative
culture, and the organism identified.

\medterm{Bacillus} A rod-shaped bacterium. The term may refer broadly to bacterial shape or specifically to members of the genus \textit{Bacillus}, some of which form resistant spores and cause human disease.

\medterm{Bacillus Anthracis} The spore-forming bacterium that causes anthrax. Infection may be cutaneous, gastrointestinal, or inhalational.

\medterm{Bacillus Cereus} A spore-forming bacterium that can cause food poisoning, usually through a rapid-onset vomiting syndrome or a diarrheal syndrome after ingestion of contaminated food.

\medterm{Bacillus Lentus} A Gram-positive, spore-forming rod bacterium in the genus Bacillus, commonly found in soil. It is generally of low virulence but may cause opportunistic infections in immunocompromised individuals.

\medterm{Bacillus Licheniformis} A Gram-positive, spore-forming rod bacterium found in soil and plant surfaces. It is an occasional cause of food contamination and has been rarely associated with opportunistic infections in immunocompromised patients.

\medterm{Bacillus Megaterium} A large, Gram-positive, spore-forming rod bacterium found in soil and water. It is of low pathogenicity but has been reported as an opportunistic cause of bacteremia and endocarditis in rare cases.

\medterm{Bacillus Mycoides} A Gram-positive, spore-forming soil bacterium recognized by its distinctive rhizoid colony morphology. It is not considered a significant human pathogen but is studied as an environmental indicator organism.

\medterm{Bacillus Pumilus} A Gram-positive, spore-forming rod bacterium commonly found in soil and on skin. It has been associated with rare opportunistic infections, including endophthalmitis and bacteremia, particularly in immunocompromised individuals.

\medterm{Bacillus Subtilis} A Gram-positive, spore-forming rod bacterium widely distributed in soil and vegetation. Generally considered non-pathogenic, it is used as a probiotic and in biotechnology; rare opportunistic infections have been reported.

\medterm{Bacitracin} Bacitracin is a topical polypeptide antibiotic that interferes with bacterial cell-wall synthesis and is used for selected superficial infections.

\medterm{Bacitracin Overdose} Toxic effects from excessive exposure to bacitracin. Topical exposure usually causes local irritation, while large, ingested, or systemic exposures may cause gastrointestinal symptoms or kidney injury; assessment depends on the dose and route.

\medterm{Bacitracin Zinc Overdose} Toxic effects from excessive exposure to bacitracin zinc. Severity depends on the dose and route; large exposures may cause gastrointestinal symptoms, allergic reactions, or kidney injury and require prompt assessment.

\medterm{Back Injury} Damage to the muscles, ligaments, joints, discs, vertebrae, or nerves of the back caused by trauma, lifting, or repetitive
strain. Pain and stiffness are common; weakness, numbness, loss of bladder or bowel control, or severe pain
after major trauma requires urgent assessment.

\medterm{Back Pain} One of the most common medical complaints, affecting up to 80 percent of the population
at some point in life, and the leading cause of disability worldwide. Back pain is
classified by duration: acute (less than 4 weeks), subacute (4-12 weeks), and chronic
(greater than 12 weeks).

\synonyms
Pain, Back

\medterm{Back Pressure} Back pressure is pressure exerted in the reverse direction of flow; in medicine it may result from obstruction in an airway, vessel, duct, or organ.
\medterm{Back-Blows} Back-blows are firm blows between the shoulder blades used as a first-aid maneuver for severe choking in a responsive person.
\medterm{Backache} Pain or discomfort located anywhere in the posterior trunk from neck to buttocks, a
symptom rather than a disease. Causes include musculoskeletal strain (most common), disc
herniation, spinal stenosis, facet arthropathy, sacroiliac joint dysfunction, and
referred pain from renal, pancreatic, or vascular sources.

\medterm{Baclofen} A muscle relaxant that activates GABA-B receptors and is used mainly to reduce spasticity. Drowsiness, weakness, confusion, and dangerous withdrawal after abrupt stopping can occur.

\medterm{Bacteraemia} Bacteraemia is the presence of viable bacteria in the bloodstream; it may be transient or lead to sepsis and infection of distant sites.
\medterm{Bacteremia} The presence of viable bacteria in the bloodstream, a serious condition that can be
transient (after dental procedures, toothbrushing), intermittent (undrained abscess), or
continuous (endovascular infection like endocarditis). Bacteremia triggers systemic
inflammatory responses ranging from fever and leukocytosis to septic shock.

\medterm{Bacteria} Single-celled microscopic organisms lacking a nucleus, which can exist independently or
in colonies; some are beneficial to health (microbiome) while others cause infectious
disease.

\medterm{Bacterial Adherence} The process by which bacteria attach to host cell surfaces, a critical first step in
colonisation and infection. Adherence is mediated by adhesins, which are surface
structures that bind to specific receptors on host cells. Adhesins include pili,
fimbriae, outer membrane proteins, and capsules.

\medterm{Bacterial Biofilms} Structured communities of bacteria attached to surfaces and embedded in a self-produced
matrix of extracellular polymeric substances. Biofilms are a major cause of chronic
infections, particularly on medical devices including catheters, prosthetic joints, and
heart valves.

\medterm{Bacterial Capsule} A polysaccharide layer surrounding the bacterial cell wall that protects against
phagocytosis and complement-mediated killing. Capsules are major virulence factors for
many pathogens including Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and
Klebsiella pneumoniae.

\medterm{Bacterial Cell Wall} The rigid outer layer of bacteria that provides structural integrity and determines cell
shape. The cell wall is composed primarily of peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugars
cross-linked by short peptides.

\medterm{Bacterial Classification} The organisation of bacteria into taxonomic groups based on genetic, phenotypic, and
evolutionary relationships. Bacteria are classified using a hierarchical system: domain,
phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species.

\medterm{Bacterial Conjugation} The transfer of genetic material between bacteria through direct cell-to-cell contact
via a pilus. Conjugation is mediated by conjugative plasmids that encode the genes for
transfer. The donor cell (F+) forms a conjugative pilus that attaches to the recipient
cell (F-).

\medterm{Bacterial Conjunctivitis} A contagious infection of the conjunctiva, often caused by staphylococci or streptococci. It commonly causes eye redness, irritation, a gritty sensation, and mucopurulent discharge that can mat the eyelids, especially after sleep. Symptoms overlap with viral and allergic conjunctivitis, and most uncomplicated cases improve within 1--2 weeks; topical antibiotics may shorten the illness. Contact-lens wearers with pain, light sensitivity, or reduced vision need prompt eye assessment.

\medterm{Bacterial Dermatitis} Inflammation of the skin caused by bacterial infection, typically presenting with
erythema, edema, warmth, pain, and purulent discharge. Common causative organisms
include Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes, and the condition may arise de
novo or as a superinfection of pre-existing dermatitis.

\medterm{Bacterial Dormancy} A state of reduced metabolic activity that allows bacteria to survive unfavourable
conditions. Dormant forms include spores, persisters, and viable but non-culturable
cells. Spores are highly resistant structures formed by Bacillus and Clostridium.
Persisters are phenotypic variants that are tolerant to antibiotics.

\medterm{Bacterial Endocarditis} Infection of the heart's inner lining or valves by bacteria, usually producing fever and a new
or changing heart murmur. It can cause valve damage and embolic complications.

\medterm{Bacterial Endocarditis Vegetations} Vegetations in bacterial endocarditis are complex masses composed of bacteria, fibrin,
and platelets that form on heart valve surfaces or endocardium. The pathogenesis begins
with bacteremia seeding a site of endothelial damage or a previously abnormal valve
surface.

\medterm{Bacterial Endospores} Highly resistant dormant structures formed by certain gram-positive bacteria including
Bacillus and Clostridium species. Endospores form during nutrient deprivation and can
survive for decades. They are resistant to heat, radiation, chemicals, and desiccation.

\medterm{Bacterial Flagella} Thread-like appendages composed of flagellin protein that provide motility to bacteria.
Flagella are composed of three parts: the filament, hook, and basal body. The basal body
is embedded in the cell wall and functions as a rotary motor.

\medterm{Bacterial Gastroenteritis} Acute inflammation of the stomach and intestines caused by pathogenic bacteria such as
Salmonella, Campylobacter, Shigella, or pathogenic Escherichia coli. It commonly causes
diarrhea, abdominal cramps, fever, and sometimes bloody stools or vomiting.

\medterm{Bacterial Genetics} The study of genes and genetic processes in bacteria. Bacteria have a single circular
chromosome and may contain plasmids, which are extrachromosomal DNA elements. Bacterial
genes are organized into operons, which are clusters of genes regulated as a unit.

\medterm{Bacterial Growth Phases} The stages of bacterial growth in culture. The lag phase is when bacteria adapt to their
environment. The log (exponential) phase is when bacteria multiply at their maximum
rate. The stationary phase is when nutrient depletion and waste accumulation limit
growth. The death phase is when bacteria die.

\medterm{Bacterial Infections} Illnesses caused by pathogenic bacteria proliferating in the body, ranging from mild
sore throats and ear infections to life-threatening sepsis and meningitis. They are
diagnosed through cultures, PCR testing, or other microbiological methods.

\medterm{Bacterial Meningitis} Infection of the meninges by bacteria, a medical emergency. In neonates: Group B Streptococcus, E. coli, Listeria. In older children: Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus pneumoniae. Presents with fever, headache, neck stiffness, altered consciousness, and Kernig/Brudzinski signs.

\medterm{Bacterial Metabolism} The chemical processes that occur within bacteria to maintain life. Bacteria have
diverse metabolic capabilities including aerobic and anaerobic respiration,
fermentation, and photosynthesis. Metabolic pathways are targets for antimicrobial
drugs.

\medterm{Bacterial Morphology} The shape and arrangement of bacteria as observed under microscopy. Basic shapes include
cocci (spheres), bacilli (rods), and spirilla (spirals). Cocci can be arranged in pairs
(diplococci), chains (streptococci), clusters (staphylococci), or tetrads. Bacilli can
be single, paired, or arranged in chains.

\medterm{Bacterial Overgrowth} Excessive proliferation of bacteria in the small intestine, commonly called
small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth. It may result from impaired intestinal motility,
anatomical stasis, blind loops, strictures, diverticula, or loss of the normal
ileocaecal barrier.

\synonyms
small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth.

\medterm{Bacterial Pathogenesis} The mechanisms by which bacteria cause disease. Pathogenesis involves adhesion, invasion, toxin production, and immune evasion. Adhesins mediate attachment to host cells. Invasins allow penetration of tissues. Toxins damage host cells.

\medterm{Bacterial Peritonitis} Infection of the peritoneal cavity, which may occur spontaneously in patients with ascites or secondarily after perforation, surgery, or another intra-abdominal infection. It requires prompt evaluation and antimicrobial treatment.

\medterm{Bacterial Pharyngitis} Infection and inflammation of the pharynx caused by bacteria, most commonly group A Streptococcus. Symptoms may include sore throat, fever, painful swallowing, and tender cervical lymph nodes.

\medterm{Bacterial Pili} Hair-like appendages on the bacterial surface composed of pilin protein. Pili are
shorter and thicker than flagella. Type I pili mediate adherence to host cells and are
important virulence factors. Type IV pili are involved in twitching motility and DNA
uptake during transformation.

\medterm{Bacterial Pneumonia} Infection of the lung tissue by bacteria, causing inflammation and air-space filling. It may cause
fever, cough, sputum, chest pain, and shortness of breath.

\medterm{Bacterial Protein} A protein produced by a bacterium that may form part of its structure, support its metabolism, help it attach to host cells, or contribute to disease.

\medterm{Bacterial Spore} A metabolically dormant, highly resistant structure produced by certain bacteria,
notably Bacillus and Clostridioides species, in response to environmental stress. Spores
resist desiccation, heat, and many disinfectants and require validated sterilization for
reliable destruction.

\medterm{Bacterial Toxemia} The presence or systemic effects of bacterial toxins in the bloodstream. It may occur during severe bacterial infection and can contribute to shock or organ injury.

\medterm{Bacterial Toxin} A harmful substance produced or released by bacteria. Toxins may damage cells directly or disrupt normal body functions and include secreted exotoxins and components such as endotoxin.

\medterm{Bacterial Toxins} Substances produced by bacteria that cause damage to host cells. There are two major
categories: exotoxins and endotoxins. Exotoxins are proteins secreted by bacteria that
have specific toxic effects. Endotoxins are lipopolysaccharide components of
gram-negative bacterial outer membranes released during cell lysis.

\medterm{Bacterial Tracheitis} Bacterial infection and inflammation of the trachea, often causing fever, a harsh cough, noisy
breathing, and potentially rapid airway obstruction.

\medterm{Bacterial Transduction} The transfer of bacterial DNA from one bacterium to another through bacteriophages
(bacterial viruses).

\medterm{Bacterial Transformation} The uptake of free DNA from the environment by competent bacteria. Transformation occurs
naturally in some bacterial species including Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus
influenzae, and Neisseria. Competence is the ability to take up DNA and is regulated by
specific genes.

\medterm{Bacterial Typing} Laboratory methods used to distinguish bacterial strains within the same species, especially for tracking transmission, investigating outbreaks, or linking an isolate to a source.

\medterm{Bacterial Vaginosis} BV: replacement of normal vaginal lactobacilli by an overgrowth of mixed anaerobes
(Gardnerella vaginalis, Mobiluncus, etc.), the most common cause of vaginal discharge in
reproductive-age women. Risk: new/multiple partners, douching, smoking.

\medterm{Bacterial Vaginosis (Causes, Symptoms, Treatment)} Alternate index label for Causes, Symptoms, Treatment.


\medterm{Bacterial Virulence Factors} Molecules produced by bacteria that contribute to their ability to cause disease. Virulence factors include adhesins, invasins, toxins, capsules, and enzymes. Adhesins mediate attachment to host cells. Toxins damage host cells directly. Capsules protect against phagocytosis.

\medterm{Bactericide} An agent that kills bacteria rather than merely inhibiting their growth
(bacteriostatic). Bactericidal antibiotics include beta-lactams (penicillins,
cephalosporins, carbapenems), aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, metronidazole, and
vancomycin.

\medterm{Bacteriocin} An antibacterial protein or peptide produced by some bacteria that inhibits or kills susceptible bacteria, often closely related strains.

\medterm{Bacteriology} The branch of microbiology concerned with the study of bacteria, including their
morphology, genetics, physiology, ecology, and clinical significance.

\medterm{Bacteriophage} A virus that infects and replicates within bacteria.

\medterm{Bacteriophage Lambda} A virus that infects Escherichia coli. It can replicate by destroying the bacterial cell or integrate its genetic material into the bacterial chromosome as a prophage.

\medterm{Bacteriophage T4} A virus that infects Escherichia coli and usually replicates by producing new phage particles and breaking open the bacterial cell.

\medterm{Bacteriophage Therapy} The investigational use of bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria, to treat selected
bacterial infections. Their activity is usually limited to susceptible bacterial strains.

\medterm{Bacteriophages} Viruses that infect bacteria and use bacterial cells to reproduce. They may destroy the host cell or insert their genetic material into the bacterial genome.

\medterm{Bacteriorhodopsin} A light-sensitive protein in some microorganisms that uses light energy to pump protons across the cell membrane and help generate chemical energy.

\medterm{Bacteriostasis} Inhibition of bacterial growth and reproduction without necessarily killing the bacteria.

\medterm{Bacteriostatic} Describing an agent or condition that inhibits bacterial growth rather than directly killing bacteria.

\medterm{Bacterium} A unicellular prokaryote lacking a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, possessing a
single circular chromosome and typically a peptidoglycan cell wall.

\medterm{Bacteriuria} The presence of bacteria in urine, detected by urinalysis or culture. It may occur with
urinary symptoms, as in a urinary-tract infection, or without symptoms, as in
asymptomatic bacteriuria; interpretation depends on specimen quality, bacterial
quantity, patient context, and the presence of inflammation or symptoms.

\medterm{Bacteroidaceae} A family of mostly Gram-negative anaerobic bacteria that includes Bacteroides and commonly forms part of the normal intestinal microbiota. Some species can cause opportunistic infection when they enter sterile tissues.

\medterm{Bacteroides} A genus of mostly Gram-negative anaerobic bacteria that commonly live in the intestine. They are usually harmless in the gut but can cause abscesses and other infections when displaced into sterile sites.

\medterm{Bacteroides Caccae} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterial species found mainly in the intestine and occasionally recovered from polymicrobial abdominal or bloodstream infections.

\medterm{Bacteroides Dorei} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterial species found in the human intestine and usually considered part of the normal gut microbiota.

\medterm{Bacteroides Eggerthii} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterial species found in the human intestine that is usually a commensal organism but can rarely be associated with opportunistic infection.

\medterm{Bacteroides Faecis} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterial species associated with the human intestinal microbiota and occasionally recovered from polymicrobial infections.

\medterm{Bacteroides Fragilis} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterium that is part of the intestinal microbiota and is an important cause of intra-abdominal abscesses and other infections after bowel disruption.

\medterm{Bacteroides Gingivalis} An older name for Porphyromonas gingivalis, an anaerobic oral bacterium associated with dental plaque and periodontal disease.

\medterm{Bacteroides Heparinolyticus} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterial species that can be found in human-associated microbial communities and may rarely be recovered from clinical infections.

\medterm{Bacteroides Intestinalis} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterial species found in the human intestinal microbiota; it is usually a commensal organism but may be recovered from polymicrobial infections.

\medterm{Bacteroides Nordii} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterial species found in the intestine and occasionally identified in polymicrobial clinical specimens.

\medterm{Bacteroides Ovatus} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterium that is part of the intestinal microbiota and can contribute to opportunistic infection if it enters damaged or normally sterile tissue.

\medterm{Bacteroides Pyogenes} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterial species that may occur in human-associated microbiota and has occasionally been recovered from polymicrobial infections.

\medterm{Bacteroides Salyersiae} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterial species associated with the human intestine and occasionally implicated in opportunistic infection.

\medterm{Bacteroides Stercoris} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterial species commonly associated with intestinal microbiota and only rarely reported in clinical infection.

\medterm{Bacteroides Thetaiotaomicron} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterium that is an important member of the human gut microbiota and helps break down complex dietary carbohydrates; it can cause opportunistic infection outside the intestine.

\medterm{Bacteroides Uniformis} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterial species commonly found in the human intestine and usually regarded as a commensal organism.

\medterm{Bacteroides Ureolyticus} A bacterial name historically applied to an anaerobic, urease-producing organism now generally classified as Campylobacter ureolyticus, which can be associated with wound or bloodstream infection.

\medterm{Bacteroides Vulgatus} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterium commonly found in the human intestine that can contribute to infection when the intestinal barrier is disrupted.

\medterm{Bacteroides Xylanisolvens} An anaerobic Gram-negative bacterial species found in the human intestine that can break down plant-derived carbohydrates and is usually a commensal organism.

\medterm{Bacteroidete} A member of the bacterial group now commonly called Bacteroidota, which includes many Gram-negative organisms found in soil, water, and the human intestine.

\medterm{Baculoviridae} Baculoviridae is a virus or viral group classified by its genetic and biologic features; clinical importance depends on the strain, host, route of transmission, tissue tropism, and ability to cause disease.

\medterm{Bad Breath} An unpleasant odor from the mouth, medically called halitosis. It most often results from bacteria, food debris, gum disease, tooth decay, dry mouth, or tonsil problems; less commonly, it may be related to nose, throat, digestive, or other medical conditions.

\synonyms
Breath Bad (Bad Breath)

\medterm{Bad Protein} Bad protein is a protein or protein-related molecule that performs a defined structural, signaling, transport, immune, or enzymatic function in cells; abnormal amounts or activity may contribute to disease.

\medterm{BAER - Brainstem Auditory Evoked Response} BAER records electrical responses from the auditory nerve and brainstem after sound stimulation, helping assess hearing pathways when behavioral testing is difficult.

\medterm{Bag-Mask Ventilation} Manual ventilation of a non-breathing or inadequately breathing patient using a
self-inflating bag (AMBU) attached to a mask sealed over the face. Effective technique:
correct mask size, jaw thrust/head extension, two-person technique (two-hand seal),
adequate tidal volumes with ETCO2 monitoring.

\medterm{Bagassosis} An occupational hypersensitivity pneumonitis (extrinsic allergic alveolitis) caused by
inhalation of dust from moldy bagasse—the fibrous residue of sugarcane after extraction.
Caused by thermophilic actinomycetes (Saccharopolyspora rectivirgula) that colonize
stored bagasse.

\medterm{Bags Under Eyes} Mild swelling or fullness beneath the eyes, commonly related to aging, fluid retention, or inherited anatomy.

Puffy Eyes

\medterm{Bainbridge Reflex} An increase in heart rate produced by stretch of the atrial walls, especially during
increased venous return. The reflex helps limit venous congestion by adjusting cardiac
output.

\medterm{Bak Protein} Bak protein is a protein or protein-related molecule that performs a defined structural, signaling, transport, immune, or enzymatic function in cells; abnormal amounts or activity may contribute to disease.

\medterm{Baker Cyst} A fluid-filled cyst in the popliteal fossa, resulting from herniation of the synovial
membrane through the posterior knee joint capsule or from a communication between the
knee joint and the gastrocnemius-semimembranosus bursa.

\synonyms
Bakers Cyst (Baker Cyst)
Popliteal Cyst (Baker Cyst)

\medterm{Baker's Cyst} A fluid-filled synovial cyst arising from the gastrocnemius-semimembranosus bursa
posterior to the knee, typically caused by chronic knee joint effusion from conditions
such as osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, or meniscal tears. It presents as a
palpable, tender mass in the popliteal fossa that may rupture causing calf swelling.

\medterm{Bakers Cyst (Baker Cyst)} Alternate index label for Baker Cyst.

\medterm{Baking Powder Overdose} Toxic effects from ingesting a large amount of baking powder, which may cause abdominal distention, vomiting, excess sodium, electrolyte disturbances, or metabolic alkalosis. Severity depends on the amount and ingredients.

\medterm{Baking Soda Overdose} Toxic effects from ingesting excessive sodium bicarbonate, which may cause vomiting, abdominal distention, high blood sodium, metabolic alkalosis, or fluid disturbances. Severe exposure requires prompt assessment.

\medterm{Balamuthia} A biomedical term referring to balamuthia; its classification, distinguishing features, and medical significance depend on the clinical or biologic context.

\medterm{Balamuthia Mandrillaris} A biomedical term referring to balamuthia mandrillaris; its classification, distinguishing features, and medical significance depend on the clinical or biologic context.

\medterm{Balamuthia Mandrillaris Infection} A rare, usually fatal infection caused by the free-living amoeba Balamuthia mandrillaris. It may
begin as a skin lesion and progress to granulomatous amoebic encephalitis with headache, confusion, seizures, or
focal neurologic deficits. Diagnosis is difficult and requires specialized testing; urgent infectious-disease and
neurologic evaluation is essential.

\medterm{Balance} The ability to maintain the body's center of mass over its base of support, relying on
input from the vestibular system, vision, and proprioception. It is essential for
walking, standing, and performing daily activities without falling. Age-related decline,
medications, and neurological disorders can impair balance, increasing fall risk.

\medterm{Balance Assessment} An evaluation of steadiness and the ability to maintain safe posture during standing and movement. It may include standardized
tests and helps identify mobility or fall risk.

\medterm{Balance Disorders And Vertigo} Balance disorders encompass conditions that cause dizziness, unsteadiness, or a
sensation of motion, often arising from dysfunction in the inner ear, brain, or sensory
pathways.

\medterm{Balance Impairment} The ability to maintain the body's center of mass over its base of support, relying on
input from the vestibular system, vision, and proprioception. See balance.

\medterm{Balance Problems} Difficulty maintaining body stability while standing or moving, often caused by disorders of the inner ear, nervous system, vision, or muscles.

\medterm{Balance Training} Exercises improving postural stability and preventing falls. Components: static balance,
dynamic balance, reactive balance. Techniques: weight shifting, tandem stance,
single-leg stance, perturbations, balance boards. Essential for elderly, neurological
conditions.

\medterm{Balanced Study} A clinical study design in which participant characteristics are evenly distributed between comparison groups at baseline, either by design or by randomization, to reduce confounding and allow fair comparison of interventions.

\medterm{Balanitis} Inflammation of the glans penis, often involving the prepuce (balanoposthitis). Causes
include poor hygiene, irritants, infection, and dermatologic conditions such as lichen
