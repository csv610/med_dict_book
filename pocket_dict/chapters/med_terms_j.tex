\medterm{JAK-STAT Pathway} A conserved, rapid intracellular signal transduction cascade that directly couples the binding of extracellular polypeptide cytokines, interferons, interleukins, and colony-stimulating factors to transmembrane receptors with downstream nuclear gene transcription, bypassing second messengers. The pathway comprises three fundamental components: (1) Cell surface receptors lacking intrinsic enzymatic kinase activity (such as cytokine receptor families for erythropoietin, thrombopoietin, growth hormone, prolactin, interferons, and interleukins); (2) Janus Kinases (JAKs: JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2), receptor-associated cytoplasmic non-receptor tyrosine kinases; and (3) Signal Transducers and Activators of Transcription (STATs: STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, STAT4, STAT5a, STAT5b, and STAT6), latent cytoplasmic transcription factors containing SH2 (Src homology 2) domains. JAKs subsequently phosphorylate STAT monomers on a conserved C-terminal tyrosine residue, driving STAT dissociation from the receptor and spontaneous SH2-mediated homo- or heterodimerization. Activated STAT dimers translocate rapidly into the cell nucleus, where they bind specific DNA promoter sequences to regulate transcriptional programs governing hematopoiesis, cellular proliferation, survival, and immune differentiation. Regulated by negative feedback proteins including Suppressors of Cytokine Signaling (SOCS) and Protein Inhibitors of Activated STAT (PIAS). Dysregulation, constitutive hyperactivation, or somatic activating mutations in JAK-STAT signaling are implicated in myeloproliferative neoplasms (notably the \textit{JAK2} V617F mutation), autoimmune diseases, and immunodeficiencies. Pharmacologic inhibitors (JAK inhibitors, "jakinibs"; such as ruxolitinib, tofacitinib, baricitinib, and upadacitinib) are therapeutic mainstays in rheumatology, dermatology, and hematology.

\medterm{J Receptor} A term for pulmonary vagal C-fiber sensory endings located near

\medterm{Jugular} Relating to the neck, especially the jugular veins that carry blood from the head and neck back to the heart.

\medterm{Jugular Bulb} A widened area at the upper end of the internal jugular vein, just below the skull. It lies close to the middle ear and can vary in size and position.

\medterm{Jugular Foramen} An opening at the base of the skull through which a major neck vein and several nerves pass. Disease in this area can cause difficulty swallowing, hoarseness, shoulder weakness, hearing symptoms, or other neurologic problems.

\medterm{Jugular Foramen Syndrome} A group of problems caused by damage to nerves passing through an opening at the base of the skull. It can cause difficulty swallowing, hoarseness, and weakness when turning the head or lifting the shoulder.
\synonyms
Vernet Syndrome; Posterior Lacerated Foramen Syndrome

\medterm{Jugular Vein} A major vein that carries blood from the head, face, and neck back to the heart. Each side has an internal jugular vein and an external jugular vein.

\medterm{Jugular Veins} Alternate name; see \textit{Jugular Vein}.

\medterm{Jugular Venous Distention} Visible fullness or raised pulsation of the neck veins, usually reflecting increased pressure in the veins returning blood to the heart. It can occur in conditions that raise pressure on the right side of the heart.

\synonyms
Jugular Venous Distension; JVD

\medterm{Jugular Venous Pressure} A vital clinical bedside parameter that provides an indirect yet reliable assessment of right atrial hemodynamics and central venous pressure (CVP). Examined clinically by inspecting the internal jugular vein (IJV)---which lacks valves between the right atrium and internal jugular bulb, acting as a direct manometer---with the patient reclined at a $45^\circ$ angle with relaxed sternocleidomastoid muscles and slight head rotation to the left. The JVP is measured as the vertical height in centimeters of the top of the internal jugular venous column above the sternal angle of Louis (which lies approximately $5\,\text{cm}$ above the mid-right atrium across all recumbent positions); normal JVP is $\le 3\text{--}4\,\text{cm}$ above the sternal angle (corresponding to $\le 8\text{--}9\,\text{cm}\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$ or $\approx 6\,\text{mmHg}$). Pathologically elevated JVP signifies elevated right ventricular filling pressures and fluid overload, seen in right- or left-sided heart failure, constrictive pericarditis, cardiac tamponade, tricuspid regurgitation/stenosis, pulmonary hypertension, and superior vena cava obstruction. The normal jugular venous waveform exhibits three distinct positive peaks and two descents: the "$a$" wave (right atrial contraction; prominent in pulmonary hypertension and tricuspid stenosis, absent in atrial fibrillation, giant "cannon $a$ waves" in complete heart block); "$c$" wave (tricuspid valve bulging during right ventricular isovolumetric contraction); "$x$" descent (atrial relaxation and downward displacement of tricuspid valve during ventricular systole); "$v$" wave (passive atrial venous filling against a closed tricuspid valve; giant systolic "$c\text{--}v$" fusion waves in tricuspid regurgitation); and "$y$" descent (rapid atrial emptying upon tricuspid valve opening; rapid steep drop in constrictive pericarditis [Friedreich sign], blunted or absent in cardiac tamponade). Inability of the JVP to fall with inspiration is Kussmaul sign (indicative of impaired right ventricular diastolic filling).

\synonyms
JVP; Jugular Venous Pulse

\medterm{Jugular Venous Waveform} The pattern of rises and falls seen in the neck vein during a heartbeat. It reflects pressure changes as the right side of the heart contracts and fills.

\synonyms

\medterm{Jugulodigastric Node} A lymph node in the upper neck near the angle of the jaw. It receives drainage from the tonsils and nearby parts of the throat and may enlarge during throat infections.

\medterm{Junctional Bradycardia} A slow heart rhythm that starts near the connection between the heart's upper and lower chambers. The heartbeat is often below 40 beats per minute, and the usual electrical signs from the upper chambers may be absent or reversed.

\medterm{Junctional Nevus} A usually benign, often flat mole formed by pigment cells at the junction between the outer and deeper layers of skin. It may change in size, shape, color, or symptoms.

\medterm{Junctional Premature Complex} An early extra heartbeat that starts near the connection between the heart's upper and lower chambers. It may occur before the next expected beat and can be followed by a brief pause.

\synonyms

\medterm{Junctional Rhythm} A heart rhythm that starts near the connection between the upper and lower chambers instead of the heart’s usual pacemaker. It may be slow or regular and can occur when the usual pacemaker is suppressed or blocked.

\medterm{Junctional Tachycardia} A fast heart rhythm that starts near the connection between the upper and lower chambers. It may occur with heart disease, certain medicines, or after heart surgery; the electrical pattern helps distinguish it from other fast rhythms.

\medterm{Jungian Analysis} Jungian analysis is a psychotherapy approach based on Carl Jung’s theories, using exploration of unconscious processes, symbols, dreams, and personality development.

\medterm{Juvenile Astrocytoma} An astrocytoma, a tumor arising from star-shaped support cells called astrocytes, in a child’s or adolescent’s brain or spinal cord. \medterm{Juvenile Dermatomyositis} An autoimmune illness in children that weakens muscles, especially around the hips and shoulders, and causes a purple rash around the eyes or raised patches over the knuckles. It may also affect swallowing, breathing, or other organs.

\medterm{Juvenile Diabetes} An outdated, imprecise name historically used for type 1 diabetes.

\medterm{Juvenile Granulosa Cell Tumor} A rare ovarian tumor arising from hormone-producing granulosa cells, usually in children or young adults. It may produce estrogen, causing early puberty in children or irregular bleeding in adults. Some tumors are cancerous.

\medterm{Juvenile Hypermobile Spectrum Disorder} A nonstandard descriptive label for
symptomatic joint hypermobility in a child or adolescent. Current pediatric classification

hypermobility spectrum disorder according to musculoskeletal complications, skin and tissue
features, and associated comorbidities. Adult hEDS criteria are used after biological maturity.

\medterm{Juvenile Hypermobility Syndrome} An older term for joint hypermobility with symptoms in a child or adolescent, such as pain, instability, or repeated sprains. The current pediatric framework uses pediatric generalized joint hypermobility (pGJH) and pediatric generalized hypermobility spectrum disorder (pgHSD); classification considers joint mobility, musculoskeletal complications, skin and tissue features, and related health conditions. The classification may change as a child grows and develops.

\medterm{Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis} An umbrella term for a clinically heterogeneous group of chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory arthritides of unknown etiology occurring in children under 16 years of age, characterized by objective persistent arthritis lasting for at least 6 weeks with exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and other defined connective tissue disorders. Classified according to the International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) into seven subtypes: Oligoarthritis (the most common subtype [$\approx 50\%$], involving $\le 4$ joints typically in large lower extremity joints such as knees and ankles; strongly associated with antinuclear antibodies [ANA] and a high risk of asymptomatic, sight-threatening chronic anterior uveitis requiring routine ophthalmologic slit-lamp surveillance); Polyarthritis rheumatoid factor-negative and Polyarthritis RF-positive (involving $\ge 5$ joints; RF-positive subtype mimics adult rheumatoid arthritis with erosive symmetric joint damage and HLA-DR4 association); Systemic JIA (formerly Still disease, characterized by quotidian spiking fevers, an evanescent salmon-pink macular rash, generalized lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, serositis, leukocytosis, and marked ferritin elevation, driven primarily by IL-1 and IL-6 cascades and complicated by life-threatening macrophage activation syndrome [MAS]); Enthesitis-related arthritis (predominant in boys $>6$ years, associated with HLA-B27, involving lower extremity enthesitis, sacroiliitis, and acute anterior uveitis); Psoriatic JIA (dactylitis, nail pitting, onycholysis, and psoriasis); and Undifferentiated arthritis. First-line therapy includes intra-articular corticosteroid injections and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), escalating to conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs, primarily subcutaneous methotrexate) and biologic agents tailored to subtype (anti-TNF agents [etanercept, adalimumab] for polyarthritis/oligoarthritis; anti-IL-1 [anakinra, canakinumab] and anti-IL-6 [tocilizumab] for systemic JIA).

\medterm{Juvenile Xanthogranuloma} A rare, usually harmless growth of immune cells in the skin of an infant or young child, appearing as a firm red, yellow, or brown bump. It often fades without treatment, but rarely affects the eye or internal organs.

\medterm{Juxta-Articular} Located next to a joint. The term may describe bone, soft tissue, swelling, a cyst, or a deposit beside a joint rather than within the joint itself.

\medterm{Juxtaglomerular Apparatus} A specialized, microscopic endocrine and neurovascular regulatory complex located within each nephron at the vascular pole of the renal corpuscle, situated immediately adjacent to the afferent arteriole, efferent arteriole, and the specialized thick ascending limb/early distal convoluted tubule. Anatomically composed of three distinct cellular components: (1) Juxtaglomerular (JG) cells (also called granular cells), modified smooth muscle cells located predominantly in the tunica media of the afferent arteriole that synthesize, store in secretory granules, and exocytose the aspartyl protease enzyme renin; (2) Macula Densa cells, specialized, crowded, columnar sensory epithelial cells located in the wall of the distal tubule that act as chemoreceptors sensing luminal sodium and chloride concentrations ($\text{NaCl}$) and tubular flow rate via apical $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+/2\text{Cl}^-$ (NKCC2) cotransporters; and (3) Extraglomerular Mesangial cells (Lacis cells or Goormaghtigh cells), specialized stromal cells interconnected by extensive gap junctions that modulate tubuloglomerular feedback signaling. The JGA governs systemic arterial blood pressure, extracellular fluid volume, and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) through two primary physiological mechanisms: Renin secretion (stimulated by decreased renal perfusion pressure sensed by renal afferent arteriolar baroreceptors, reduced $\text{NaCl}$ delivery to the macula densa, or increased renal sympathetic nerve activity via $\beta_1$-adrenergic receptors), which initiates the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS); and Tubuloglomerular Feedback (TGF), an autoregulatory mechanism wherein elevated tubular delivery of $\text{NaCl}$ triggers macula densa ATP and adenosine release, inducing afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction to protect the glomerulus from hyperfiltration and maintain constant GFR across fluctuating systemic arterial pressures.

\medterm{Juxtaglomerular Cells} Specialized cells in the kidney that release renin when blood flow or salt delivery to the kidney is low. Renin helps regulate blood pressure and the body's salt and fluid balance.

\synonyms
JG Cells; Granular Cells

\synonyms
