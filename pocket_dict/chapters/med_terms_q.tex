\medterm{Q Angle} The quadriceps angle formed by the intersection of a line drawn from the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) to the central patella and a line from the central patella to the tibial tubercle (normally 10 to 14 degrees in males, 15 to 17 degrees in females); an increased Q angle accentuates lateral patellar displacement and predisposes to patellofemoral pain syndrome.
\synonyms
Quadriceps Angle; Patellofemoral Tracking Angle

\medterm{Q-Angle Measurement} An orthopedic clinical measurement of the lateral pull of the quadriceps femoris muscle relative to the patellofemoral joint, defined as the angle formed between a line drawn from the anterior superior iliac spine to the center of the patella and a second line extending from the patellar center to the tibial tuberosity. An abnormally elevated Q-angle (greater than 20 degrees) predisposes to patellofemoral pain syndrome, lateral patellar subluxation, and chondromalacia patellae.

\synonyms
Quadriceps Angle; Patellar Q-Angle

\medterm{Q Arm Of A Chromosome} The longer arm of a chromosome, identified by the letter q. A genetic report may use this label to show where a gene change or missing, extra, or rearranged DNA segment is located.

\medterm{Q-Banding} A laboratory staining method that creates bright and dim bands on chromosomes when viewed under ultraviolet light. The pattern helps show chromosome structure and some rearrangements.

\medterm{Q Fever} An infection caused by the bacterium Coxiella burnetii, usually caught by breathing dust contaminated by infected cattle, sheep, goats, or their birth products. It often causes sudden fever, severe headache, tiredness, and muscle aches; some people develop pneumonia, hepatitis, or long-term heart-valve infection.

\medterm{QRS Axis} The general direction of the heart’s electrical signal as the lower chambers contract, measured on an electrocardiogram. A shifted direction can occur with changes in the heart muscle, conduction system, or strain on the heart.

\medterm{QRS Complex} The group of waves on an electrocardiogram showing the electrical signal that makes the heart’s lower chambers contract. An unusually wide or shaped complex may indicate a conduction problem, abnormal rhythm, or electrolyte disturbance.

\medterm{QRS Duration} The time taken for the electrical signal to spread through the heart’s lower chambers, measured on an electrocardiogram. A longer-than-usual time can occur with a conduction problem or an abnormal rhythm.

\medterm{Q-Switch Laser} A specialized dermatologic and ophthalmologic laser modality that delivers ultra-short pulses (nanosecond domain) of high peak energy, used for selective photothermolysis of cutaneous pigmented lesions, tattoo pigment clearance, and YAG posterior capsulotomy.
\synonyms
Quality-Switched Laser; Nanosecond Pulsed Laser

\medterm{QTc Dispersion} The difference between the longest and shortest corrected QT intervals on an electrocardiogram. It describes variation in the heart’s recovery time between beats, but its meaning is limited and depends on the full ECG and clinical setting.
\synonyms
Interlead QT Dispersion; Spatial Repolarization Heterogeneity

\medterm{QT Interval} The time on an electrocardiogram representing electrical activation and recovery of the heart's lower chambers. Because heart rate changes this time, clinicians often use a corrected value when assessing rhythm risk.

\medterm{QT Prolongation} An unusually long QT interval on an electrocardiogram. It may result from an inherited condition, a medicine, or abnormal body salts and can increase the risk of a rapid, dangerous rhythm that may cause fainting or cardiac arrest.

\medterm{Quadrant} One of four sections used to describe a location, especially in the abdomen. The four abdominal quadrants help clinicians record where pain, tenderness, a lump, or another finding is located.

\medterm{Abdominal Quadrant} One of four topographical anatomical divisions of the abdomen created by perpendicular vertical and horizontal imaginary planes intersecting at the umbilicus (right upper, left upper, right lower, and left lower quadrants), utilized in clinical triage to localize abdominal pain and organ pathology.

\medterm{Quadrantanopia} Loss of vision in one quarter of the normal visual field, often from damage to the brain pathways carrying sight. The missing area may occur on the same side in both eyes and can affect reading, driving, walking, or safety.

\medterm{Quadrantectomy} Surgery to remove one section of an organ, most often part of the breast containing a tumor plus a margin of healthy tissue. The need for lymph-node surgery or further treatment depends on the tumor and test results.

\medterm{Quadrate Lobe} A small area on the underside of the liver between the gallbladder and the main vessels and ducts entering the liver. It is part of the left functional section of the liver.
\synonyms
Lobus Quadratus Hepatis; Couinaud Segment IVb

\medterm{Quadratus Femoris} A small muscle deep in the buttock that turns the thigh outward and helps draw it toward the body.

\medterm{Quadratus Lumborum} A muscle along the back of the abdomen that bends the body to one side and helps steady the lowest rib during breathing.

\medterm{Quadratus Plantae} A deep muscle in the sole of the foot that helps the long toe-flexing muscle pull the toes straight downward.

\synonyms
Flexor Accessorius

\medterm{Quadriceps} The four muscles on the front of the thigh that extend the knee; the rectus femoris also assists hip flexion. The quadriceps tendon connects them to the patella and contributes to the extensor mechanism.

\medterm{Quadriceps Active Test} A knee examination used to look for injury to the posterior cruciate ligament. The person tightens the thigh muscle while the knee is bent, and forward movement of a previously sagging shinbone may support the finding. The test does not confirm a tear by itself.
\synonyms
Active Quadriceps Drawer Test; PCL Active Contraction Sign

\medterm{Quadriceps Contusion} A blunt injury to the muscles at the front of the thigh, often called a charley horse. It can cause torn muscle fibers, bleeding within the muscle, pain, swelling, and limited knee movement.
\synonyms
Charley Horse; Intramuscular Thigh Hematoma

\medterm{Quadriceps Femoris} The group of four muscles at the front of the thigh. It straightens the knee for standing, walking, climbing, and kicking; one part also helps bend the hip.

\medterm{Quadriceps Femoris Muscle} The four-muscle group at the front of the thigh that straightens the knee. It is essential for standing, walking, climbing, and rising from a chair; injury can cause pain and difficulty extending the leg.

\medterm{Quadriceps Injury} Damage to the quadriceps muscle or tendon caused by sudden force, overuse, or direct trauma. It may cause front-of-thigh pain, bruising, weakness, or difficulty extending the knee. Severe pain, a visible or felt gap, or inability to bear weight may occur with a major tear.

\medterm{Quadriceps Tendinitis} Overuse-related inflammation or degeneration of the quadriceps tendon, causing pain above the kneecap that worsens with jumping, running, or resisted knee extension.

\medterm{Quadriceps Tendon Rupture} Complete traumatic disruption of the confluent tendon of the quadriceps femoris muscle at its insertion into the superior pole of the patella, typically occurring following eccentric loading on a flexed knee in patients over 40 years of age, presenting with a palpable suprapatellar gap and inability to perform an active straight leg raise.
\synonyms
Suprapatellar Tendon Rupture; Extensor Mechanism Disruption

\medterm{Quadriceps Tendon Ruptures} A tear of the tendon connecting the quadriceps muscle to the patella, causing weakness
or inability to extend the knee. Complete tears often require surgical repair; treatment
depends on the extent of injury and the person's functional needs.

\medterm{Quadrigeminal Cistern} A subarachnoid cistern located dorsal to the midbrain tectum (corpora quadrigemina / superior and inferior colliculi), ventral to the splenium of the corpus callosum and tentorium cerebelli, containing the vein of Galen, the basal veins of Rosenthal, and the posterior cerebral arteries.
\synonyms
Cisterna Quadrigeminalis; Cisterna Ambiens Dorsalis

\medterm{Quadrigeminal Plate} The dorsal roof (tectum) of the midbrain bearing four rounded sensory eminences: the paired superior colliculi (visual reflexes and saccadic eye movements) and inferior colliculi (auditory relay processing).

\synonyms
Tectal Plate; Lamina Tecti

\medterm{Quadrilateral Space Syndrome} A neurovascular compression syndrome caused by entrapment of the axillary nerve and posterior circumflex humeral artery within the quadrilateral space (bounded by teres minor, teres major, long head of triceps, and humerus), presenting with posterior shoulder ache, paresthesias over the lateral shoulder, and deltoid weakness.
\synonyms
Quadrangular Space Syndrome; Axillary Nerve Compression Syndrome

\medterm{Quadriparesis} Weakness affecting both arms and both legs. It may result from disease or injury to the neck spinal cord, brain, nerves, or muscles and can impair movement, breathing, and bladder or bowel control.

\medterm{Quadriplegia} Paralysis affecting both arms and both legs, usually with weakness of the trunk, because of damage to the neck portion of the spinal cord or another major nervous-system injury. It can impair breathing and bladder or bowel control.

\medterm{Quadruple Screen Test} A second-trimester blood test that combines four pregnancy-related markers to estimate the chance of selected chromosome conditions and neural-tube defects. It does not diagnose a condition; abnormal results need further assessment.

\medterm{Quadruplet Pregnancy} A pregnancy involving four fetuses. It has increased risks of preterm birth, restricted fetal growth, high blood pressure, metabolic problems, and illness in the pregnant person.

\medterm{Quad Screen} A second-trimester blood screening test that estimates the chance of certain chromosome conditions and neural-tube defects in the fetus. It estimates risk rather than making a diagnosis; an abnormal result may require ultrasound or diagnostic testing.

\synonyms
Quadruple Marker Test; AFP-Plus; Second-Trimester Screen

\medterm{Qualitative HCG Blood Test} A diagnostic serological laboratory test that detects the presence or absence of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) in maternal blood. Primarily utilized for early confirmation of pregnancy.

\synonyms
Qualitative Serum Pregnancy Test; Blood hCG Screen; Qualitative Beta-hCG

\medterm{Quality-Adjusted Life Year} A standardized composite health economic metric that combines both the quantity (length) and quality (health-related utility weight from 0 for death to 1 for perfect health) of life generated by a medical intervention, widely used in cost-effectiveness analysis and health technology assessment.

\synonyms
QALY

\medterm{Quality Of Life} A person’s experience of physical health, emotional well-being, relationships, independence, and ability to do meaningful activities. Illness and treatment may affect these areas differently for each person.

\medterm{Quantitative Bence-Jones Protein Test} A urine test measuring abnormal light-chain proteins made by some plasma-cell cancers. It can help diagnose or monitor disorders such as multiple myeloma, but results are interpreted with blood tests and other findings.

\medterm{Quantitative Computed Tomography} A CT scan that measures bone density while showing bone structure. It can help assess osteoporosis, unusual bone loss, or fracture risk when standard bone-density testing is unsuitable or more detail is needed.

\medterm{Quantitative HCG Blood Test} A blood test that measures the amount of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG). It can help assess early pregnancy, possible ectopic pregnancy or miscarriage, and some pregnancy-related or germ-cell tumors.

\synonyms
Quantitative Beta-hCG; Serial hCG Test; Serum hCG Level

\medterm{Quantitative Immunoglobulins} A blood test measuring the main classes of antibodies. Low levels may suggest an immune deficiency, while high or uneven levels may occur with infection, inflammation, liver disease, or some blood cancers; results need clinical interpretation.

\medterm{Quantitative Nephelometry Test} A laboratory test that estimates the amount of selected proteins by measuring how much light they scatter. It can measure antibodies, complement proteins, and other proteins involved in immune, kidney, or blood disorders.

\medterm{Quantitative PET} Analysis of a PET scan that estimates how much radioactive tracer is taken up by tissue. Standardized measurements can help compare disease activity or treatment response, but results are affected by the tracer, timing, blood sugar, and scanning method.

\medterm{Quantitative Sensory Testing} A test that measures how strongly a person senses vibration, temperature, or pain. It can help assess nerve damage affecting small or large nerve fibers.
\synonyms
QST; Psychophysical Sensory Threshold Assay

\medterm{Quarantine} Temporary separation or movement restrictions for people exposed to an infectious disease while monitoring whether they become ill. It differs from isolation, which separates people who are already infected.

\medterm{Quartan Fever} A fever pattern in which attacks recur about every 72 hours, classically seen with the malaria parasite Plasmodium malariae. Fever timing alone cannot diagnose malaria; testing is needed in anyone with possible exposure and fever.

\medterm{Queckenstedt Test} A classical diagnostic maneuver performed during lumbar puncture; bilateral manual compression of the internal jugular veins causes a prompt rise in CSF pressure on spinal manometry in normal individuals; failure of CSF pressure to rise indicates a complete mechanical blockage in the spinal subarachnoid space.
\synonyms
Queckenstedt's Sign; Jugular Venous Spinal Manometry Test

\medterm{Queensland Tick Typhus} An Australian spotted fever group rickettsial infection caused by the obligate intracellular bacterium \textit{Rickettsia australis}, transmitted to humans by the bite of the paralysis tick \textit{Ixodes holocyclus}. Clinically characterized by fever, headache, regional lymphadenopathy, a localized necrotic inoculation eschar at the tick bite site, and a maculopapular or vesicular rash; responds rapidly to doxycycline.

\synonyms
QTT; Australian Tick Typhus

\medterm{Quellung Reaction} A classic microbiological microscopic diagnostic reaction in which specific polyvalent or monovalent anticapsular antibodies bind to the polysaccharide capsule of encapsulated bacteria (such as \textit{Streptococcus pneumoniae}, \textit{Neisseria meningitidis}, or \textit{Haemophilus influenzae}), increasing its refractive index and causing it to appear sharply demarcated and swollen.

\synonyms
Neufeld Quellung Phenomenon; Capsular Swelling Test

\medterm{Quetiapine Fumarate} A form of quetiapine, a medicine used for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and selected depression treatment. It can cause sleepiness, dizziness on standing, weight or blood-sugar changes, and movement problems.

\medterm{Quickening} The first time a pregnant person feels fetal movement, often as a flutter or tapping sensation during the second trimester. Timing varies with the pregnancy, body shape, and position of the placenta.

\medterm{Quick Relievers} Fast-acting asthma medicines used when symptoms suddenly worsen. They quickly relax tightened airway muscles and can ease wheezing, coughing, chest tightness, and breathlessness, but they do not replace regular treatment that controls airway inflammation.

\synonyms
Rescue Inhalers; Fast-Acting Bronchodilators

\medterm{Quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment} A bedside score used with suspected infection to identify people at increased risk of serious illness. It considers changes in alertness, fast breathing, and low blood pressure. It supports, but does not replace, a full assessment for sepsis.

\synonyms
qSOFA; Quick SOFA Score

\medterm{Quick Test} The historical and foundational one-stage prothrombin time (PT) coagulation test introduced by Armand Quick in 1935, which measures the extrinsic and common pathways of coagulation (factors II, V, VII, X, and fibrinogen) following addition of tissue thromboplastin and calcium.
\synonyms
Quick Prothrombin Time; One-Stage Prothrombin Assay

\medterm{Cellular Quiescence} A reversible, non-dividing physiological state in which a cell exits the active cell division cycle and enters the G0 phase in response to anti-mitogenic signals or nutrient deprivation, while retaining the capacity to re-enter the proliferative cycle upon appropriate growth factor stimulation.

\medterm{Quinalphos} A poisonous insecticide that disrupts nerve-signal control. Significant exposure can cause sweating, excess saliva, vomiting, muscle twitching, weakness, seizures, breathing failure, or death.

\medterm{Quinapril} Quinapril is an angiotensin-converting-enzyme inhibitor used for hypertension and heart failure; cough, hyperkalaemia, renal-function changes, angioedema, and fetal toxicity are important risks.

\medterm{Quincke Edema} An acute, episodic, well-demarcated swelling of the deep dermis, subcutaneous tissues, or submucosal tissues (angioedema), classically involving the lips, eyelids, tongue, larynx, or gastrointestinal tract, mediated by histamine release or excessive bradykinin generation.
\synonyms
Angioneurotic Edema; Acute Angioedema

\medterm{Quincke Needle} A traditional spinal needle featuring a sharp, medium-length cutting bevel with a matching stylet, utilized for diagnostic lumbar puncture and spinal anesthesia, historically associated with a higher incidence of post-dural puncture headache compared to atraumatic pencil-point needles.

\medterm{Quincke Pulse} A physical clinical sign of severe aortic regurgitation characterized by visible, rhythmic, alternating subungual capillary flushing and blanching occurring synchronously with the cardiac cycle when gentle pressure is applied to the distal edge of a fingernail.
\synonyms
Quincke's Sign; Subungual Capillary Pulsation

\medterm{Quincke Pulse Sign} An eponymous physical cardiovascular sign characterized by alternating subungual erythema and blanching of the nail bed capillary plexus, synchronized with ventricular systole. Elicited by applying gentle compressive pressure to the tip of a fingernail; it represents a manifestation of the hyperdynamic wide pulse pressure and rapid runoff characteristic of severe chronic aortic regurgitation.

\synonyms
Quincke Capillary Pulsation; Quincke Sign

\medterm{Quincke's Disease} Sudden swelling of deeper layers of the skin or mucosa, commonly affecting the lips, face, tongue, throat, or bowel; airway involvement is an emergency.

\synonyms
Angioedema; Giant Urticaria

\medterm{Quincke's Edema} An older term for angioedema, sudden swelling in the deeper layers of the skin or mucous membranes. It commonly affects the lips, face, tongue, or throat; throat swelling or breathing difficulty is an emergency.

\medterm{Quincke's Pulse} Visible flushing and paling of the nail bed or skin with each heartbeat. It may occur when the difference between the upper and lower blood-pressure numbers is unusually wide, especially with severe aortic-valve leakage, but it is not diagnostic alone.

\medterm{Quincke's Sign} Alternating redness and paling of the nail bed or skin with each heartbeat, usually caused by a wide pulse pressure. It can be seen in severe aortic-valve leakage but is not diagnostic by itself.

\medterm{Quinidine} A heart-rhythm medicine that changes electrical conduction in the heart. It is used selectively because it can cause dangerous abnormal rhythms, low blood pressure, low platelets, diarrhea, or other serious side effects.

\medterm{Quinidine Syncope} Sudden syncope or cardiac arrest occurring in patients treated with quinidine or related class Ia antiarrhythmic agents, caused by marked prolongation of the QT interval triggering self-terminating or degenerating episodes of polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (torsades de pointes).
\synonyms
Antiarrhythmic Drug-Induced Syncope; Quinidine Torsades Syncope

\medterm{Quinine} A medicine derived from cinchona bark that can treat selected malaria infections. It is used less often because safer options are available and it can cause low blood sugar, ringing in the ears, blood-cell problems, or abnormal heart rhythms.

\medterm{Quinine Amblyopia} Acute toxic retinal and optic nerve damage caused by quinine toxicity (cinchonism), presenting with sudden bilateral visual acuity loss, marked concentric visual field constriction, retinal arteriolar attenuation, and optic disc pallor.
\synonyms
Quinine Retinopathy; Cinchona Retinal Toxicity

\medterm{Quinolone} A class of antibiotics that stop bacteria from copying and repairing their DNA. Some treat serious infections, but side effects can include tendon injury, nerve symptoms, abnormal heart rhythms, or severe diarrhea, so use depends on the infection and risks.

\medterm{Quinolone Antibiotics} A major synthetic bactericidal antibiotic class (fluoroquinolones, such as ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and moxifloxacin) that selectively inhibits bacterial type II topoisomerases (DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV), thereby arresting bacterial DNA replication and transcription.

\synonyms
Fluoroquinolones

\medterm{Quinolone-Induced Tendinopathy} An adverse musculoskeletal drug reaction associated with fluoroquinolone antimicrobial therapy (such as ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin), characterized by painful tendinitis and an elevated risk of tendon rupture (predominantly the Achilles tendon), especially in patients receiving concurrent corticosteroids or with renal impairment.
\synonyms
Fluoroquinolone Tendonitis; Fluoroquinolone-Associated Achilles Rupture

\medterm{Quinsy} An abscess beside a tonsil, usually after a throat infection. It can cause severe one-sided throat pain, fever, muffled speech, drooling, difficulty opening the mouth, or breathing difficulty. Treatment usually includes drainage and antibiotics.

\medterm{Quinsy Peritonsillar Abscess} A suppurative localized collection of purulent exudate in the potential space between the palatine tonsil capsule and the superior pharyngeal constrictor muscle, typically arising as a complication of acute bacterial tonsillopharyngitis (frequently involving \textit{Streptococcus pyogenes} and oral anaerobes). Characterized by severe unilateral sore throat, odynophagia, \"hot potato\" muffled voice, trismus, and medial displacement of the tonsil with contralateral uvular deviation.

\synonyms
Quinsy; Peritonsillar Abscess; PTA

\medterm{Quinupristin-Dalfopristin} An intravenous antibiotic combination used rarely for selected serious infections caused by resistant gram-positive bacteria. It can cause muscle or joint pain, liver problems, infusion reactions, and medicine interactions.

\medterm{Quit Smoking} Alternate name; see \textit{Smoking Cessation}.

\medterm{Quorum Sensing} A density-dependent bacterial intercellular communication mechanism mediated by the release and detection of small diffusible autoinducer signaling molecules, coordinating group behaviors such as biofilm formation, bioluminescence, and virulence factor expression once a threshold bacterial population density is achieved.

\medterm{Quotidian Fever} A fever pattern in which fever rises or attacks occur about every 24 hours. It can occur with malaria and other infections, but the timing of fever alone does not identify the cause.

\medterm{Quotidian Malaria} A recurring pattern of malarial paroxysms characterized by daily febrile spikes occurring at 24-hour intervals, classic for infection caused by Plasmodium knowlesi or resulting from overlapping broods of Plasmodium vivax or Plasmodium falciparum.
\synonyms
24-Hour Malarial Paroxysm; Daily Malarial Fever

\medterm{Q Wave} A downward movement that may begin the electrical pattern of a heartbeat on an electrocardiogram. It can be normal in some recording positions or suggest previous heart-muscle injury, depending on its size and location.

\medterm{Q Waves} Early downward waves on an electrocardiogram. Some patterns may suggest a previous heart attack or another heart problem, but Q waves must be interpreted with the other ECG findings and the person's history.
\synonyms
Pathologic Q Wave; Myocardial Infarction Q Wave
