\medterm{Xanthelasma} A harmless yellowish patch or bump caused by fat deposits in the skin of the eyelids, usually near the inner corners of the eyes. It can occur with high blood fats but may also occur when blood-fat levels are normal.

\synonyms
Xanthelasma Palpebrarum; Eyelid Xanthoma; Palpebral Xanthoma

\medterm{Xanthine} A purine breakdown product and an intermediate in uric-acid production. The enzyme xanthine oxidoreductase converts it to uric acid; when this step is impaired, xanthine can build up and form urinary stones.

\medterm{Xanthine Oxidase} An enzyme in purine breakdown that converts hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to uric acid. Medicines that block it lower uric-acid production and are used to treat gout and some cases of high uric acid.

\medterm{Xanthine Oxidase Inhibitors} Medicines such as allopurinol and febuxostat that lower uric-acid production by blocking xanthine oxidase. They are used to lower uric acid in gout and in some cases of high uric acid associated with cancer treatment.

\synonyms
XOIs

\medterm{Xanthinuria} A rare inherited disorder in which reduced xanthine oxidoreductase activity impairs the conversion of hypoxanthine and xanthine to uric acid. Uric-acid levels in blood and urine are very low; excess xanthine can form kidney or urinary stones, though some people have no symptoms.

\medterm{Xanthochromia} A pink, yellow, or amber discoloration of the fluid surrounding the brain and spinal cord (cerebrospinal fluid) observed after centrifugation. It occurs when red blood cells that have leaked into the spinal fluid break down over several hours into yellow-tinted pigments, such as bilirubin. Its presence is a crucial diagnostic indicator that helps doctors confirm a subarachnoid hemorrhage (bleeding around the brain) and distinguish it from accidental minor bleeding caused by the spinal tap needle itself.

\synonyms
CSF Xanthochromia; Cerebrospinal Fluid Xanthochromia

\medterm{Xanthogranulomatous} Describing inflammation in which tissue contains many immune cells filled with fat. This pattern can occur in long-lasting infections and other inflammatory conditions.

\medterm{Xanthogranulomatous Cholecystitis} A rare, long-lasting inflammation of the gallbladder that can thicken and damage its wall. It may spread to nearby tissues and can look like gallbladder cancer on scans.

\synonyms
XGC

\medterm{Xanthogranulomatous Oophoritis} A rare, chronic inflammation of an ovary that can form a mass resembling ovarian cancer on imaging. Reported associations include infection and endometriosis; symptoms may include pelvic pain, fever, or abnormal bleeding.

\medterm{Xanthogranulomatous Pyelonephritis} A chronic, destructive inflammation of the kidney, usually associated with urinary blockage, stones, and infection. It replaces normal kidney tissue with inflamed tissue and may form a mass that resembles a tumor.

\medterm{Xanthoma} A skin condition characterized by the formation of yellowish, fatty growths or nodules beneath the skin, caused by the localized accumulation of lipid-laden immune cells (macrophages). Xanthomas often develop when blood levels of cholesterol or triglycerides are abnormally high, which can occur from inherited lipid disorders, poorly controlled diabetes, or liver disease. They commonly appear on or around the eyelids (xanthelasma), over the elbows, knees, buttocks, or along tendons such as the Achilles tendon.

\synonyms
Cutaneous Xanthoma; Lipid Granuloma

\medterm{Xanthoma Disseminatum} A rare disorder in which many yellow, red-brown, or orange bumps develop on the skin, especially in body folds. The mouth, throat, or other organs may also be affected, and some people develop problems controlling water balance.

\synonyms
Montgomery Syndrome; Disseminate Xanthoma

\medterm{Xanthoma Eruptivum} A sudden outbreak of many small yellow-orange bumps, usually on the buttocks, arms, legs, or shoulders. It is often linked to very high blood triglyceride levels.

\synonyms
Eruptive Xanthoma; Eruptive Xanthomatosis

\medterm{Xanthoma Planum} Flat or slightly raised yellow patches caused by lipid-filled immune cells in the skin. They may occur in skin folds or over wider areas and can be associated with abnormal blood lipids or some blood disorders. Palmar crease xanthomas are a distinct pattern.

\synonyms
Plane Xanthoma

\medterm{Xanthomas} See \medref{Xanthoma}.
\medterm{Xanthoma Striatum Palmare} Yellow or orange lines or patches in the creases of the palms caused by fat deposits in the skin. They are strongly associated with inherited blood-fat disorders, especially type III dysbetalipoproteinemia.

\synonyms
Palmar Crease Xanthoma; Planar Palmar Xanthoma

\medterm{Xanthoma Tendinosum} Firm, usually painless fat deposits in tendons or ligaments, most often in the Achilles tendon or tendons of the hands. They are strongly linked to inherited high cholesterol.

\synonyms
Tendon Xanthoma; Tendinous Xanthoma

\medterm{Xanthomatosis} A disorder in which fatty deposits called xanthomas develop in the skin, tendons, or organs. It may be linked to inherited or acquired problems with blood fats.

\medterm{Xanthoma Tuberosum} Slowly enlarging, painless, firm, yellowish-red nodular lipid deposits occurring over extensor pressure areas, particularly the elbows, knees, heels, and buttocks. They are strongly associated with familial hypercholesterolemia and type III hyperlipoproteinemia.

\synonyms
Tuberous Xanthoma; Tuberous Xanthomatosis

\medterm{Xanthopsia} A visual disturbance in which objects appear yellow or yellow-tinted. It can occur with retinal or optic-nerve disorders and with toxicity from some medicines, including digoxin.

\medterm{Xanthosis} Yellow discoloration of skin or other tissues caused by carotene or another yellow substance. Unlike jaundice, it does not usually make the whites of the eyes yellow; causes include dietary pigments and some systemic conditions.

\medterm{X Chromosome} One of the two human sex chromosomes. It carries genes involved in sex development and many other body functions. Most people have one or two X chromosomes, although chromosome patterns vary.

\medterm{Xenobiotic} A chemical foreign to an organism, such as a medicine, pollutant, pesticide, or industrial compound. The body may change or remove it; the original chemical or its products can have biological effects.

\medterm{Xenobiotic Metabolism} The body’s chemical processing of foreign substances, including medicines, pollutants, and toxins. Enzymes, especially in the liver, may make them easier to eliminate, alter their activity, or produce harmful products.

\medterm{Xenobiotic Resistance} The ability of a cell or organism to withstand a foreign chemical, medicine, or toxin. It may result from faster breakdown, removal from cells, or changes in the substance’s target.

\synonyms
Xenobiotic Tolerance

\medterm{Xenograft} Tissue, cells, or a biological material transplanted between different species, such as animal tissue used in a human. Rejection and infection are important risks, although some materials are used temporarily as tissue coverings or substitutes.

\medterm{Xenon Anesthesia} General anesthesia produced by inhaled xenon, a noble gas that acts on brain signaling. The gas is eliminated mainly through the lungs; its clinical use is limited by cost and availability.

\medterm{Xenotransplantation} Transplantation of living cells, tissues, or organs from one species to another, usually from a nonhuman animal into a person. The recipient’s immune system may reject the graft, and animal infections may be transmitted. In regulatory definitions, the term also includes transplanting, implanting, or infusing human cells, tissues, or body fluids that contacted living nonhuman animal material outside the body.

\medterm{Xerochilia} Persistent dryness and scaling of the lips, sometimes with superficial cracks. Causes include dry weather, repeated lip licking, mouth breathing, and medicines such as oral retinoids.

\synonyms
Dry Lips

\medterm{Xeroderma} Alternate name; see \medref{Dry Skin}.

\medterm{Xeroderma Pigmentosum} A rare inherited genetic condition in which the body's cells cannot repair the DNA damage caused by ultraviolet (UV) light, such as sunlight. Even minimal sun exposure causes severe sunburns, blistering, and heavy freckling at an early age. Because damaged DNA accumulates without repair, individuals have an extremely high risk of developing multiple skin cancers, severe eye damage, and, in some cases, progressive neurological complications.

\synonyms
XP; DeSanctis-Cacchione Syndrome (in neurodegenerative subtypes)

\medterm{Xeroderma Pigmentosum Complementation Groups} Genetic subtypes of xeroderma pigmentosum, each associated with a different gene involved in nucleotide-excision repair or DNA damage tolerance. The subtype helps explain the molecular cause; all types increase sensitivity to ultraviolet radiation and the risk of skin cancer.

\synonyms
XP Complementation Groups; NER Defect Groups

\medterm{Xerophthalmia} The spectrum of eye disease caused by vitamin A deficiency, ranging
from night blindness and dryness of the conjunctiva or cornea to corneal ulceration and
keratomalacia, the softening and destruction of corneal tissue. Advanced disease can scar
the cornea and cause permanent vision loss.
\synonyms
Vitamin A Deficiency Eye Disease

\medterm{Xerophthalmic Ulceration} Corneal ulceration caused by severe vitamin A deficiency, sometimes progressing to keratomalacia (softening and destruction of the cornea). In the WHO classification, stage X3A affects less than one-third of the cornea and X3B affects at least one-third; scarring and vision loss can result.

\synonyms
Vitamin A Corneal Ulcer

\medterm{Xerosis} Alternate name; see \medref{Dry Skin}.

\medterm{Xerostomia} Alternate name; see \medref{Dry Mouth}.

\medterm{Xerotic Eczema} An itchy form of eczema caused by very dry skin, with scaling and fine cracks that may form a netlike pattern. It often affects the lower legs and is more common in cold, dry weather and in older adults.

\synonyms
Asteatotic Eczema; Eczema Craquele; Winter Itch

\medterm{X-Inactivation} The process by which most genes on one X chromosome are switched off in each cell with more than one X chromosome. It balances X-linked gene activity; some genes escape inactivation, and the chromosome switched off can differ among cells.

\medterm{Xiphisternal Joint} The joint between the lower part of the breastbone and the xiphoid process. It often becomes partly or fully hardened with age.

\synonyms
Xiphisternal Synchondrosis; Xiphisternal Articulation

\medterm{Xiphoidalgia} Pain or tenderness over the xiphoid process, the small lower part of the breastbone. Pain may spread to the upper abdomen, chest, back, or shoulders and may worsen with touch, bending, lifting, or coughing.

\synonyms
Xiphoidodynia; Hypersensitive Xiphoid Syndrome; Xiphoid Syndrome

\medterm{Xiphoidectomy} Surgery to remove the xiphoid process, the small lower part of the breastbone. It may be done for persistent pain in this area or to provide access during some chest or abdominal operations.

\medterm{Xiphoid Process} The small pointed lower part of the breastbone. Its shape varies between people and it provides attachment for some muscles and ligaments.

\medterm{XLA} Inherited immune deficiency where B-cell precursors fail to mature, leaving the body unable to produce antibodies against infections. See \medref{X-Linked Agammaglobulinemia}.

\medterm{X-Linked} Describing a gene or condition involving a gene on the X chromosome. Its inheritance and effects depend on the gene and a person's sex-chromosome pattern. A father passes his X chromosome to children who inherit his X, not to children who inherit his Y.

\medterm{X-Linked Adrenoleukodystrophy} An inherited disorder in which very-long-chain fatty acids build up and can damage the brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerves, or adrenal glands. Childhood cerebral disease may cause changes in behavior, learning, or movement; adrenal insufficiency and adult-onset nerve or spinal-cord disease also occur.
\synonyms
X-ALD

\medterm{X-linked Adult Spinal Muscular Atrophy} An older name for X-linked spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy (Kennedy disease), an inherited androgen-receptor disorder. It usually begins in adulthood and causes slowly progressive muscle weakness, wasting, and twitching; swallowing difficulty, breast enlargement, and reduced fertility may occur.

\medterm{X-Linked Agammaglobulinemia} A rare inherited immune disorder affecting males in which the body is unable to produce mature B cells, the specialized white blood cells responsible for making antibodies (immunoglobulins). While maternal antibodies protect the baby for the first several months of life, their decline leaves the child vulnerable to recurrent, severe bacterial infections---such as ear infections, pneumonia, sinusitis, and gastrointestinal infections.

\synonyms
XLA; Bruton Agammaglobulinemia; Congenital Agammaglobulinemia

\medterm{X-Linked Chronic Granulomatous Disease} An inherited immune disorder, usually affecting boys, caused by a change in the \textit{CYBB} gene. Certain white blood cells cannot kill some bacteria and fungi normally, leading to repeated serious infections and areas of ongoing inflammation.

\synonyms
X-Linked CGD; CYBB Deficiency; Cytochrome b-245 Beta Deficiency

\medterm{X-Linked Dominant Inheritance} An inheritance pattern in which a variant in one copy of a gene on the X chromosome can cause a condition. An affected father passes the variant to all daughters and no sons; an affected mother has a 50\% chance of passing it to each child.

\medterm{X-Linked Emery-Dreifuss Muscular Dystrophy} An inherited muscle disorder, usually caused by a variant in the \textit{EMD} gene, that causes joint contractures, slowly worsening muscle weakness, and heart-conduction problems.

\synonyms
EDMD1; Emerin Deficiency Muscular Dystrophy

\medterm{X-Linked Hyper-IgM Syndrome} An inherited immune disorder, usually caused by a defect in CD40 ligand on T cells. This impairs B-cell antibody class switching, so IgM is present but other antibody types are low; recurrent infections may include opportunistic infections.

\synonyms
HIGM1; CD40L Deficiency; Hyper-IgM Syndrome Type 1

\medterm{X-Linked Hypophosphatemia} An inherited disorder, usually caused by a variant in the \textit{PHEX} gene, in which the kidneys lose too much phosphate. Low phosphate impairs bone mineralization, causing rickets in children or osteomalacia in adults, with bone pain, leg deformity, dental problems, or reduced height.

\medterm{X-Linked Ichthyosis} An inherited skin disorder caused by deficient steroid sulfatase, usually due to a variant or deletion in the \textit{STS} gene. It mainly affects males and causes widespread, dark, polygonal scales, often from infancy.

\medterm{X-Linked Inheritance} Inheritance of a gene variant located on the X chromosome. The pattern and effects depend on the gene, the variant, and the person's sex-chromosome pattern. A father passes his X chromosome only to children who inherit his X, not to those who inherit his Y.

\medterm{X-linked Intellectual Disability} Intellectual disability caused by a change in a gene on the X chromosome. Features vary widely and may include developmental delay, learning difficulty, behavior differences, seizures, or associated physical findings.

\medterm{X-linked Juvenile Retinoschisis} An older name for X-linked retinoschisis, an inherited retinal disorder that usually begins in childhood.

\medterm{X-Linked Lymphoproliferative Disease Type 1} An inherited immune disorder, usually affecting boys, in which the body can react severely to Epstein--Barr virus. It may cause severe glandular fever, very high inflammation, low antibody levels, or lymphoma.

\synonyms
XLP1; Duncan Disease; SAP Deficiency

\medterm{X-Linked Lymphoproliferative Syndrome} A group of inherited immune disorders in which infection, especially with Epstein--Barr virus, can trigger an abnormal immune response, including severe inflammation, low blood-cell counts, or lymphoma. Type 1 is caused by \textit{SH2D1A} variants; type 2 is caused by \textit{XIAP} variants.

\medterm{X-Linked Myotubular Myopathy} A rare inherited muscle disease, usually affecting boys, that causes severe weakness and low muscle tone from birth. Babies may have drooping eyelids, feeding difficulty, and dangerous weakness of the breathing muscles.

\synonyms
XLMTM; Myotubularin Deficiency

\medterm{X-Linked Recessive Inheritance} A pattern in which a disease-causing variant on the X chromosome usually causes disease in people with one X chromosome. People with two X chromosomes may carry one variant copy and sometimes have symptoms. A father passes his X chromosome only to children who inherit his X, not to those who inherit his Y.

\medterm{X-Linked Retinitis Pigmentosa} An inherited disorder in which the retina slowly loses its ability to sense light. It usually causes night blindness followed by narrowing of the field of vision and worsening sight.

\synonyms
XLRP; X-Linked Pigmentary Retinopathy

\medterm{X-Linked Retinoschisis} An inherited retinal disorder, usually caused by a variant in the \textit{RS1} gene, in which layers of the retina split apart. It usually begins in childhood and causes reduced central vision; bleeding into the eye or retinal detachment can occur.

\medterm{X-Linked Severe Combined Immunodeficiency} A rare, life-threatening inherited genetic disorder of the immune system that occurs almost exclusively in males. A mutation on the X chromosome impairs the development of vital white blood cells---particularly T cells and natural killer (NK) cells---leaving the infant with virtually no immune defenses. Affected infants appear healthy at birth but quickly develop recurrent, severe, and persistent infections from common viruses, bacteria, and fungi, as well as chronic diarrhea and failure to gain weight.

\synonyms
X-SCID; Common Gamma Chain Deficiency SCID; Bubble Boy Disease

\medterm{X-Linked Sideroblastic Anemia} An inherited anemia, usually caused by a variant in the \textit{ALAS2} gene. Defective heme production causes iron to build up in developing red blood cells, often producing small, pale red cells.

\synonyms
XLSA; ALAS2 Deficiency

\medterm{X-Linked Spinal and Bulbar Muscular Atrophy} An inherited muscle and nerve disorder that usually begins in adulthood and slowly causes weakness. It may also cause muscle twitching, trouble speaking or swallowing, breast enlargement, and reduced fertility.

\synonyms
Kennedy Disease; SBMA; Bulbo-Spinal Muscular Atrophy

\medterm{XO Syndrome} Alternate name; see \medref{Turner Syndrome}.

\medterm{XP} Abbreviation for xeroderma pigmentosum, a rare DNA-repair disorder causing extreme sensitivity to ultraviolet light and a markedly increased risk of early skin and eye cancers.

\medterm{X-Ray} An imaging test that uses a small amount of radiation to create a picture of structures inside the body. It is especially useful for bones, lungs, teeth, and some other tissues.

\medterm{X-Ray Attenuation} The reduction in intensity of an X-ray beam as it passes through tissue, through absorption and scattering. Different tissues attenuate X-rays by different amounts, creating contrast in an image.

\medterm{X-Ray Crystallography} A laboratory method that uses X-rays to work out the three-dimensional structure of molecules such as proteins. The molecule is made into a crystal, and the pattern produced by the X-rays is analyzed.

\synonyms
X-Ray Crystal Diffraction Analysis

\medterm{X-Ray Shield} A barrier made of lead or another radiation-absorbing material that reduces exposure to scattered X-rays, especially for staff or the public outside the imaging room. Patient shields can obscure anatomy or interfere with automatic exposure controls, sometimes reducing image quality or increasing radiation dose.

\medterm{X-Ray Therapy} Treatment that uses carefully aimed X-rays, usually from a machine outside the body, to damage cancer cells or relieve symptoms.

\medterm{XX Male Syndrome} An older name for 46,XX testicular difference of sex development, in which a person with two X chromosomes develops typically male traits, often because the \textit{SRY} gene has moved onto an X chromosome. Infertility is common.

\medterm{XXX Syndrome} Alternate name; see \medref{Triple X Syndrome}.

\medterm{XXXX Syndrome} A rare sex-chromosome variation, also called tetrasomy X, in which a person has four X chromosomes (48,XXXX). Features vary and may include developmental or learning difficulties, differences in physical development, and ovarian dysfunction.

\medterm{XXY Males} Older name; see \medref{Klinefelter Syndrome}.

\medterm{XXY Syndrome} Alternate name; see \medref{Klinefelter Syndrome}.
\medterm{Xylitol} A sugar alcohol used as a sweetener and in some oral-care products. Large amounts can cause abdominal discomfort and diarrhea.

\medterm{Xylose} A simple sugar found in some plants. A form called D-xylose has been used in tests of how well the small intestine absorbs nutrients and is also used in laboratory studies.

\medterm{Xylose Absorption Test} A test that measures D-xylose in the blood or urine after it is swallowed to assess absorption by the small intestine. A low result may indicate impaired intestinal absorption, although kidney function and other factors can affect the result.

\synonyms
D-Xylose Absorption Test; D-Xylose Tolerance Test

\medterm{XYY Syndrome} A sex-chromosome variation in which a person has an extra Y chromosome (47,XYY). Many have no obvious physical features; some have tall stature or speech, learning, or developmental difficulties.
