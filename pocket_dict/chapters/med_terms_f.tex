\medterm{Functional Gastrointestinal Disorders} Digestive symptoms caused by altered gut function without an evident structural disease or infection.

\medterm{Functional Incontinence} Involuntary urine leakage because a person cannot reach or use the toilet in time despite recognizing the need to void.

\medterm{Fabry Disease} An X-linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by deficient alpha-galactosidase A
activity and accumulation of globotriaosylceramide in multiple organs.

\medterm{Face Presentation} A fetal presentation in which the neck is hyperextended and the face is the presenting
part. The position of the fetal chin influences whether vaginal birth is possible and how labor is managed.

\medterm{Facet Joints} Zygapophysial joints connecting adjacent vertebral articular processes. Orientation
varies: cervical (45 degrees, allowing rotation), thoracic (coronal, allowing rotation
but limiting flexion/extension), lumbar (sagittal, allowing flexion/extension but
limiting rotation). Innervated by medial branches of dorsal rami.

\medterm{Facial Edema} Swelling caused by excess fluid in the tissues of the face. Causes include allergy, infection,
trauma, kidney or heart disease, and venous obstruction. Sudden swelling of the lips,
tongue, or throat with breathing difficulty is an emergency.

\medterm{facelift} Rhytidectomy: a surgical procedure to improve the appearance of the face and neck by removing excess skin and tightening underlying tissues. It can address sagging skin, deep folds, and jowls. Recovery includes swelling and bruising for several weeks.

\synonyms
Rhytidectomy

\medterm{Facial Nerve Problems} Disorders affecting cranial nerve VII, which controls facial expression, taste from part of the tongue, and some
tear and salivary function. Weakness may result from Bell palsy, stroke, infection, trauma, or a tumor. Sudden facial
weakness, especially with arm weakness or speech difficulty, requires emergency assessment.

\medterm{Facial hair, excess in women} Hirsutism: excessive androgen-dependent hair growth in male-pattern distribution (face, chest, back) in women. Most common cause is polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). Other causes include adrenal hyperplasia, tumors, and medications. Evaluation includes hormone levels and imaging.

\synonyms
Hirsutism

\medterm{Facial Pain} Pain felt in the face that may arise from dental disease, sinus disease, temporomandibular disorders, nerve disorders, infection, or other causes.

\medterm{Facial palsy} Weakness or paralysis of the facial muscles on one side, most commonly caused by Bell palsy (idiopathic cranial nerve VII dysfunction). Other causes include stroke, infection (herpes zoster oticus), trauma, tumor, or Lyme disease. Sudden facial weakness with other neurological signs requires urgent evaluation.

\synonyms
Bell's palsy; facial paralysis

\medterm{Facial paralysis} Weakness or loss of movement of the facial muscles caused by dysfunction of the facial nerve or its pathways; sudden onset requires urgent evaluation for stroke.

\medterm{Facilitated Diffusion} Passive transport of molecules across cell membranes via specific transmembrane protein
carriers or channels, moving substances down their concentration gradient without energy
expenditure. Channel-mediated facilitated diffusion uses ion channels (voltage-gated,
ligand-gated, or mechanically-gated) for rapid ion movement.

\medterm{Factitious Disorder} Presentation of physical or psychological symptoms or imposing injury or disease on
another, with the deceptive intention of assuming the sick role. In factitious disorder
imposed on self, the individual falsifies signs or symptoms, induces injury or disease,
and presents to medical providers.

\medterm{Factor H Deficiency} Autosomal recessive (CFH mutation). Factor H regulates alternative pathway (cofactor for
factor I, accelerates decay). Uncontrolled alternative pathway activation -> C3
consumption -> low C3, normal C4. Atypical HUS (aHUS): microangiopathic hemolytic
anemia, thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury.

\medterm{Factor I Deficiency} Autosomal recessive (CFI mutation). Factor I cleaves C3b/C4b (cofactor: factor H, MCP,
C4BP). Uncontrolled complement -> low C3, low C4, low factor H. Recurrent pyogenic
infections, aHUS, C3 glomerulopathy, SLE-like disease. Treatment: eculizumab for
aHUS/C3G; plasma infusion (contains factor I); IVIG.

\medterm{Factor V Deficiency} An inherited or acquired bleeding disorder caused by reduced factor V activity,
impairing thrombin generation and fibrin formation. It may cause easy bruising,
nosebleeds, heavy menstrual bleeding, postoperative bleeding, or severe bleeding in rare
cases.

\medterm{Factor V Leiden} An inherited factor V variant that reduces sensitivity to activated protein C and increases the risk of venous thromboembolism. The risk varies with zygosity, family history, and other factors.

\medterm{Factor V Leiden mutation} A factor V variant resistant to activated protein C, causing inherited thrombophilia and
increased venous thrombosis risk.

\medterm{Factor VII} A blood-clotting protein made mainly in the liver that begins the tissue-factor pathway of coagulation.
Inherited deficiency can cause abnormal bleeding, ranging from easy bruising to serious
bleeding after injury or procedures.

\medterm{Factor VIII} A clotting protein that works with factor IX to help form a stable blood clot. Inherited deficiency
causes hemophilia A, characterized by prolonged or spontaneous bleeding into joints,
muscles, or other tissues.

\medterm{Facultative Anaerobe} A microorganism capable of growth under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions, using
respiration when a suitable terminal electron acceptor is present and fermentation or
anaerobic respiration when it is not.

\medterm{FAD (Flavin Adenine Dinucleotide)} A flavin-derived redox coenzyme, usually bound tightly to enzymes as a prosthetic group.
FAD accepts two hydrogen atoms to form FADH2, which donates electrons to the respiratory
chain through selected dehydrogenases.

\synonyms
Flavin Adenine Dinucleotide

\medterm{Failed Induction of Labor} The inability to achieve active labor and progressive cervical change after an adequate
trial of induction. Definition: no active labor (cervical change) after at least 12--18 hours
of oxytocin with ruptured membranes and adequate contractions. Management: the latent
phase may be prolonged (up to 24 hours or more).

\medterm{Failed Intubation} Inability to successfully place an endotracheal tube after multiple attempts. In the
operating room, a failed intubation algorithm is followed: call for help, maintain
oxygenation (face mask, supraglottic airway), and decide to wake the patient (if
non-emergent) or proceed with a supraglottic airway (if surgery is urgent).

\medterm{Failure to Thrive} Inadequate growth in children, defined as weight below the 5th percentile or crossing
two major percentile lines on growth charts. Causes include caloric insufficiency
(neglect, poor feeding), organic disease (congenital heart disease, renal disease), and
psychosocial factors.

\medterm{Fainting} Fainting is a brief loss of consciousness from transient inadequate brain perfusion, often caused by reflex, orthostatic, or cardiac mechanisms; cardiac warning features require urgent evaluation.

\synonyms
syncope.

\medterm{Falciform ligament} A sickle-shaped peritoneal fold that attaches the liver to the anterior abdominal wall
and diaphragm, separating the left and right lobes of the liver. It contains the
ligamentum teres, the obliterated remnant of the umbilical vein. It may be implicated in
rare complications such as internal herniation or necrosis following trauma.

\medterm{Fall} Unintended change in position to ground. Major cause of morbidity/mortality in elderly.
Risk factors: gait/balance impairment, muscle weakness, visual impairment, polypharmacy,
environmental hazards, orthostatic hypotension. Prevention: exercise, medication review,
vision correction, home modification, hip protectors.

\medterm{Fall Prevention} Measures that reduce the risk of falling, including strength and balance training, medication review,
vision assessment, safer footwear, and environmental modification.

\medterm{Fall Risk} The likelihood that a person will fall, based on factors such as gait, balance, strength, vision, medicines,
orthostatic blood-pressure changes, and the environment.

\medterm{Fall Screening} Assessment of a person's history and risk factors for falling to guide prevention and further evaluation.

\medterm{Fallen arches} Pes planus: flattening of the medial longitudinal arch of the foot. May be flexible (appearing only when standing) or rigid. Can cause foot, ankle, or knee pain, or be asymptomatic. Treatment includes supportive footwear, orthotics, and stretching exercises.

\synonyms
Pes planus; flat feet

\medterm{Fallopian Tube} The paired muscular tubes (10-12 cm) connecting the ovaries to the uterus, conducting
oocytes and sperm and the site of fertilization (usually ampulla). Segments:
infundibulum (fimbriae—sweep the oocyte), ampulla, isthmus, intramural.

\medterm{Fallopian Tube Cancer} A rare tumor histologically similar to high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma. Presents
with a pelvic mass, pain, or watery discharge; staging and treatment follow the
principles of ovarian cancer.

\medterm{Fallopian tube torsion} Twisting of the fallopian tube around its pedicle, usually around an underlying mass
such as a hydrosalpinx or paraovarian cyst, causing acute pelvic pain and requiring
urgent surgical intervention.

\medterm{False Ribs} Ribs 8-10 not articulating directly with the sternum; their cartilages join rib 7's
cartilage forming the costal margin. Greater mobility and flexibility of the lower
thoracic wall. More susceptible to fractures at their angles due to greater length and
less rigid attachment.

\medterm{False-Negative Result} A negative test result in a person who does have the target condition. Causes include
inadequate specimen, early or late testing, low organism or biomarker concentration,
prior treatment, technical error, and limited test sensitivity.

\medterm{False-Positive Result} A positive test result in a person who does not have the target condition. Causes
include limited specificity, cross-reactivity, technical error, contamination, and
testing in a low-prevalence population.

\medterm{Familial Adenomatous Polyposis} Hereditary condition with hundreds to thousands of adenomas throughout the colon caused
by germline APC gene mutations with autosomal dominant inheritance. Polyps develop
during the second decade and colorectal cancer occurs by age forty if untreated.
Diagnosis is by colonoscopy showing numerous polyps and genetic testing.

\medterm{Familial Hypercholesterolemia} An inherited disorder causing persistently high low-density lipoprotein cholesterol,
usually because of impaired LDL-receptor pathway function. It increases the risk of premature
atherosclerotic disease; diagnosis uses lipid levels, family history, physical findings, and
genetic testing when available.

\medterm{Familial Hypocalciuric Hypercalcemia} Autosomal dominant condition caused by inactivating mutations in the calcium-sensing
receptor gene leading to a higher set point for calcium-mediated PTH suppression.
Presents with mild hypercalcemia, inappropriately normal or mildly elevated PTH, and low
urinary calcium excretion with calcium-to-creatinine clearance ratio less than 0.01.

\medterm{Familial Mediterranean Fever} An autosomal recessive autoinflammatory disorder (MEFV gene mutations,
pyrin/inflammasome dysregulation) causing recurrent self-limited febrile attacks with
serositis (peritonitis, pleuritis, synovitis), erysipelas-like erythema, lasting 12-72
hours.

\medterm{Familial multiple lipomatosis} An autosomal dominant condition characterized by the development of numerous,
symmetrically distributed subcutaneous lipomas, typically appearing in early adulthood.
The lipomas are encapsulated, painless, and slow-growing, affecting the trunk and
extremities. Unlike Dercum disease, they are not painful.

\medterm{Family Involvement} The participation of family members or other support persons in care planning, treatment, rehabilitation, and support, with attention to the patient’s preferences and caregiver burden.

\medterm{Fanconi Anemia} An autosomal recessive (or X-linked) bone marrow failure syndrome caused by mutations in
at least 22 FANC genes involved in DNA repair (the FA/BRCA pathway).

\medterm{Fanconi Syndrome} Generalized proximal tubular dysfunction with impaired reabsorption of glucose, amino
acids, phosphate, uric acid, and bicarbonate. Causes glycosuria, aminoaciduria,
phosphaturia, and metabolic acidosis. Associated with proximal RTA. Causes include
myeloma, heavy metal toxicity, Wilson disease, and genetic disorders.

\medterm{Farmer's Lung} The most common HP, caused by thermophilic actinomycetes in moldy hay. Presents acutely
4--8 hours after exposure with fever, cough, and dyspnea.

\medterm{Farsightedness} Hyperopia, a refractive error in which light focuses behind the retina, causing near blur or eyestrain; glasses or contact lenses can correct it.

\synonyms
hyperopia, in which light focuses behind the retina when.

\medterm{Fascial Loop} A loop or sling made from a person’s tissue or synthetic material and used to support or reinforce a body structure, such as the urethra.

\medterm{Fasciitis} Inflammation of fascia, the connective tissue surrounding muscles and other structures. Causes and severity vary widely, from localized plantar fasciitis to serious infectious fasciitis.

\medterm{Fascioliasis} A parasitic infection caused by liver flukes of the genus Fasciola, acquired by
ingesting metacercariae on contaminated aquatic plants or in water. Acute hepatic
migration may cause fever, right-upper-quadrant pain, hepatomegaly, and eosinophilia;
chronic biliary infection may cause colic, cholangitis, or biliary obstruction.

\medterm{Fasciotomy} Surgical incision through the fascia to relieve compartment syndrome. Indications: acute
compartment syndrome (trauma, reperfusion, burns, crush injury). Classic signs: pain out
of proportion, pain with passive stretch, paresthesias, pallor, pulselessness (late).

\medterm{Fast Heart Rate} A heart rate faster than expected for age or activity, usually called tachycardia. It
may be a normal response to exercise, fever, pain, or stress, or a sign of arrhythmia or
another medical disorder.

\medterm{Fasting Guidelines} Preoperative fasting guidelines minimize aspiration risk while reducing patient
discomfort and catabolism. Clear liquids (water, clear juice without pulp, black coffee,
sports drinks) are allowed up to 2 hours before surgery. Breast milk up to 4 hours.
Infant formula up to 6 hours. Light meal (toast and clear liquid) up to 6 hours.

\medterm{Fat Embolism} Fat embolism occurs when fat globules enter the bloodstream, typically following long
bone fractures, orthopedic surgery, or severe trauma involving adipose tissue.

\medterm{Fat Necrosis} A specialized form of necrosis that occurs in adipose tissue, most commonly following
acute pancreatitis when activated pancreatic lipases escape into the surrounding tissues
and digest triglycerides in mesenteric or subcutaneous fat cells.
In the breast, it may follow trauma or surgery and can produce a firm lump that mimics malignancy on examination or imaging.

\medterm{Fat-Soluble Toxins} Lipophilic toxins stored in fat, prolonged elimination. Examples: organochlorine
pesticides, PCBs, dioxins, tetrachlorodiphenylmethane.

\medterm{Fatigue} Fatigue is a persistent sense of low energy or reduced capacity that may result from sleep problems, anemia, infection, endocrine disease, medication, mood disorder, or systemic illness.
relieved by rest. It may result from sleep disturbance, anemia, infection, endocrine
disease, chronic organ disease, medication exposure, or psychological and neurological
conditions.

\medterm{Fatigue (Cancer)} Persistent tiredness or reduced energy associated with cancer or its treatment. It may result from the disease, anemia, sleep problems, medicines, mood, or other factors.

\synonyms
Cancer

\medterm{Fatty Acid} A carboxylic acid with a hydrocarbon chain that may be saturated or contain one or more
double bonds. Fatty acids provide energy, form membrane lipids, and serve as precursors
for signaling molecules.

\medterm{Fatty Acid Synthase} Multi-enzyme complex for fatty acid synthesis. Produces palmitate.

\medterm{Fatty Liver Disease} A condition characterized by the accumulation of excess fat in liver cells, most
commonly associated with obesity, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome.

\medterm{Febrile} Feverish; having a high body temperature.

\medterm{Febrile Neutropenia} Fever in a patient with a sufficiently low absolute neutrophil count to create a high
risk of serious infection, generally defined as a single temperature of at least 38.3°C
or sustained temperature of at least 38.0°C with ANC below 500/\(\mu\mathrm{L}\) or expected to fall
below that level.

\medterm{Febrile Seizure} A seizure occurring in a child usually between 6 months and 5 years of age with fever
but without evidence of central nervous system infection or a previous afebrile seizure.
Simple febrile seizures are generalized, brief, and nonrecurrent within the same
illness; prolonged or focal events require further evaluation.

\medterm{Fecal Calprotectin} Neutrophil-derived protein measured in stool as a marker of intestinal inflammation. It
is stable in stool at room temperature for up to seven days. Useful for differentiating
inflammatory from functional bowel disease and monitoring inflammatory bowel disease
activity.

\medterm{Fecal Elastase} Pancreatic enzyme measured in stool as a marker of exocrine pancreatic function. Low
levels below two hundred micrograms per gram indicate pancreatic insufficiency, and
levels below one hundred are consistent with severe exocrine insufficiency.

\medterm{Fecal Immunochemical Test} A stool-based screening test that uses antibodies to detect human hemoglobin.
A positive result generally requires evaluation of the colon, commonly with colonoscopy. Test intervals and follow-up
depend on screening guidance and clinical risk.

\medterm{Fecal Impaction} A large, firm mass of stool retained in the rectum or colon that cannot be evacuated
normally. It may cause abdominal or rectal pain, distention, overflow diarrhoea, urinary
symptoms, nausea, or bowel obstruction and is associated with constipation, immobility,
dehydration, neurological disease, and medications.

\medterm{Fecal Incontinence} Inability to control bowel movements ranging from minor soiling to complete loss of
continence. Causes include sphincter injury from obstetric trauma or surgery, neuropathy
from diabetes or spinal cord injury, diarrhea, rectal prolapse, and cognitive
impairment.

\medterm{Fecal microbiota transplantation} Transfer of processed stool from a screened donor into a patient’s gastrointestinal tract to
restore microbial diversity. It may be used for selected recurrent Clostridioides difficile
infection when standard treatment has failed; careful donor screening and clinical
supervision are required.

\medterm{Fecundity} The biological ability to conceive and produce a live birth. Fecundity is influenced by
age (declines significantly after 35), ovarian reserve, tubal patency, uterine factors,
and male fertility (sperm count, motility, morphology). Fecundability: the probability
of achieving pregnancy in a single menstrual cycle.

\medterm{Feedback Inhibition} Regulatory mechanism where the end product of a metabolic pathway inhibits an earlier
enzyme (usually the first committed step). Examples: isoleucine inhibiting threonine
deaminase, CTP inhibiting aspartate transcarbamoylase, ATP inhibiting
phosphofructokinase. Allows efficient resource utilization and prevents overproduction.

\medterm{Feeding Jejunostomy} Surgical or endoscopic placement of a feeding tube into the jejunum for enteral
nutrition when upper gastrointestinal access is not feasible. Used in patients with head
and neck cancers, esophageal obstruction, or severe dysphagia. Provides long-term
nutritional support and avoids the complications of parenteral nutrition.

\medterm{Feeding Tube} A flexible tube used to deliver nutrition, fluids, or medicines to the stomach or intestine when oral intake is inadequate or unsafe.

\medterm{Felty Syndrome} Triad of rheumatoid arthritis, splenomegaly, and neutropenia (ANC <2000). Complication
of long-standing, severe, seropositive RA. Increased infection risk. Treatment: DMARDs
(methotrexate, TNF inhibitors), G-CSF for neutropenia, splenectomy in refractory cases.

\medterm{Female Genital Mutilation} FGM: the partial or total removal of female external genitalia for non-medical reasons,
a human rights violation practiced in parts of Africa, the Middle East, and Asia (types
I-IV). Immediate complications: severe pain, hemorrhage, infection, urinary retention.

\medterm{Female Infertility} Failure to achieve pregnancy after a defined period of regular unprotected intercourse, due to factors
involving ovulation, the reproductive tract, ovarian reserve, or other causes.

\medterm{Female Orgasmic Disorder (Anorgasmia)} Persistent or recurrent delay in, infrequency of, or absence of orgasm that causes personal
distress. It may be lifelong or acquired and can have medical, psychological,
relationship, medication-related, or other causes.

\synonyms
Anorgasmia

\medterm{Female Sexual Interest/Arousal Disorder} Persistent or recurrent reduction in sexual interest, thoughts, responsiveness, or arousal that
causes personal distress. Causes may include medical conditions, medicines, mood,
relationship factors, pain, or other psychological and social factors.

\medterm{Femoral Artery} Continues from external iliac through the femoral triangle and adductor canal to become
the popliteal artery. Branches: superficial/deep circumflex femoral, deep femoral
(profunda femoris), muscular. Palpable in the femoral triangle; most common site for
arterial catheterization. Related to femoral vein medially and nerve laterally.

\medterm{Femoral Hernia} A hernia in which abdominal contents pass through the femoral ring into the femoral canal,
below the inguinal ligament and medial to the femoral vessels. It may become incarcerated or strangulated and often
requires surgical assessment.

\medterm{Femoral Nerve} Largest lumbar plexus nerve (L2-L4), passing through psoas major and entering the thigh
under the inguinal ligament lateral to the femoral artery. Innervates quadriceps,
sartorius, pectineus; sensation to anterior thigh and medial leg (saphenous nerve). Used
for knee surgery nerve block anesthesia.

\medterm{Femoral Shaft Fractures} Femoral shaft fractures: high energy (MVC, fall from height) or low energy (elderly,
pathologic). Winquist-Hansen classification of comminution: I (none), II (butterfly
<50\% width), III (butterfly >50\%), IV (segmental).

\medterm{Femoral Triangle} Upper anterior thigh space bounded superiorly by the inguinal ligament, laterally by
sartorius, and medially by adductor longus. Contents from lateral to medial: femoral
nerve, artery, vein, and deep inguinal lymph nodes. The femoral artery is used for
arterial catheterization. The femoral vein is accessed for central venous lines.

\medterm{Femoral Vein} The major deep vein of the thigh, continuation of the popliteal vein (through the
adductor hiatus), running with the femoral artery in the femoral sheath (medial to
artery) through the femoral triangle, becoming the external iliac vein at the inguinal
ligament. Receives the great saphenous vein.

\medterm{Femur} Longest, heaviest, strongest body bone. Proximal: head, neck, trochanters. Shaft: linea aspera. Distal: condyles, epicondyles. The neck connects head to shaft at approximately 125 degrees.

\medterm{Femur Length} The length of the fetal femur (diaphysis), measured from the greater trochanter to the
distal femoral metaphysis. Used for dating (14-26 weeks) and growth assessment. Normal
growth: ~2 mm per week. Short femur: can be constitutional (especially with short
parents), associated with aneuploidy (trisomy 21, skeletal dysplasias), or IUGR.

\medterm{Fencer's Pose} The posture produced by the asymmetric tonic neck reflex, with extension of the arm and leg on the side
toward which the head is turned and flexion on the opposite side.

\medterm{FeNO (Fractional Exhaled Nitric Oxide)} A breath test that estimates type 2, often eosinophilic, airway inflammation. The result is interpreted with symptoms, age, treatment, and other clinical findings.

\synonyms
Fractional Exhaled Nitric Oxide

\medterm{Fenofibrate} A fibric acid derivative (fibrate) activating PPAR-alpha, increasing HDL and reducing
triglycerides, with modest LDL effects. Used for severe hypertriglyceridemia
(pancreatitis risk), mixed dyslipidemia, and as an adjunct in residual
hypertriglyceridemia.

\medterm{Fentanyl Derivatives} Potent synthetic opioids related to fentanyl that may be used for analgesia or anesthesia. Examples include
sufentanil, remifentanil, and alfentanil; their potency, duration, dosing, and risks
differ, and they require careful monitoring because they can depress breathing.

\medterm{Ferritin} Intracellular iron storage protein reflecting total body iron stores. Low ferritin: iron
deficiency anemia (<30 ng/mL, absolute; <100 can be consistent with iron deficiency in
inflammation).

\medterm{Fertilisation} The union of a sperm and an egg (oocyte) to form a zygote, the first cell of a new
individual.

\medterm{Fertility} The biological capacity to conceive and produce offspring. Fertility depends on the
proper functioning of the reproductive system in both males and females, including
ovulation, sperm production, and hormonal regulation.

\medterm{Fertility Preservation} Measures taken before gonadotoxic cancer therapy to preserve reproductive potential,
including embryo or oocyte cryopreservation, sperm banking, and ovarian or testicular
tissue cryopreservation.

\medterm{FESS} Functional endoscopic sinus surgery is a minimally invasive surgical technique that uses
nasal endoscopes and specialized instruments to restore normal sinus drainage and
ventilation by removing obstructive tissue and opening blocked sinus ostia, while
preserving as much normal sinus mucosa and anatomy as possible.

\medterm{Fetal Acid-Base Status} Assessment of fetal oxygenation and acid-base balance, usually using umbilical cord blood gases at
birth or other intrapartum information. Interpretation depends on the complete gas panel,
sampling site, timing, clinical condition, and laboratory reference ranges; marked
acidemia may indicate impaired oxygenation.

\medterm{Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder} A broad spectrum of physical, behavioral, and cognitive impairments caused by prenatal
alcohol exposure.

\medterm{Fetal Alcohol Syndrome} A collection of birth defects resulting from exposure of the fetus to alcohol during
pregnancy, representing the most severe form of fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD).

\medterm{Fetal Attitude} The relationship of the fetal body parts to one another, especially the degree of flexion or extension of
the head and limbs. The usual attitude is flexion, but deflexed, neutral, or extended
attitudes can affect the presenting part and labor.

\medterm{Fetal Bradycardia} A fetal heart-rate baseline below 110 beats per minute for at least 10 minutes. Causes include
medicines, maternal conditions, fetal conduction disease, hypoxia, cord problems, or placental
abruption; persistent or severe bradycardia requires urgent obstetric assessment.

\medterm{Fetal Distress} Abnormal fetal status during labor, indicated by abnormal fetal heart rate patterns
(late or prolonged decelerations, minimal variability, absent accelerations). May
indicate fetal hypoxia, cord compression, or placental insufficiency.

\medterm{Fetal Echocardiography} A specialized ultrasound examination of the fetal heart used to assess cardiac structure, rhythm, and function, including possible congenital heart disease.

\medterm{Fetal Growth Restriction} Failure of a fetus to reach its biologic growth potential, often associated with
placental insufficiency. It is assessed using serial growth measurements, amniotic
fluid, Doppler studies, and fetal surveillance rather than a single estimated-weight
threshold alone.

\medterm{Fetal Heart Rate} Normal fetal heart rate ranges from 110-160 beats per minute. Monitored via auscultation
with a fetoscope, Doppler ultrasound, or continuous electronic fetal monitoring.
Baseline FHR is determined over a 10-minute window and is modulated by the autonomic
nervous system.

\medterm{Fetal Hydrops} Abnormal accumulation of fluid in at least two fetal compartments, such as the skin, chest, abdomen, or
around the heart. Causes include fetal anemia, heart disease, infection, chromosome conditions,
and immune incompatibility; it requires specialist obstetric evaluation.

\medterm{Fetal Hypoxia} Inadequate oxygen available to the fetus before or during birth. It may result from placental

\medterm{Food Allergy} Immune-mediated reaction to food protein. IgE-mediated: anaphylaxis. Non-IgE: EoE, food
protein-induced enterocolitis. Common allergens: milk, egg, peanut, tree nut, fish,
shellfish, soy, wheat.

\medterm{Food Intolerance} Adverse reaction not immune-mediated. Lactose intolerance, gluten sensitivity
(non-celiac), histamine intolerance, FODMAP sensitivity. Diagnosis: elimination diet,
challenge.

\medterm{Food Poisoning} Illness caused by consuming food contaminated with bacteria, viruses, parasites, or
their toxins. Common pathogens include Salmonella, Escherichia coli, Listeria, and
norovirus, with symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal cramps, and
fever.

\medterm{Food-Borne Disease} Illness caused by eating food or drinking water contaminated with infectious organisms, toxins, or harmful chemicals.

\medterm{Food-Drug Interaction} An alteration in drug absorption, metabolism, distribution, or effect caused by a food,
beverage, nutrient, or supplement. Examples include vitamin K affecting warfarin
response, grapefruit affecting selected CYP3A4 substrates, and calcium impairing
absorption of some medicines.

\medterm{Foodborne Illness} Any illness resulting from the ingestion of contaminated food, encompassing infections
and intoxications caused by pathogens, toxins, or chemical contaminants. It affects
millions annually and poses particular risks to pregnant women, young children, older
adults, and immunocompromised individuals.

\medterm{Foot Drop} Weakness or paralysis of ankle dorsiflexion causing the forefoot to drag or the patient
to lift the knee abnormally while walking. Common causes include common peroneal nerve
injury, L5 radiculopathy, motor-neuron disease, and peripheral neuropathy.

\medterm{Foot Pain} Pain in the foot caused by injury, overuse, arthritis, nerve disease, infection, or circulation problems.
Persistent pain, inability to bear weight, numbness, discoloration, fever, or an open wound
requires medical assessment.

\medterm{Force Plates} Instruments that measure forces exerted by the feet on the ground during standing or movement, helping assess gait, balance, symmetry, and loading.

\medterm{Forced Expiratory Volume} The volume of air forcibly exhaled in a specified time during a maximal expiratory
effort. FEV1 is the volume exhaled in the first second; normally at least 70-80 percent
of the forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC ratio).

\medterm{Forced Oscillation Technique} Similar to impulse oscillometry, applying small pressure oscillations at various
frequencies during tidal breathing to measure respiratory resistance and reactance. It
provides information about airway mechanics without requiring forced expiratory
maneuvers.

\medterm{Forced Vital Capacity} Total air exhaled after maximal inspiration. Reduced in both obstructive and restrictive
disease. In restrictive disease, FVC reduced with preserved ratio.

\medterm{Forceps} Grasping instrument. Tissue forceps: with teeth. Dressing forceps: without teeth.

\medterm{Forceps (Obstetric)} A paired instrument used in selected operative vaginal deliveries to assist delivery of the fetal head.

\synonyms
Obstetric

\medterm{Forceps (Surgical)} Handheld instruments used to grasp, hold, move, or manipulate tissue or medical materials during a procedure.

\synonyms
Surgical

\medterm{Forceps (Vascular)} A surgical instrument used to grasp, hold, or manipulate tissue, vessels, or medical materials.

\synonyms
Vascular

\medterm{Forceps Delivery} Assisted vaginal delivery using forceps (curved blades applied to the fetal head) to
expedite delivery for maternal or fetal indications (prolonged second stage, fetal
distress, maternal exhaustion, medical conditions limiting pushing).

\medterm{Forceps-Assisted Delivery} Operative vaginal delivery using forceps (two curved blades applied to the fetal head).
Types: outlet (forceps applied at the introitus, fetal scalp visible), low (station
\(\geq +2\), not at outlet), midpelvic (station \(\geq +2\) but head above ischial spines), and
rotational (forceps used to rotate from OP to OA).

\medterm{Fordyce's angiokeratomas} Benign vascular ectasias of the superficial dermal capillaries covered by an epidermal
reaction, appearing as small, dark red to purple, asymptomatic papules on the scrotum,
vulva, or oral mucosa. They are common, increase with age, and are clinically
insignificant. They may bleed if traumatized.

\medterm{Foreign Body} Any object lodged in body. Types: organic (wood, thorn), inorganic (metal, glass, plastic). Radiopaque vs. radiolucent. Removal: surgical, endoscopic, spontaneous passage.

\medterm{Foreign Body Aspiration} Inhalation of object into airway. Common in children (peanuts, coins, small toys).
Signs: choking, coughing, stridor, wheezing, decreased breath sounds. Diagnosis: chest
X-ray (may be negative), bronchoscopy.

\medterm{Foreign Body Reaction} Inflammation and immune response to material that remains in tissue, such as a splinter, suture,
implant, or other medical device. It may cause a lump, redness, drainage, pain, or scarring;
treatment depends on the material and whether infection is present.

\medterm{Foreign-Body Aspiration} Entry of an object into the larynx, trachea, or bronchi, most often in toddlers. Sudden
cough, choking, unilateral wheeze, or focal air trapping may occur; complete airway
obstruction is an immediate emergency and requires age-appropriate resuscitation.

\medterm{Forensic Anthropology} The application of physical-anthropology methods to legal investigations involving human remains,
especially skeletal material. It can help establish whether remains are human, estimate
biological characteristics, assess trauma, and support identification.

\medterm{Forensic Entomology} The study of insects and other arthropods in legal investigations, principally to
estimate the postmortem interval (PMI) and to link suspects to scenes.

\medterm{Forensic Evidence} Physical, biological, documentary, or testimonial material gathered, preserved, and
analyzed to establish facts in a legal investigation. Categories include biological
evidence (blood, semen, hair), trace evidence (fibers, glass, gunshot residue), pattern
evidence (fingerprints, toolmarks, bloodstain patterns), and digital evidence.

\medterm{Forensic Examination} Structured medicolegal evaluation of a living or deceased person, scene, or evidence to
document findings relevant to a legal matter. Components include sexual assault
examination with evidence collection, child abuse assessment, injury documentation,
driving-impairment evaluation, and autopsy.

\medterm{Forensic Medicine (Legal Medicine)} Branch of medicine that applies medical, biological, and scientific knowledge to legal
questions and the administration of justice.

\synonyms
Legal Medicine

\medterm{Forensic Odontology} The application of dental science to legal investigations. It may support human identification by
comparing dental records, radiographs, and distinctive dental anatomy and may assist in
the assessment of dental injuries.

\medterm{Forensic Pathology} A branch of pathology that applies medical knowledge to legal questions, especially the cause and
manner of death and the documentation of injuries. Forensic pathologists examine deaths
that are sudden, unexpected, violent, or otherwise subject to legal investigation.

\medterm{Forensic Psychiatry} The medical specialty at the interface of psychiatry and the law. Core activities:
competency to stand trial, criminal responsibility/insanity defense (mental state at
time of offense), dangerousness/risk assessment, civil commitment, testamentary
capacity, and personal injury claims.

\medterm{Forensic Radiography} The use of imaging (X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound) in forensic and medico-legal
investigation.

\medterm{Forensic Toxicology} The analysis of drugs, poisons, and their metabolites in biological specimens (blood,
urine, vitreous humor, hair, tissues) to determine their role in intoxication,
poisoning, or death.

\medterm{Forensic Toxicology Screen} Laboratory analysis of biological specimens for drugs, alcohols, poisons, and their
metabolites. A screening result is presumptive until confirmed and quantified by a
validated method such as mass spectrometry, with interpretation based on specimen type,
timing, therapeutic use, and postmortem changes.

\medterm{Formaldehyde} Preservative, industrial chemical. Irritant, carcinogen (nasopharyngeal cancer).
Sensitizer. Use in fume hood, PPE required.

\medterm{Formalin-Fixed Paraffin-Embedded Tissue} Tissue preserved in formalin, processed, and embedded in paraffin wax for sectioning and
microscopic examination. FFPE blocks support histology, immunohistochemistry, and many
molecular tests but may contain fixation-related nucleic-acid or antigen alterations.

\medterm{Formication} A sensation that insects are crawling on or under the skin when none are present. It may occur with
nerve disorders, menopause, substance withdrawal, medication effects, or psychiatric illness.
Persistent or new symptoms should be medically evaluated.

\medterm{Formula Feeding} Alternative to breastfeeding using commercially prepared infant formula. Types include
cow's milk-based, soy-based, hydrolyzed protein, and amino acid-based formulas.
Iron-fortified formulas standard to prevent iron deficiency anemia. Preparation requires
clean water and proper sterilization of bottles.

\medterm{Fornix} The conjunctival fornix is the redundant, dome-shaped fold of conjunctiva where the
palpebral conjunctiva transitions to the bulbar conjunctiva, forming a deep recess
superiorly (superior fornix), inferiorly (inferior fornix), and medially and laterally
(medial and lateral fornices).

\medterm{Fortification} Addition of nutrients to food. Examples: iodized salt, vitamin D milk, folic acid
grains. Public health strategy for deficiency prevention.

\medterm{Four-Dimensional Ultrasound} Real-time 3D ultrasound. Used in obstetrics for fetal imaging.

\medterm{Fournier Gangrene} A rapidly progressive necrotizing infection of the perineum, external genitalia, or
perianal region. It causes severe pain, swelling, fever, systemic toxicity, and tissue
necrosis and requires immediate surgical debridement and broad-spectrum antimicrobial
treatment.

\medterm{Fourth-Degree Tear} A severe perineal tear during childbirth that extends through the anal sphincter and into
the rectal mucosa. It requires careful examination, repair, follow-up, and assessment for complications.

\medterm{Fovea} A tiny depression in the center of the retina that provides the sharpest and most
detailed visual acuity.

\medterm{Fovea Centralis} Central macular pit (approximately 1.5 mm) with the highest cone density (approximately
200,000 per square millimeter) and no rods, providing the sharpest visual acuity and
color vision. Avascular with overlying retinal layers displaced laterally for direct
light access to cones.

\medterm{Fractional Excretion of Sodium} The percentage of filtered sodium excreted in urine, calculated from serum and urine
sodium and creatinine. It may help distinguish prerenal physiology from tubular injury,
although diuretics, CKD, sepsis, and other conditions limit interpretation.

\medterm{Fractional Excretion of Urea (FEUrea)} The percentage of filtered urea excreted in urine. It may be used as an adjunct when
diuretics make FENa less interpretable, but it is also affected by catabolism, sepsis,
gastrointestinal bleeding, and chronic kidney disease.

\synonyms
FEUrea

\medterm{Fractional Flow Reserve} An invasive physiological measurement during coronary catheterization using a pressure
wire to assess the hemodynamic significance of a coronary stenosis. FFR = Pa/Pd (aortic
pressure / distal coronary pressure) during maximal hyperemia (adenosine).

\medterm{Fractional IPV} A reduced-dose schedule of inactivated polio vaccine used in the WHO and India Universal
Immunization Schedule, where one-fifth of the standard intramuscular IPV dose (0.1 mL
instead of 0.5 mL) is given by intradermal injection at each of two deltoid sites (total
0.2 mL per fractional dose), rather than the full 0.5 mL intramuscular dose.

\medterm{Fracture} A break in the continuity of bone, classified by location, pattern (transverse, oblique,
spiral, comminuted), displacement, and whether the fracture communicates with the
external environment (open vs. closed). Fracture healing occurs through inflammatory,
reparative, and remodeling phases.

\medterm{Fracture Displacement} Loss of normal anatomic alignment between fracture fragments, described by translation,
angulation, shortening, or rotation. The acceptable degree depends on the bone, joint
involvement, patient age, and functional demands.

\medterm{Fracture Liaison Service} A coordinated program that identifies patients with fragility fractures, evaluates bone
health and secondary causes, initiates fracture prevention, and ensures follow-up to
reduce recurrent fractures.

\medterm{Fracture of the temporal bone} A break in the thin bones forming the skull base and ear structures, potentially
injuring the facial nerve, carotid artery, or middle ear and presenting with hearing
loss, vertigo, or cerebrospinal fluid leak.

\medterm{Fracture Prevention} Measures that reduce the risk of bone fracture, including assessment and treatment of osteoporosis, strength and balance exercise, fall prevention, and correction of relevant nutritional or medication-related risks.

\medterm{Fracture-Fixation Device} An implant such as a plate, screw, intramedullary nail, or external-fixation component
used to stabilize a broken bone and maintain alignment during healing. Selection depends
on fracture pattern, bone quality, and soft-tissue condition.

\medterm{Fragile X Syndrome} An inherited neurodevelopmental disorder caused by expansion of a CGG repeat in the FMR1 gene, leading to reduced gene expression. It may cause intellectual disability, characteristic physical features, and behavioral or autism-spectrum features.

\medterm{FRAIL Scale} A brief frailty screen assessing Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illnesses, and Loss of
weight. It identifies risk of frailty but is not a substitute for a comprehensive
geriatric assessment.

\medterm{Frailty} Clinical syndrome of decreased reserve and resilience to stressors. Components:
unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, low physical activity, slow gait speed, weak grip
strength. Identification: Fried phenotype, frailty index. Consequences: adverse outcomes
(falls, hospitalization, mortality), polypharmacy, functional decline.

\medterm{Frameshift Mutation} Insertion or deletion of nucleotides not in multiples of three, shifting reading frame.
Usually produces nonfunctional protein. Examples: some cystic fibrosis mutations,
Tay-Sachs disease.

\medterm{Francisella tularensis} A gram-negative coccobacillus that causes tularemia, a zoonotic infection transmitted by
ticks, deer flies, or direct contact with infected animals. The organism can be acquired
through the skin, respiratory tract, or gastrointestinal tract. Ulceroglandular
tularemia presents with skin ulcer and regional lymphadenopathy.

\medterm{Frank-Starling Mechanism} The physiological principle stating that stroke volume increases as end-diastolic volume
(preload) increases, up to an optimal point. Within physiological limits, greater
ventricular stretch leads to stronger contraction due to increased calcium sensitivity
of myofilaments and optimal overlap of actin and myosin.

\medterm{Free Radical} A molecule or atom containing an unpaired electron, making it chemically reactive. Excessive free-radical activity can contribute to oxidative injury.

\medterm{Free Radical Injury} Free radicals are highly reactive molecules with unpaired electrons that can damage
cellular components through oxidative stress. Reactive oxygen species include the
superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide, and the hydroxyl radical generated through the
Fenton reaction.

\medterm{Free Water Clearance} The rate at which the kidneys excrete water in excess of or less than the amount needed
to produce urine isotonic to plasma. Positive clearance indicates excretion of dilute
urine; negative clearance indicates retention of water through concentration of urine.

\medterm{Frenectomy} Surgical removal or repositioning of frenulum. Indications: diastema, gingival
recession, tongue-tie, interference with denture.

\medterm{Frenulum Breve} An abnormally short penile frenulum that may cause pain, tearing, or bleeding during erection or sexual activity. Treatment depends on symptoms and may include observation or a minor surgical procedure.

\medterm{Frequency} Increased daytime micturition, typically more than seven episodes per day, distinguished
from polyuria (high urine volume) and nocturia (nighttime voiding). Common causes
include urinary tract infection, benign prostatic hyperplasia, overactive bladder, and
diabetes mellitus.

\medterm{Frey’s syndrome} Aberrant regeneration of parasympathetic nerve fibers to sweat glands after injury to
the auriculotemporal nerve, most commonly following parotid surgery or trauma. It
results in gustatory sweating and facial flushing while eating. Diagnosis is confirmed
by the starch-iodine test.

\medterm{Friedman Curve} The classic labor curve described by Emanuel Friedman in the 1950s, plotting cervical
dilatation against time. Defined the latent and active phases, with active phase
beginning at 4 cm. The Friedman curve was the standard for labor management for decades.

\medterm{Friedreich Ataxia} An inherited neurodegenerative disorder causing progressive gait and limb ataxia, sensory loss,
weakness, and sometimes cardiomyopathy or diabetes.

\medterm{Frontal Bone} Forms the forehead and superior orbits, with the squama frontalis, orbital plates, and
nasal part. Contains frontal sinuses draining into the nasal cavity via the frontonasal
duct. Articulates with parietal bones at the coronal suture. The metopic suture fuses by
age six.

\medterm{Frontal Lobe} The largest lobe of the cerebral cortex, lying anterior to the central sulcus,
comprising the primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus, Brodmann 4), premotor and
supplementary motor areas, prefrontal cortex, and Broca's area (dominant hemisphere,
expressive language).

\medterm{Frontal Sinus} The frontal sinuses are paired, air-filled cavities located within the frontal bone
above the orbits and the root of the nose, separated by a bony septum that may be
deviated to one side, and they are the last of the paranasal sinuses to develop,
typically appearing around age seven and reaching full size by late adolescence.

\medterm{Frontal Sinusitis} Inflammation or infection of the frontal sinuses, causing forehead pain or pressure, nasal congestion,
and drainage. Severe headache, swelling around the eye, high fever, confusion, or neurologic
symptoms may indicate a complication requiring urgent care.

\medterm{Frontotemporal Dementia} Dementia affecting frontal and temporal lobes. Behavioral variant (personality changes)
or language variant (aphasia). Younger onset (50-60).

\medterm{Frostbite} Frostbite is freezing injury to tissue, causing numbness, pallor, waxy skin, blistering, and possible tissue necrosis; rewarming and assessment are urgent.
degree (superficial, numbness, erythema), second degree (blisters, clear fluid, pain),
third degree (hemorrhagic blisters, necrosis, full-thickness skin loss), fourth degree
(deep tissue necrosis, mummification, autoamputation).

\medterm{Frotteuristic Disorder} A paraphilic disorder involving recurrent, intense sexual arousal from touching or
rubbing against a nonconsenting person, lasting \(\geq 6\) months and causing distress or
impairment or involving acting on these urges. Typically occurs in crowded public places
(subways, buses, concerts).

\medterm{Frozen Section} An intraoperative pathology consultation in which a fresh tissue specimen is rapidly
frozen, sectioned on a cryostat, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin within 10 to 20
minutes to guide immediate surgical decision-making. It is used to establish benign
versus malignant, assess margins, and identify lymph node metastases.

\medterm{Frozen Shoulder} Adhesive capsulitis: progressive shoulder pain and stiffness. Stages: freezing (pain),
frozen (stiffness), thawing (recovery). Associated with diabetes, thyroid disease,
immobilization. Treatment: physical therapy, corticosteroid injection, manipulation
under anesthesia, arthroscopic capsular release.

\medterm{Fructosamine} A measurement of glycated serum proteins, primarily albumin, that reflects average
glycemia over approximately two to three weeks. It is useful when HbA1c is unreliable
because of altered red-cell survival, but it is affected by changes in protein
concentration and turnover.

\medterm{Fructose Malabsorption} Poor absorption of fructose leading to GI symptoms. Fermentation by bacteria produces
gas, osmotic diarrhea. Low-fructose diet beneficial.

\medterm{Fructose Metabolism} The enzymatic conversion of fructose primarily in the liver through fructokinase,
aldolase B, and downstream glycolytic intermediates. Essential fructosuria is usually
benign, whereas aldolase B deficiency causes hereditary fructose intolerance and
hypoglycemia after fructose ingestion.

\medterm{Fuchs Dystrophy} A progressive corneal endothelial disorder in which endothelial cell loss impairs fluid
regulation and causes corneal oedema. Early disease may cause morning blur and glare;
advanced disease produces persistent oedema, bullae, pain, and reduced vision. Diagnosis
is clinical and supported by slit-lamp examination and corneal imaging.

\medterm{Full Agonist} A full agonist is a drug that binds to a receptor and produces the maximum possible
biological response that the receptor can generate, achieving 100 percent of the maximal
effect. Full agonists have both high affinity for the receptor and high intrinsic
activity, meaning they are very effective at activating the receptor once bound.

\medterm{Functional Asplenia} Loss of splenic function due to sickling and infarction in sickle cell disease,
typically by age 5 years. Increases risk of overwhelming bacterial infection,
particularly from encapsulated organisms (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus
influenzae).

\medterm{Functional Assessment} Evaluation of ability to perform daily activities. Tools: Barthel Index, Lawton IADL
Scale, Katz Index. Identifies needs for assistance, predicts outcomes, guides care
planning.

\medterm{Functional Capacity} The maximum level of physical, cognitive, communication, or self-care performance a
person can safely achieve under specified conditions. It is assessed in relation to
activities, participation, environment, and personal goals.

\medterm{Functional Dyspepsia} Chronic epigastric pain or discomfort without identifiable structural cause. The Rome IV
criteria include postprandial distress syndrome with early satiety and postprandial
fullness, and epigastric pain syndrome with epigastric pain or burning unrelated to
meals.

\medterm{Functional Electrical Stimulation} Application of electrical impulses to peripheral nerves or muscles to produce purposeful
contraction during a functional task, such as foot clearance during gait or cycling
after neurologic injury.

\medterm{Functional Independence Measure} FIM: standardized assessment of disability. Scores 18 (total assistance) to 7
(independent). 18 items: self-care, sphincter control, transfers, locomotion,
communication, social cognition.

\medterm{Functional MRI} An MRI method that estimates brain activity by detecting task-related changes in blood oxygenation and blood flow. It is used in research and in selected preoperative assessments.

\medterm{Functional Neurological Disorder} Neurologic symptoms such as weakness, abnormal movements, sensory loss, or seizures that
are incompatible with recognized patterns of structural neurologic disease and arise
from altered nervous-system function. Diagnosis is based on positive clinical signs, not
merely on normal investigations.

\medterm{Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder} A condition in which neurological symptoms such as weakness, abnormal movement, sensory
loss, or episodes resembling seizures are incompatible with recognized neurological
disease patterns and cause significant distress or impairment.

\medterm{Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder (Conversion Disorder)} A condition in which patients present with neurological symptoms (weakness, paralysis,
non-epileptic seizures, sensory loss, gait abnormalities, dystonia, speech difficulties)
that cannot be explained by structural neurological disease. The symptoms are not
intentionally produced (unlike malingering or factitious disorder).

\synonyms
Conversion Disorder

\medterm{Functional Residual Capacity} The volume of air remaining in the lungs at the end of a normal expiration, equal to the
sum of expiratory reserve volume plus residual volume (approximately 2.2-3 L in adults).
FRC represents the lung volume at which the outward recoil of the chest wall balances
the inward recoil of the lungs, the resting equilibrium position.

\medterm{Functional Screening} A brief assessment of a person's ability to perform daily activities, move safely, communicate, and manage basic self-care.

\medterm{Functional Status} Ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs) and instrumental ADLs (IADLs).
ADLs: bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, feeding. IADLs: shopping,
cooking, managing medications, finances, transportation, housekeeping. Decline indicates
need for assistance.

\medterm{Functional Urinary Incontinence} Urinary incontinence due to physical or cognitive impairment preventing toilet access.

\medterm{Fundal Height} The distance from the symphysis pubis to the top of the uterus measured in centimeters.
Used to estimate gestational age and fetal growth. Generally correlates with weeks of
gestation between 20-36 weeks (e.g., 24 cm at 24 weeks). Discrepancies may suggest fetal
growth restriction, macrosomia, polyhydramnios, or multiple gestation.

\medterm{Fundus Photography} The documentation of the appearance of the retina, optic nerve, and retinal vasculature
using a specialized camera that illuminates and photographs the posterior segment of the
eye, providing a permanent, objective record of retinal findings that can be used for
comparison over time, patient education, and telemedicine consultations.

\medterm{Fungal Arthritis} Infection and inflammation of a joint caused by a fungus, usually after surgery, trauma, injection, bloodstream spread,
or in a person with impaired immunity. It often develops gradually with persistent pain, swelling, and reduced
movement. Joint-fluid culture and tissue testing guide prolonged antifungal and sometimes surgical treatment.

\medterm{Fungal endophthalmitis} Fungal infection of the interior of the eye, often associated with bloodstream infection, surgery,
trauma, or immunosuppression and capable of causing severe visual loss.

\medterm{Fungal Infection} An infection caused by a fungus. It may be superficial, affecting the skin, nails, or mucous membranes,
or invasive, affecting the lungs, bloodstream, or other organs. The presentation and risk
depend on the organism and the person’s immune status.

\medterm{Fungal Infection (Tinea/Dermatophytosis)} Superficial fungal infections of skin, hair, or nails caused by dermatophytes
(Trichophyton, Microsporum, Epidermophyton). Tinea corporis (ringworm): annular scaling
plaque with central clearing. Tinea pedis (athlete's foot): interdigital scaling,
maceration. Tinea cruris (jock itch): groin rash sparing scrotum.

\synonyms
Tinea/Dermatophytosis

\medterm{Fungal Nail Infection} Infection of a fingernail or toenail by dermatophytes or other fungi, causing
thickening, discoloration, brittleness, separation from the nail bed, or subungual
debris.

\medterm{Fungal Nails} A fungal infection of the nail, usually caused by dermatophytes, producing thickened, discolored, brittle, or crumbly nails.
The infection may spread to surrounding skin and recur. Diagnosis may require microscopy or culture, and treatment
depends on the organism, number of nails, severity, and the person’s medical history.

\medterm{Fungal pathogenesis} The mechanisms by which fungi cause disease. Fungal pathogenesis involves adhesion, invasion, and evasion of host defences. Adhesins mediate attachment to host cells. Invasive hyphae penetrate tissues. Capsules and melanin protect against immune responses.

\medterm{Fungal resistance mechanisms} Mechanisms by which fungi develop resistance to antifungal agents. Azole resistance
occurs through upregulation of efflux pumps (CDR1, CDR2, MDR1), mutations in the target
enzyme ERG11, and overexpression of ERG11. Echinocandin resistance is primarily due to
mutations in the FKS1 and FKS2 genes encoding beta-(1,3)-D-glucan synthase.

\medterm{Fungal Skin Infections} Skin infections caused by dermatophytes or yeasts, including ringworm (tinea), athlete's
foot, and candidiasis. These infections thrive in warm, moist environments and present
with red, itchy, scaly, or ring-shaped lesions. Treatment typically involves topical or
oral antifungal medications and measures to keep the skin clean and dry.

\medterm{Fungemia} The presence of viable fungi in the bloodstream. Candida species are the most common
cause in hospitalized patients; fungemia requires prompt evaluation for a source,
metastatic infection, and appropriate antifungal treatment.

\medterm{Fungus} A eukaryotic microorganism with a chitin cell wall and ergosterol-containing membrane,
classified as yeasts, moulds, or dimorphic fungi. Reproducing by spores, fungi cause
superficial, mucosal, subcutaneous, and systemic infections, especially in
immunocompromised hosts.

\medterm{Furuncle} A painful, pus-filled infection of a hair follicle, usually caused by Staphylococcus aureus. It may form an
erythematous nodule with a central necrotic core or progress to a skin abscess. Treatment
depends on size, location, recurrence, and systemic illness and may include drainage or
antimicrobial therapy.

\medterm{Furuncle and Carbuncle} Furuncle (boil) is a deep infection of the hair follicle caused by Staphylococcus
aureus, presenting as a painful, erythematous nodule that may drain pus. Carbuncle is a
cluster of interconnected furuncles causing a larger, more deeply invasive infection.

\medterm{Furuncular myiasis} A cutaneous infestation caused by larvae of certain fly species (such as Dermatobia
hominis or Cuterebra) that develop within boil-like furuncular lesions in the skin,
presenting with a painful, erythematous nodule with a central punctum through which the
larva breathes.

\medterm{Furunculosis} Recurrent or multiple furuncles, which are deep infections of hair follicles and
surrounding tissue, usually caused by Staphylococcus aureus. Lesions are painful,
erythematous, and purulent and may occur with colonization, close-contact transmission,
skin disease, diabetes, immune dysfunction, or other risk factors.

\medterm{Fusariosis} An opportunistic invasive fungal infection caused by Fusarium species, affecting
predominantly immunocompromised patients, particularly those with prolonged neutropenia
or hematological malignancies. It causes disseminated infection with skin lesions,
bloodstream infection, and involvement of multiple organs.

\medterm{Fusion} Union of two tooth buds. Results in large tooth with reduced tooth count.

\medterm{Fabry's Disease} An inherited disorder in which a lipid accumulates in cells, causing pain, skin findings, kidney disease, and heart or nervous-system complications.

\medterm{Facial Nerve} The seventh cranial nerve, which controls facial expression and carries taste sensation from part of the tongue.

\medterm{Factor V} A blood-clotting protein required for formation of thrombin and fibrin.

\medterm{False Positive} A test result that indicates a disease or condition is present when it is not.

\medterm{Familial Dysautonomia} An inherited disorder that impairs development and function of sensory and autonomic nerves.

\medterm{Fatty Liver} Excess accumulation of fat in liver cells.

\medterm{Femoral Neuropathy} Damage to the femoral nerve, causing weakness or sensory loss in the thigh and leg.

\medterm{Filarial Elephantiasis} Severe swelling and thickening of tissues caused by lymphatic obstruction from filarial infection.

\medterm{Flat Affect} Markedly reduced outward expression of emotion.

\medterm{Focal Glomerulosclerosis} Scarring affecting some glomeruli or only parts of affected glomeruli, which can impair kidney filtration.

\medterm{Foodborne Disease} Illness caused by eating food or drinking water contaminated with infectious organisms, toxins, or harmful chemicals.

\medterm{Frasier Syndrome} A rare genetic disorder associated with abnormal sexual development, kidney disease, and increased risk of gonadal tumors.

\medterm{Friedreich's Ataxia} An inherited degenerative disorder causing progressive problems with coordination, sensation, and heart function.

\medterm{Fuchs' Dystrophy} A progressive disorder in which corneal endothelial cells deteriorate, causing corneal swelling and impaired vision.

\medterm{Fucosidosis} A rare inherited metabolic disorder caused by deficiency of the enzyme alpha-L-fucosidase.

\medterm{Funisitis} Inflammation of the umbilical cord, usually associated with infection or an inflammatory response.

\medterm{Fowler Position} A semiupright position in which the head and upper body are elevated, usually about 45 to 60 degrees.

\medterm{Facet Rhizotomy} A procedure that interrupts selected sensory nerves supplying spinal facet joints to reduce chronic pain.
