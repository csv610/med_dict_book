Acute Uric Acid Nephropathy; Crystal-Induced Urate Nephropathy

\medterm{Urate-Lowering Therapy} Long-term treatment that lowers urate in the blood, helping dissolve gout crystals and prevent further attacks and joint damage. The medicine is chosen according to kidney function, other illnesses, and treatment goals.

\medterm{Urates} Salts or ions of uric acid. Monosodium urate crystals can accumulate in joints in gout, and urate crystals may contribute to urinary stones.

\medterm{Urea} The principal nitrogen-containing waste product of protein metabolism, formed in the liver and excreted mainly by the kidneys.

\medterm{Urea Breath Test} A noninvasive test for active Helicobacter pylori infection: the person drinks labeled urea, and labeled carbon dioxide in exhaled breath indicates bacterial urease activity.

\medterm{Urea Cycle} A series of chemical reactions in the liver that changes toxic ammonia, produced when the body breaks down protein, into urea. The kidneys then remove urea in urine.

\medterm{Urea Cycle Disorder} An inherited problem in the liver’s process for removing ammonia. Ammonia can build up in the blood and cause vomiting, poor feeding, sleepiness, confusion, seizures, or brain injury, especially during illness or fasting.

\medterm{Urea Nitrogen Urine Test} A urine test measuring urea nitrogen excretion, interpreted with blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, diet, and urine volume to assess protein metabolism and renal handling.

\medterm{Ureaplasma} A genus of very small bacteria without a cell wall that can colonize the genital or urinary tract and is sometimes associated with urethritis or pregnancy complications.

\medterm{Ureaplasma urealyticum} A bacterium without a cell wall that can live in the genital or urinary tract. It is sometimes associated with urethritis, pregnancy complications, or infection in vulnerable newborns.

\medterm{Uremia} A clinical syndrome caused by advanced kidney failure and accumulation of waste products and other biologically active
substances. Symptoms may include nausea, itching, fatigue, confusion, bleeding, or
pericarditis; laboratory values and symptoms must be interpreted together.

\medterm{Uremic Bleeding} A tendency to bleed caused by poorly functioning platelets in advanced kidney failure. It may cause easy bruising, nosebleeds, or prolonged bleeding; treatment depends on the severity and may include dialysis or medicines that improve platelet function.

\medterm{Uremic Encephalopathy} Brain dysfunction caused by the buildup of waste products in severe kidney failure. It may cause confusion, extreme sleepiness, muscle twitching, seizures, or coma. It is a medical emergency and is often treated by addressing the kidney failure, sometimes with dialysis.

\medterm{Uremic Fetor} A urine-like or ammonia-like odor on the breath in severe kidney failure, caused when oral bacteria break down urea in saliva.
\synonyms
Ammoniacal Breath; Uremic Breath Odor

\medterm{Uremic Frost} Rare white, powdery crystals of urea that appear on the skin when severe untreated kidney failure causes very high waste levels in the blood. It indicates advanced kidney failure.

\medterm{Uremic Pericarditis} Inflammation of the sac around the heart caused by severe kidney failure and buildup of waste products. It may cause sharp chest pain, a scratchy heart sound, or fluid around the heart; fluid can sometimes compress the heart and impair its pumping.

\medterm{Uremic Platelet Dysfunction} Reduced ability of platelets to stick together and stop bleeding because of advanced kidney failure. It can cause easy bruising, nosebleeds, heavy menstrual bleeding, or bleeding from the stomach or bowel and may improve when the kidney failure is treated.

\medterm{Uremic Pruritus} A refractory, distressing cutaneous itch affecting patients with advanced chronic kidney disease and end-stage renal disease on hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis, related to immune dysregulation, microinflammation, peripheral neuropathy, and opioid receptor imbalance.

\synonyms
Chronic Kidney Disease-Associated Pruritus; CKD-aP

\medterm{Uremic Stomatitis} Inflammation of the mouth caused by severe kidney failure and buildup of waste products. It may cause a bad taste, mouth soreness, white patches, ulcers, or bleeding gums and tongue.

\medterm{Ureter} One of two muscular tubes that carry urine from a kidney to the bladder. A stone or narrowing can block a ureter and cause severe side pain, swelling of the kidney, infection, or kidney damage.

\medterm{Ureter Duplex} A birth variation in which one kidney has two urine tubes instead of one. It may cause no problems but can lead to urine flowing backward, blockage, repeated infections, or poor drainage.

\medterm{Ureter Fissus} A birth difference in which a ureter is divided lengthwise or partly duplicated. It may cause no symptoms, but abnormal urine flow can lead to urine reflux, blockage, repeated infection, or poor kidney drainage.

\medterm{Ureter Obstruction} Alternate name; see \textit{Ureteral Obstruction}.

\medterm{Ureteral Calculi} Alternate name; see \textit{Ureteral Stones}.

\medterm{Ureteral Catheters} Thin tubes passed into a ureter to drain urine, inject contrast, measure pressure, or guide a procedure. Possible complications include infection, bleeding, a tear, movement of the tube, or blockage.

\medterm{Ureteral Injury During Gynecologic Surgery} Damage to a ureter during pelvic surgery, including cutting, tying off, heat injury, loss of blood supply, or blockage. It may cause flank pain, urine leakage, kidney swelling, fever, or delayed kidney injury.

\medterm{Ureteral Obstruction} Blockage of urine flow through a ureter, the tube from a kidney to the bladder. Stones, scar tissue, tumors, or pressure from nearby structures can cause it and may lead to kidney swelling, infection, or reduced kidney function.

\medterm{Ureteral Orifice} One of the two openings where the ureters enter the bladder and release urine. The angled passage through the bladder wall helps reduce backward flow of urine toward the kidneys.

\medterm{Ureteral Perforation} A tear or hole in the ureter, sometimes caused by a stone, trauma, surgery, or an endoscopic procedure. Urine may leak into surrounding tissues and cause pain, infection, or inflammation.

\medterm{Ureteral Polyp} Usually a benign fibroepithelial growth arising from the ureter. It may cause hematuria, flank pain, or obstruction and must be distinguished from urothelial malignancy when evaluated.

\medterm{Ureteral Reimplantation} Surgery that moves the lower end of a ureter to a new opening in the bladder. It may be used to correct urine flowing backward, blockage, injury, or an abnormal ureter entry.

\synonyms
Ureteroneocystostomy; Ureteral Neoimplantation

\medterm{Ureteral Retrograde Brush Biopsy} A small brush passed through a thin camera into the ureter to collect cells from its lining. A laboratory examines the cells for cancer or other abnormal changes.

\medterm{Ureteral Stenosis} Alternate name; see \textit{Ureteral Stricture}.

\medterm{Ureteral Stent} A thin tube placed inside the ureter to keep urine flowing from the kidney to the bladder when a stone, narrowing, tumor, or swelling blocks the passage. It can cause urinary urgency, discomfort, blood in the urine, infection, or mineral buildup and is usually temporary.

\medterm{Ureteral Stones} Stones in a ureter, the tube carrying urine from a kidney to the bladder. They may cause wave-like side or groin pain, blood in the urine, nausea, blockage, or infection.

\medterm{Ureteral Stricture} A scar-related narrowing of the ureter that can slow urine drainage and cause kidney swelling, side pain, infection, or reduced kidney function.

\medterm{Ureteric Bud} A small outgrowth in the developing embryo that grows into the tissue that will form a kidney. It develops into the ureter, renal pelvis, calyces, and collecting ducts.

\synonyms
Metanephric Diverticulum

\medterm{Ureteric Calculi} Alternate name; see \textit{Ureteral Stones}.

\medterm{Ureteric Colic} Severe, cramping pain caused by a stone blocking a ureter, the tube that carries urine from a kidney to the bladder. Pain often starts in the side or back and moves toward the lower abdomen or groin; blood in the urine, nausea, or vomiting may occur.
\synonyms
Renal Colic; Ureteral Calculus Pain

\medterm{Ureteric Perforation} Alternate name; see \textit{Ureteral Perforation}.

\medterm{Ureteritis} Inflammation of the ureter, usually related to infection, obstruction, stone passage,
instrumentation, or adjacent inflammatory disease. It may cause flank or abdominal pain,
urinary symptoms, hematuria, and fever.

\medterm{Ureterocele} A balloon-like dilation of the lower ureter where it enters the bladder, usually congenital; it can obstruct urine flow and cause infection, hydronephrosis, reflux, stones, or impaired renal function.

\medterm{Ureterolithiasis} Alternate name; see \textit{Ureteral Stones}.

\medterm{Ureteropelvic Junction Obstruction} A blockage where the kidney’s urine-collecting area joins the ureter. It may be present from birth or develop later and can cause kidney swelling, side pain, infections, stones, or loss of kidney function.

\medterm{Ureteroscope} A thin camera instrument passed through the urinary passage to examine the ureter and kidney. It can help remove stones, take tissue samples, or place a stent.

\medterm{Ureteroscopes} Alternate name; see \textit{Ureteroscope}.

\medterm{Ureteroscopy} Ureteroscopy uses a small endoscope passed through the urinary tract to inspect, biopsy, or treat ureteral or kidney stones and other lesions.

\medterm{Ureterostomy Procedure} Surgery that creates an opening from a ureter to the skin or another urinary conduit to divert urine when normal drainage to the bladder is impossible or must be bypassed.

\medterm{Urethra} The tube that carries urine from the bladder out of the body. In people with male reproductive anatomy it also carries semen; the shorter urethra in people with female reproductive anatomy makes bladder infections more likely.

\medterm{Urethra Disorder} A condition affecting the urethra, such as infection, inflammation, stricture, trauma, congenital abnormality, or tumor, that may cause pain, discharge, weak stream, or retention.

\medterm{Urethral Cancer} Alternate name; see \textit{Urethral Carcinoma}.

\medterm{Urethral Carcinoma} A rare cancer arising from the urethra. It may cause bleeding, a lump, discharge, pain, or difficulty urinating.

\medterm{Urethral Caruncle} A small, usually benign red growth at the opening of the urethra, most often seen after menopause. It may cause bleeding, tenderness, or pain during urination.
\synonyms
Benign Meatal Vascular Polyp; Estrogen-Deficient Urethral Lesion

\medterm{Urethral Catheter} A tube inserted through the urethra into the bladder to drain urine, obtain a specimen, instill medication, or monitor output. Urinary infection and urethral trauma are important risks.

\medterm{Urethral Discharge} Fluid or pus coming from the urethral opening. It may result from urethritis, including sexually transmitted infection, but can also follow irritation or injury and warrants appropriate medical assessment and testing.

\medterm{Urethral Diverticulum} A pocket beside the urethra that connects with the urine tube, often developing after infection or blockage of nearby glands. It may cause burning urination, urine dribbling after voiding, repeated infections, pain during sex, or a vaginal-wall lump.

\medterm{Urethral Fistula} An abnormal channel between the urethra and another epithelial surface, often the vagina or skin. It can cause continuous urine leakage, recurrent infection, and local irritation.

\medterm{Urethral Hypermobility} Excessive movement of the urethra when abdominal pressure rises, contributing to urine leakage during coughing, lifting, or exercise.

\medterm{Urethral Infection} Infection involving the urethra, commonly caused by bacteria, including sexually transmitted organisms.
Symptoms may include burning urination, discharge, frequency, or pelvic discomfort; testing and
appropriate treatment help prevent complications and transmission.

\medterm{Urethral Injury} Alternate name; see \textit{Urethral Trauma}.

\medterm{Urethral Meatus} The external opening of the urethra through which urine leaves the body. Its position may be abnormal in conditions such as hypospadias or epispadias.

\medterm{Urethral Obstruction} Partial or complete blockage of urine leaving the bladder, often from stricture, stone, prostate disease, tumor, or a congenital valve. Acute retention can damage the bladder or kidneys.

\medterm{Urethral Pressure Profile} A test using a small catheter to measure pressure along the urethra and assess how well it closes. It may help investigate certain types of urine leakage or difficulty emptying the bladder.

\medterm{Urethral Prolapse} Protrusion of the inner lining of the urethra through its opening. It may appear as a red ring of tissue and can cause bleeding, pain, or difficulty urinating; it occurs most often in young children and after menopause.
\synonyms
Urethral Mucosal Prolapse; Meatal Eversion

\medterm{Urethral Sling} A strip of synthetic material or the person’s own tissue placed beneath the urethra to support it and reduce urine leakage during coughing, lifting, or exercise. Possible complications include urgency, difficulty urinating, infection, or erosion.

\medterm{Urethral Sphincter} Muscles around the urine tube that help keep urine from leaking and relax when a person urinates. Nerve or muscle damage can weaken control and cause leakage or difficulty emptying the bladder.

\medterm{Urethral Stenosis} Alternate name; see \textit{Urethral Stricture}.

\medterm{Urethral Stent} A small tube placed inside the urethra to help keep a narrowed or blocked segment open and allow urine to pass. Migration, blockage, infection, pain, or tissue injury can occur.

\medterm{Urethral Stricture} A narrowing of the urethra, the tube that carries urine out of the body, usually caused by scar tissue. The narrowing can slow or block urine flow and may cause a weak or spraying stream, straining, pain with urination, dribbling, or incomplete bladder emptying. It can lead to repeated urinary infections, urinary retention, and, if severe or prolonged, damage to the bladder or kidneys. Causes include injury, infection, surgery, radiation, or previous use of a catheter or other instrument; sometimes no cause is found.

\medterm{Urethral Syndrome} Urinary frequency, urgency, dysuria, or discomfort without a clear bacterial urinary-tract infection; causes may include irritation, pelvic-floor dysfunction, or inflammation.

\medterm{Urethral Trauma} Injury to the urethra caused by a pelvic fracture, blunt or penetrating trauma, or medical instrumentation. It may cause bleeding at the urethral opening, difficulty passing urine, bruising, or leakage of urine into nearby tissues.

\medterm{Urethritis} Inflammation of the urethra, often caused by infection, including sexually transmitted infections, but sometimes by irritation or injury. It can cause burning urination, itching, pelvic discomfort, or discharge.

\medterm{Urethrocele} Prolapse of the urethra into the front wall of the vagina. It may cause a vaginal bulge, pelvic pressure, urine leakage, or difficulty emptying the bladder, but can also cause no symptoms.

\medterm{Urethrography} An X-ray test in which contrast liquid outlines the urethra, the tube carrying urine out of the body. It can show narrowing, leaks, injury, abnormal connections, or birth differences.

\medterm{Urethroplasty} Surgery that rebuilds or widens the urine tube, most often to treat a narrowing called a stricture. It may use nearby tissue or a graft and aims to improve urine flow.

\medterm{Urethroscopy} Examination of the urethra with a thin camera passed through its opening. It can identify narrowing, injury, inflammation, stones, tumors, or other abnormalities and may allow treatment during the same procedure.

\medterm{Urge Incontinence} Leakage of urine that happens with or just after a sudden, hard-to-delay need to urinate. It may occur with overactive bladder, infection, bladder irritation, nerve disease, or other conditions.
\synonyms
Detrusor Overactivity Incontinence; Urgency Urinary Incontinence

\medterm{Urge Urinary Incontinence} Alternate name; see \textit{Urge Incontinence}.

\medterm{Urgency} A sudden, difficult-to-delay need to urinate. It may result from a urinary infection, bladder irritation, overactive bladder, a stone, nerve disease, prostate enlargement, or pelvic-floor problems.

\medterm{URI (Upper Respiratory Infection)} URI usually means upper respiratory infection, commonly a viral infection of the nose, throat, or upper airways causing congestion, cough, and sore throat.

\medterm{Uric Acid} A waste product formed when the body breaks down purines. High blood levels can cause gout or kidney stones, although many people with high levels have no symptoms.

\medterm{Uric Acid Nephrolithiasis} Formation of stones made mainly of uric acid in the kidneys or urinary tract. They form more readily when urine is persistently acidic or concentrated and may not show on a plain X-ray, though they can be seen on CT.
\synonyms
Uric Acid Calculi; Radiolucent Kidney Stones

\medterm{Uric Acid Nephropathy} Kidney injury caused when too much uric acid forms crystals in the kidney’s small tubes. It can occur during rapid breakdown of cancer cells or severe uric-acid excess.

\medterm{Uric Acid Stones} Kidney stones composed primarily of uric acid. They form more readily in acidic urine and are
associated with hyperuricemia, gout, high purine intake, or chronic diarrhea. Some can dissolve when urine is alkalinized.

\medterm{Uric Acid Urine Test} A urine test measuring uric acid excretion, used in evaluation of gout, kidney stones, uric-acid overproduction, and selected metabolic disorders.

\medterm{Uricosuria} Excretion of uric acid in urine. The amount depends on uric-acid
production, diet, kidney handling, and medicines. Excess urinary uric acid, called
hyperuricosuria, can contribute to uric acid or calcium oxalate stones and may be assessed
with a timed urine collection.

\medterm{Uricosuric Drug} A medicine that increases renal excretion of uric acid, such as probenecid, used in selected patients with gout.

\medterm{Uridine Triacetate} An oral prodrug that supplies uridine and is used as an emergency antidote for severe or life-threatening toxicity from fluorouracil or related fluoropyrimidines, under specialist direction.

\medterm{Urinal} A portable container used to collect urine from someone who cannot easily reach a toilet. It should be emptied, cleaned, and stored safely to reduce spills, odor, and infection risk.

\medterm{Urinalysis} Laboratory examination of urine for its appearance, acidity, blood, cells, protein, sugar, ketones, bacteria, and other substances. It can provide clues to infection, kidney disease, bleeding, diabetes, dehydration, and other illness.

\medterm{Urinary} Relating to urine or to the organs and processes involved in making, storing, and passing urine, including the kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra.

\medterm{Urinary Anion Gap} A calculation using urine sodium, potassium, and chloride levels. It can help doctors assess how the kidneys are handling acid during some types of blood acidosis. The result is only one part of the assessment and cannot by itself identify the cause.

\synonyms
UAG; Urine Net Charge

\medterm{Urinary Bladder} A hollow muscular organ in the lower abdomen that stores urine until it is released through the urethra.

\medterm{Urinary Bladder Disorder} A disease affecting bladder storage or emptying, including infection, overactivity, pain syndrome, stones, tumor, neurologic dysfunction, or outlet obstruction.

\medterm{Urinary Bladder Squamous Cell Carcinoma Pathology} An invasive malignant squamous neoplasm of the bladder showing keratinization, intercellular bridges, or both. It may be associated with chronic irritation, infection, stones, or schistosomiasis and is assessed for depth of invasion.

\medterm{Urinary Calculi} Hard deposits formed when substances in urine crystallize and
aggregate in the kidneys, ureters, bladder, or urethra. Composition varies and may include
calcium salts, uric acid, struvite, or cystine. Some pass without symptoms; others can
cause renal colic, blood in urine, obstruction, infection, or kidney injury, depending on
their size and location.

\medterm{Urinary Casts} Small tube-shaped particles formed inside kidney tubules and passed in urine. Their appearance can provide clues about kidney inflammation, bleeding, infection, or damage.

\medterm{Urinary Catheter} A hollow, flexible tube inserted through the urethra into the bladder to drain urine; it may be used intermittently or left in place.

\medterm{Urinary Catheterization} Insertion of a tube into the bladder to drain urine, measure output, obtain a specimen, or deliver treatment; infection, trauma, and blockage are possible complications.

\medterm{Urinary Catheters} Urinary catheters drain the bladder when a person cannot empty it normally or accurate urine measurement is needed, with risks including infection, trauma, blockage, and discomfort.

\medterm{Urinary Diversion} Surgical or catheter-based rerouting of urine when the normal passage from the kidneys to the urethra cannot be used, often to a stoma or bowel reservoir.

\medterm{Urinary Fistula} An abnormal connection between part of the urinary tract and another organ or the skin, allowing urine to leak outside its normal pathway. Causes include surgery, childbirth injury, inflammation, and cancer.

\medterm{Urinary Frequency} The need to urinate more often than usual, which may result from infection, bladder irritation, increased urine production, or other urinary disorders.

\medterm{Urinary Hesitancy} Alternate name; see \textit{Urinary Hesitation}.

\medterm{Urinary Hesitation} A delay or difficulty starting urination after a person is
ready to void. Causes include prostate enlargement, urethral narrowing, medicines, pain,
nerve disorders, or weak bladder contraction. Hesitancy is a symptom rather than a
diagnosis; accompanying stream changes or incomplete emptying help characterize it.

\medterm{Urinary Incontinence} Unwanted leakage of urine. It may happen with coughing or exercise, a sudden strong urge, an overfull bladder, difficulty reaching the toilet, or nerve and muscle problems. More than one pattern may occur in the same person.

\synonyms
Involuntary Urination; Loss of Bladder Control

\medterm{Urinary Lithiasis} Alternate name; see \textit{Urolithiasis}.

\medterm{Urinary Retention} Inability to empty the bladder fully. It may be sudden and painful or develop gradually with a weak stream, frequent small amounts of urine, or leakage from an overfull bladder. Blockage, medicines, nerve problems, or weak bladder muscles can cause it.

\medterm{Urinary Retraining} Scheduled toilet visits and bladder-control techniques used to reduce urgency, frequent urination, or leakage. A clinician may combine it with pelvic-floor exercises, fluid changes, or treatment of the underlying cause.

\medterm{Urinary Stasis} Slowing or stopping of normal urine flow, which can promote urinary infection, stone formation, or obstruction-related kidney injury.

\medterm{Urinary System} The kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra. Together they filter blood, make and transport urine, store and remove it, and help regulate fluid volume, electrolytes, acid-base balance, blood pressure, and hormone-related functions such as red-cell production.

\medterm{Urinary Tract} The kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra. Together these organs make urine, carry it to the bladder, store it, and remove it from the body.

\medterm{Urinary Tract Disorder} Any disease of the kidneys, ureters, bladder, or urethra, including infection, stones, obstruction, congenital abnormalities, cancer, and functional or neurologic disorders.

\medterm{Urinary Tract Imaging} Imaging tests used to examine the kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra. Methods include ultrasound, X-ray studies, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and nuclear scans, chosen according to the suspected problem.

\medterm{Urinary Tract Infection} Infection of the urinary tract, classified as lower infection such as cystitis or urethritis,
or upper infection such as pyelonephritis. Symptoms vary; dysuria, frequency, urgency, fever,
flank pain, or nausea may occur. Escherichia coli is a common cause, but organisms vary by
setting and patient factors.

\medterm{Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)} Alternate name; see \textit{Urinary Tract Infection}.

\medterm{Urinary Tract Infection In Adults} A urinary infection in an adult involving the bladder, urethra, kidneys, or another part of the urinary tract. Symptoms vary by site and may include painful urination, frequency, urgency, blood in the urine, fever, or flank pain.

\medterm{Urinary Tract Infection In Children} A urinary infection in a child involving the bladder, kidneys, ureters, or urethra. Symptoms may include fever, painful or frequent urination, new wetting, abdominal or back pain, vomiting, or unusual irritability.

\synonyms
Pediatric UTI

\medterm{Urinary Tract Infection In Pregnancy} Infection of the urinary tract during pregnancy, ranging from asymptomatic bacteriuria to kidney infection. Pregnancy increases the risk of complications, so screening and treatment follow pregnancy-specific clinical guidance.

\medterm{Urinary Tract Infections in Pregnancy} Alternate name; see \textit{Urinary Tract Infection In Pregnancy}.

\medterm{Urinary Tract Neoplasm} A benign or malignant growth in the kidneys, ureters, bladder, or urethra; evaluation depends on its site, symptoms, tissue type, and extent of spread.

\medterm{Urinary Tract Obstruction} A blockage that slows or stops urine flow anywhere from the kidneys to the urethral opening. It can cause urine to back up, kidney injury, or infection.

\medterm{Urinary Urgency} A sudden, difficult-to-delay need to urinate. It may occur with urinary infection, bladder irritation, overactive bladder, stones, neurologic disease, or other conditions.

\medterm{Urination} The process of releasing urine from the bladder through the urethra and out of the body.

\synonyms
Micturition; Voiding

\medterm{Urination Disorder} Abnormal storage or emptying of urine causing frequency, urgency, dysuria, incontinence, hesitancy, weak stream, retention, or excessive or reduced urine output.

\medterm{Urine} The liquid waste produced by the kidneys, containing water and dissolved substances eliminated from the blood.

\medterm{Urine 24-Hour Volume} Twenty-four-hour urine volume is the total urine produced in a day and helps assess hydration, kidney function, endocrine disease, or abnormal urine production.

\medterm{Urine Albuminuria} Alternate name; see \textit{Albuminuria}.

\medterm{Urine Alkalinization} Treatment that makes urine less acidic, usually with a carefully monitored medicine, to help remove certain poisons such as salicylate. It can disturb body salts or fluid balance and is used only for selected cases.

\medterm{Urine Amylase Test} A urine test that measures the amount of amylase passed from the body. It may help assess pancreatitis, salivary-gland problems, or unusually high amylase levels in the blood.

\medterm{Urine Analysis} Alternate name; see \textit{Urinalysis}.

\medterm{Urine Bilirubin Test} A clinical urinalysis that detects conjugated (water-soluble) bilirubin in urine. A positive result indicates hepatobiliary disease, hepatocellular injury, or biliary tract obstruction; unconjugated bilirubin is bound to albumin and does not filter into urine.

\synonyms
Urine Bilirubin; Urinary Bilirubin Test

\medterm{Urine Calcium Test} A diagnostic urinalysis quantifying urinary calcium excretion, typically measured in a 24-hour urine collection. It is utilized in the evaluation and metabolic workup of nephrolithiasis, hyperparathyroidism, osteomalacia, and idiopathic hypercalciuria.

\synonyms
Urinary Calcium; 24-Hour Urine Calcium; Urine Calcium Excretion

\medterm{Urine Catecholamine Test} A urine test that measures catecholamines or their breakdown products. It may help assess certain hormone-producing tumors, such as pheochromocytoma or paraganglioma; tests of metanephrines are commonly preferred for these tumors.

\synonyms
24-Hour Urine Catecholamines; Urinary Catecholamines; Fractionated Urinary Catecholamines

\medterm{Urine Chemistry} Measurement of chemical constituents in urine, such as glucose, protein, electrolytes, ketones, blood, and pH, to evaluate kidney function and systemic disease.

\medterm{Urine Chloride Test} A diagnostic urinalysis quantifying urinary chloride excretion, crucial in differentiating chloride-responsive (e.g., vomiting, diuretic use) from chloride-resistant (e.g., hyperaldosteronism) metabolic alkalosis.

\synonyms
Urinary Chloride; Urine Chloride Excretion; Spot Urine Chloride
\medterm{Urine Concentration Test} A test of the kidney’s ability to concentrate urine, assessed by urine osmolality or specific gravity after controlled fluid conditions when appropriate.

\medterm{Urine Conductivity} Urine conductivity measures how readily urine carries electrical current and reflects its dissolved electrolyte and solute concentration.

\medterm{Urine Culture} A laboratory test that grows microorganisms from a urine specimen to identify urinary-tract infection and guide antimicrobial selection. Results may report the organism, quantity of growth, and antimicrobial susceptibility. Proper collection is important because contamination can produce misleading growth; results must be interpreted with symptoms and urinalysis.

\medterm{Urine Cytology} Microscopic examination of cells shed into urine, mainly used to help detect high-grade urothelial carcinoma and some other abnormalities. A negative result does not exclude all urinary-tract cancers.

\medterm{Urine Dipstick} A strip placed in urine that changes color to screen for findings such as blood, protein, sugar, ketones, and signs of infection. Abnormal results may need confirmation with other tests.

\medterm{Urine Drainage Bags} Urine drainage bags collect urine from a catheter or urinary diversion and require correct positioning, emptying, hygiene, and monitoring for blockage or infection.

\medterm{Urine Electrolytes Test} A diagnostic urinalysis measuring the concentrations of sodium, potassium, and chloride in urine to evaluate acute kidney injury, dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, and acid-base disturbances.

\synonyms
Urinary Electrolytes; Spot Urine Electrolytes; 24-Hour Urine Electrolytes

\medterm{Urine Formation} The process by which the kidneys make urine: they filter blood, return needed water and substances to the body, and add selected wastes and extra ions to the fluid that leaves as urine.

\medterm{Urine HCG Test} A urine test that detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone produced during pregnancy. Testing too early or using diluted urine can give a false-negative result; a positive result confirms pregnancy but does not show its location or health.

\synonyms
Urine hCG Test

\medterm{Urine Hemoglobin} Hemoglobin detected in urine, which may reflect intravascular hemolysis or contamination with intact red blood cells. Interpretation requires comparison with urine microscopy and clinical findings.

\medterm{Urine Immunoelectrophoresis} A laboratory test that separates proteins in urine and uses antibodies to identify abnormal proteins, especially monoclonal light chains that can occur in multiple myeloma.

\synonyms
Urine IEP; Urinary Protein Immunoelectrophoresis

\medterm{Urine Immunofixation} A laboratory test that uses electrophoresis and antibodies to identify abnormal proteins in urine, especially monoclonal light chains or immunoglobulins.

\synonyms
Urine IFE; Urinary Immunofixation Electrophoresis; Bence Jones Protein IFE

\medterm{Urine Osmolality} A measure of how concentrated urine is. It helps assess whether the kidneys can conserve or remove water and must be interpreted with fluid intake, medicines, kidney function, and blood tests.

\medterm{Urine pH} A measurement of urine acidity or alkalinity. It varies with diet, metabolism, medicines, infection, and kidney function and can influence the formation of some urinary stones.

\medterm{Urine PH Test} Alternate name; see \textit{Urine pH}.

\medterm{Urine Protein Dipstick Test} A screening test using a reagent strip to detect mainly albumin in urine; positive results require confirmation and quantitative assessment when clinically indicated.

\medterm{Urine Protein Electrophoresis Test} A laboratory test that separates urine proteins by electrical charge and size to help detect abnormal proteins, including monoclonal light chains associated with plasma-cell disorders.

\medterm{Urine Retention} Alternate name; see \textit{Urinary Retention}.

\medterm{Urine Sediment} The cells, crystals, casts, and other particles seen under a microscope after urine is concentrated. Their appearance can provide clues to bleeding, infection, stones, or kidney inflammation.

\medterm{Urine Sodium} The amount of sodium in urine. It can help assess fluid balance and kidney handling of salt, but results are strongly affected by diet, medicines, hydration, and the timing of the sample.

\medterm{Urine Specific Gravity Test} A test comparing the density of urine with the density of water. It gives a rough estimate of how concentrated the urine is and can help assess hydration and the kidneys’ ability to conserve water.

\medterm{Urine Toxicology} Laboratory analysis of urine for medicines, drugs, or their metabolites. Results require interpretation because detection does not
always establish impairment or the timing of exposure.

\medterm{Urinoma} A collection of urine that has leaked into tissues around the kidney or urinary tract, usually because of an injury, blockage, or medical procedure. It may cause pain, swelling, or infection and may need drainage or repair.

\synonyms
Urine Pseudocyst

\medterm{Urinometer} An instrument that measures how dense urine is compared with water. The result gives an estimate of urine concentration and may help assess hydration or the kidney’s ability to conserve water.

\medterm{Urobilinogen} A colourless product formed in the intestine from bilirubin and partly reabsorbed and excreted in urine; abnormal levels may provide clues to liver disease or haemolysis.

\medterm{Urodynamic Testing} Alternate name; see \textit{Urodynamics}.

\medterm{Urodynamics} A group of tests that measures how the bladder stores urine, how pressure changes as it fills, and how the urethra and bladder release urine. It helps investigate leakage, urgency, weak stream, or difficulty emptying when simpler tests are inconclusive.

\medterm{Uroflow} A test that measures how quickly and steadily urine leaves the body during urination. The result can suggest blockage, weak bladder muscles, or other emptying problems, but it is interpreted with symptoms and sometimes additional tests.

\medterm{Uroflowmetry} A noninvasive test measuring how fast and steadily urine leaves the body. A low or abnormal flow may reflect blockage, weak bladder contraction, or an incomplete test and must be interpreted with symptoms and other findings.

\medterm{Urogenital} Relating to the urinary and reproductive systems or structures involving both. The term may describe organs, infections, cancers, birth conditions, injuries, symptoms, tests, or treatments affecting these closely connected body systems.

\medterm{Urogenital Fistula} An abnormal connection between the urinary and genital tracts, such as a vesicovaginal or ureterovaginal fistula. It commonly causes continuous urinary leakage and requires specialist evaluation.

\medterm{Urogenital Sinus} A birth-related abnormality in which the urethra and vagina join into a common channel before reaching the outside of the body. It may occur with differences in the external genitalia, urinary tract, or reproductive organs.

\medterm{Urogenital System} The related urinary and reproductive organs and their associated ducts, vessels, and supporting tissues. The systems share developmental origins and anatomic relationships.

\medterm{Urogenital Tract} The combined urinary and reproductive organs and passages. These include kidneys, urine tubes, bladder, urethra, reproductive organs, and connected structures that develop partly from related tissues before birth.

\medterm{Urography} Imaging of the urinary tract, traditionally using X-rays after contrast dye is given. It can show the kidneys, urine tubes, bladder, narrowing, stones, leaks, blockage, or other structural problems.

\medterm{Urokinase} A plasminogen activator produced by the body and also used as a thrombolytic medicine to help dissolve blood clots.

\medterm{Urolithiasis} Formation or presence of stones in the urinary tract, including the kidneys, ureters, bladder, or urethra. Stones may contain calcium salts, uric acid, struvite, or cystine.

\medterm{Urologic Diagnostic Yield} The proportion of urinary-system tests or procedures that provide a clear, useful diagnosis. A test may have a low yield when the sample is too small, the disease is difficult to detect, or the result does not change care.

\medterm{Urologist} A doctor who specializes in the urinary system and, in many practices, the male reproductive system. Urologists diagnose and treat kidney stones, infections, urine leakage, prostate problems, cancers, infertility, and conditions requiring procedures or surgery.

\medterm{Urology} The medical specialty that diagnoses and treats diseases of the kidneys, urine tubes, bladder, and urethra, as well as the male reproductive organs. Treatment may include medicines, procedures, or surgery.

\medterm{Uropathy} Disease or damage affecting the kidneys or urinary tract. Blockage, infection, stones, urine flowing backward, medicines, and other conditions can cause it and may reduce kidney function.

\medterm{Urosepsis} A life-threatening whole-body response to a severe urinary infection. It may start in the bladder or kidney and cause fever or low temperature, confusion, low blood pressure, reduced urine, organ failure, or shock.

\medterm{Urostomy} A surgically created opening that diverts urine from the urinary tract through the abdominal wall into an external pouch or appliance. It may be formed with a segment of intestine after bladder removal or when normal urinary drainage is not possible.

\medterm{Urostomy Leak} Escape of urine beneath or around a urostomy appliance or from a surgical connection. Persistent leakage can irritate the skin and may signal poor appliance fit, obstruction, infection, or a surgical complication.

\medterm{Urostomy Procedure} Surgery that diverts urine through a stoma into an external pouch or conduit, commonly after bladder removal or when the lower urinary tract cannot safely transport urine.

\medterm{Urostomy Prolapse} Protrusion of a segment of bowel used as a urostomy stoma beyond its usual level. It may cause bleeding, swelling, appliance problems, or impaired urine drainage and should be assessed clinically.

\medterm{Urostomy Stenosis} Narrowing of a urinary stoma or conduit that impedes drainage, causing reduced urine output, leakage, infection, or kidney-pressure changes and sometimes requiring revision.

\medterm{Urothelial Carcinoma} A cancer arising from the stretchable lining of the urinary tract, most often the bladder but also the kidney’s drainage area, ureters, or urethra. Blood in the urine is common, and treatment depends on location, depth, grade, and spread.

\medterm{Urothelium} A specialized stretchable lining of the renal pelvis, ureters, bladder, and part of the urethra. It forms a protective barrier between urine and underlying tissues and allows the bladder to expand.

\medterm{Urotrauma} Injury to the urinary or genital organs, including the kidneys, ureters, bladder, urethra, or external genitalia. It may result from blunt force, penetrating wounds, fractures, surgery, or medical instruments.

\medterm{Ursodeoxycholic Acid} A hydrophilic bile acid used for primary biliary cholangitis and selected cholestatic liver diseases; it may also dissolve certain small cholesterol gallstones when bile flow is preserved.

\medterm{Urticaria} A skin reaction causing transient, itchy raised welts that change location and usually resolve within 24 hours.
It may occur with angioedema, which is deeper swelling often affecting the lips, eyelids,
tongue, or extremities.

\medterm{Urticaria Multiforme} A short-lived allergic-type rash in children that causes widespread, ring-shaped or target-like raised patches, often with swelling of the hands, feet, or face. It may follow an infection or medicine and usually fades without scarring.

\medterm{Urticaria Pigmentosa} An older name for maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis, a condition in which mast cells collect in the skin and form tan or brown spots or bumps that may swell and itch when rubbed. Some people also have symptoms from mast-cell chemicals released elsewhere in the body.

\medterm{Urticarial Vasculitis} Inflammation of small blood vessels causing hive-like patches that usually last longer than 24 hours and may burn, bruise, or leave dark marks. It can be limited to the skin or linked with joint, kidney, lung, or other disease.

\medterm{Usher Syndrome} An inherited condition causing hearing loss together with gradually worsening vision from retinal disease. The age and severity vary by type; hearing, vision, balance, communication, and genetic support may all be needed.

\medterm{Ustekinumab} A biologic medicine that blocks immune signals involved in inflammation. It is used for psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, Crohn disease, ulcerative colitis, and other approved conditions; infections and allergic reactions are important risks.

\medterm{Usual Ductal Hyperplasia} A benign increase in the number of cells lining the breast ducts. The cells look close to normal, unlike the abnormal cells in atypical ductal hyperplasia; it is associated with a slight increase in breast cancer risk.

\medterm{Usual Interstitial Pneumonia} A pattern of permanent scarring in the lungs, often seen in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Scarring is usually greatest near the outer and lower parts of the lungs and can form small spaces called honeycombing. It causes increasing breathlessness and cough and is assessed with lung tests and CT imaging.

\medterm{Usual Interstitial Pneumonia Pathology} A patchy, temporally heterogeneous interstitial fibrosing pattern with subpleural or paraseptal fibrosis, fibroblastic foci, architectural distortion, and honeycomb change. Clinical and radiologic correlation is required.

\medterm{Ut Dict.} An abbreviation of the Latin phrase “ut dictum,” meaning “as stated” or “as directed.” In medical notes it refers the reader to information already given or to an instruction previously specified.

\medterm{Uterine Adnexae} The structures beside and connected to the uterus, mainly the fallopian tubes, ovaries, and supporting tissues. Problems in these structures can cause pelvic pain, abnormal bleeding, infertility, or an ectopic pregnancy.

\medterm{Uterine Artery Embolization} A minimally invasive procedure that blocks blood flow to uterine fibroids so they shrink. It can reduce heavy bleeding and pelvic pressure, but may cause pain, infection, or effects on fertility and future pregnancy.

\medterm{Uterine Atony} Failure of the uterus to contract effectively after childbirth, causing heavy postpartum bleeding. It is the most common cause of postpartum hemorrhage and may be managed with medicines, uterine massage, procedures, or surgery, depending on severity and response.

\synonyms
Postpartum Uterine Atony

\medterm{Uterine Balloon Tamponade} A treatment for severe bleeding after childbirth when medicines do not control bleeding. A soft balloon is placed inside the uterus and filled with sterile fluid to press against the uterine wall and slow or stop blood loss while other treatment is arranged.

\medterm{Uterine Bleeding} Bleeding from the uterus outside the expected menstrual pattern or in an abnormal amount. Causes include pregnancy complications, fibroids, polyps, hormonal changes, medicines, infection, and cancer; heavy or postmenopausal bleeding can have an underlying medical cause.

\medterm{Uterine Cancer} Cancer arising in the uterus, most commonly from the endometrium. It may cause abnormal vaginal bleeding, bleeding after menopause, pelvic pain, or unusual discharge.

\medterm{Uterine Cavity} The hollow space inside the uterus, lined by the endometrium. A fertilized egg normally implants in this lining, which thickens during the menstrual cycle and supports pregnancy; polyps, fibroids, scar tissue, or abnormal shape may affect it.

\medterm{Uterine Compression Suture} A stitch placed around the uterus to press its walls together and control severe bleeding after childbirth when the uterus does not contract well. The B-Lynch suture is one example; other compression-suture techniques are also used.

\synonyms
Uterine Bracing Suture

\medterm{Uterine Contraction} Tightening of the uterine muscle. Contractions occur during menstruation and labor and may also occur at other times, including as irregular practice contractions in pregnancy. Their timing, strength, and pattern vary with the cause.

\medterm{Uterine Diseases} Conditions affecting the womb, including fibroids, adenomyosis, endometriosis, infection, unusual bleeding, prolapse, and cancer. Symptoms may include pain, heavy periods, infertility, pressure, discharge, or bleeding after menopause.

\medterm{Uterine Fibroid} Alternate name; see \textit{Uterine Fibroids}.

\medterm{Uterine Fibroid Embolization} Alternate name; see \textit{Uterine Artery Embolization}.

\medterm{Uterine Fibroids} Noncancerous growths made of muscle and other tissue that develop in or on the uterus. Many cause no symptoms. Depending on their size and location, they may cause heavy or painful periods, pelvic pressure, frequent urination, constipation, or problems with fertility or pregnancy. They are also called leiomyomas or myomas.

\synonyms
Uterine Fibroid; Uterine Leiomyoma; Uterine Leiomyomas; Uterine Leiomyomata; Uterine Myoma; Uterine Myomas; Fibroid; Fibroids; Fibroid Tumor; Fibromyoma; Fibromyomas

\medterm{Uterine Fistula} A uterine fistula is an abnormal passage connecting the uterus with another organ or the skin, often after surgery, childbirth injury, infection, or cancer.

\medterm{Uterine Fornix} One of the shallow spaces at the top of the vagina surrounding the cervix. A clinician can examine these spaces during a pelvic examination, and selected procedures may use them as an access route.

\medterm{Uterine Gland} A small gland in the lining of the uterus that produces fluid and helps support the early embryo. Its activity changes during the menstrual cycle and pregnancy.

\medterm{Uterine Growths} Uterine growths may include fibroids, polyps, adenomyosis, or rarely cancer. They can cause heavy or irregular bleeding, pelvic pressure, pain, infertility, or no symptoms; examination and imaging determine the type and treatment.

\medterm{Uterine Hemorrhage} Heavy or abnormal bleeding from the uterus, occurring during pregnancy, after childbirth, or at other
times. Causes include pregnancy complications, fibroids, hormonal disorders, and malignancy;
severe bleeding with dizziness or weakness is an emergency.

\medterm{Uterine Inversion} A rare, life-threatening complication of childbirth in which the uterus turns partly or completely inside out and may protrude into or beyond the vagina. It can cause severe bleeding, shock, and intense pain. Treatment restores the uterus and controls blood loss.

\medterm{Uterine Leiomyoma} Alternate name; see \textit{Uterine Fibroids}.

\synonyms
Fibroid

\medterm{Uterine Leiomyosarcoma} A rare malignant tumor of uterine smooth muscle that may cause abnormal bleeding, pelvic pain, or an enlarging mass; diagnosis is based on tissue examination and staging.

\medterm{Uterine Monitoring} Recording how often and how long the uterus contracts during labor. Contraction strength may be estimated by touch or measured with a pressure sensor inside the uterus; monitoring helps assess labor patterns and the response to labor medicines.

\medterm{Uterine Myomectomy} Surgical removal of uterine fibroids while preserving the uterus; it may be performed through hysteroscopy, laparoscopy, or open surgery depending on fibroid size and location.

\medterm{Uterine Obstruction} Blockage that prevents normal passage through the uterus or cervix, which may cause menstrual outflow obstruction, infertility, pregnancy complications, or retained blood or tissue.

\medterm{Uterine Perforation} A hole through the wall of the uterus, most often caused by an instrument used during a procedure such as hysteroscopy or removal of uterine tissue. It can cause internal bleeding or injury to nearby organs and may need repair.

\medterm{Uterine Polyp} A growth from the lining of the uterus that sticks into its inner space. Most are not cancer. They may cause irregular or heavy bleeding, bleeding after menopause, or difficulty becoming pregnant, but often cause no symptoms. Some contain precancerous or cancer cells.

\synonyms
Uterine Polyps; Endometrial Polyp; Endometrial Polyps; Intrauterine Polyp; Intrauterine Polyps

\medterm{Uterine Polyps} Alternate name; see \textit{Uterine Polyp}.

\medterm{Uterine Prolapse} Descent of the uterus toward or through the vaginal opening because pelvic-floor muscles and supporting tissues have weakened. It may cause pelvic pressure, a bulge, backache, urinary or bowel problems, or discomfort during sex; childbirth and aging increase risk.

\synonyms
Descensus Uteri; Uterine Herniation

\medterm{Uterine Rupture} A full-thickness tear of the uterine wall that occurs in
late pregnancy before labor or during labor. It most often involves a prior uterine
surgical scar, especially after cesarean delivery or myomectomy, but can occur in an
unscarred uterus. It may cause severe maternal hemorrhage and compromise fetal oxygen
supply. Unlike scar dehiscence, complete rupture extends through the outer uterine layer.

\medterm{Uterine Rupture in Pregnancy} Alternate name; see \textit{Uterine Rupture}.

\medterm{Uterine Sarcoma} Uterine sarcoma is a rare malignant tumor arising from uterine muscle, connective tissue, or related cells and may cause bleeding, pain, or an enlarging mass.

\medterm{Uterine Tachysystole} More than five contractions in 10 minutes, averaged over 30 minutes during labor. Very frequent contractions can reduce the baby’s oxygen supply, especially after labor medicines, and may require prompt treatment adjustment.

\medterm{Uterine Tube} A paired muscular tube that carries an egg from the ovary toward the uterus and is the usual site of fertilization.
\synonyms
Fallopian Tube; Oviduct

\medterm{Uterine Tumors} Abnormal growths in or on the uterus. They include noncancerous fibroids and polyps as well as rare cancers; assessment depends on symptoms, imaging, and sometimes tissue testing.

\medterm{Utero-Ovarian Ligament} A band of tissue connecting each ovary to the uterus. It helps hold the ovary in position within the pelvis and lies near the fallopian tube and other pelvic supporting tissues.

\synonyms
Ovarian Ligament; Proper Ovarian Ligament

\medterm{Uteroplacental Insufficiency} Reduced blood flow or function of the placenta that limits the supply of oxygen and nutrients to the fetus. It may contribute to poor fetal growth or signs of fetal distress; causes include maternal health conditions and problems with placental development.

\synonyms
Placental Insufficiency

\medterm{Uterosacral Ligament} One of two strong bands of tissue supporting the uterus and top of the vagina by attaching them toward the sacrum at the back of the pelvis. Weakness can contribute to pelvic-organ prolapse; endometriosis can also affect or cause pain in this area.

\medterm{Uterus} A hollow muscular organ in the pelvis between the bladder and rectum. Its inner lining thickens to prepare for pregnancy and is shed during menstruation. A fertilized egg normally implants in this lining, and a fetus develops in the uterus during pregnancy.
\commonname{Womb}

\medterm{Uterus Didelphys} A congenital condition in which two separate uteruses develop, often with two cervixes and a dividing wall in the vagina. Many people can become pregnant, but miscarriage, premature birth, abnormal fetal position, or other pregnancy complications are more common.

\synonyms
Double Uterus; Didelphic Uterus

\medterm{Uterus Disorder} A condition affecting the uterus, including fibroids, adenomyosis, endometriosis, infection, abnormal bleeding, congenital anomalies, prolapse, and cancer.

\medterm{Uterus Neoplasm} A benign or malignant growth arising in the uterus, including fibroids, endometrial cancer, and sarcomas; diagnosis depends on examination, imaging, and tissue sampling.

\medterm{UTI} Alternate name; see \textit{Urinary Tract Infection}.

\medterm{Utricle} A small balance organ in the inner ear that senses head position and movement in a straight line, such as forward, backward, or side-to-side motion.

\medterm{UV Light} Ultraviolet radiation, an invisible part of sunlight or artificial light that can affect the skin and eyes and may cause sunburn, aging,
or skin cancer.

\medterm{Uvea} The middle layer of the eye, made up of the iris, ciliary body, and choroid. It controls the amount of light entering the eye, helps focus vision, and supplies blood to eye tissues.

\medterm{Uveal Disease} A disorder of the iris, ciliary body, or choroid, including uveitis, tumors, vascular disease, and inflammation that may cause eye pain, photophobia, floaters, or visual loss.

\medterm{Uveal Melanoma} The most common primary cancer inside the adult eye. It begins in pigment-producing cells of the iris, ciliary body, or choroid and may cause visual changes or be found during an eye examination; it can spread to other organs.

\medterm{Uveal Neoplasm} A tumor arising in the uveal tract of the eye—the iris, ciliary body, or choroid; malignant melanoma may threaten vision and spread to distant organs.

\medterm{Uveitic Glaucoma} Glaucoma that develops in a person with uveitis, inflammation inside the eye. Inflammation, scarring, or steroid treatment can raise eye pressure and damage the optic nerve.
\synonyms
Inflammatory Glaucoma

\medterm{Uveitis} Inflammation of the uvea, the middle layer of the eye containing the iris, ciliary body, and choroid. It may also affect nearby eye tissues and may result from infection, immune-mediated disease, injury, or an unknown cause. Symptoms vary with the affected area and may include pain, redness, light sensitivity, floaters, or blurred vision.

\synonyms
Intraocular Inflammation

\medterm{Uveoparotid Fever} A form of sarcoidosis causing fever, swelling of both parotid glands near the ears, and inflammation in the front of the eyes. It may also cause facial-nerve weakness; treatment depends on the organs affected and severity.

\medterm{Uvula} A small, soft flap hanging from the back of the roof of the mouth. It helps with swallowing and speech; swelling can cause gagging, pain, snoring, or difficulty breathing.

\medterm{Uvulitis} Inflammation and swelling of the uvula, the small tissue hanging at the back of the throat. Infection, allergy, injury, dehydration, or irritation may cause it; severe swelling or breathing difficulty can threaten the airway.

\medterm{Uvulopalatopharyngoplasty} An operation that removes or reshapes parts of the soft palate, the hanging uvula, and the throat to widen the breathing passage. It may help selected people with obstructive sleep apnea, but can cause pain, swallowing changes, bleeding, or recurrence.
