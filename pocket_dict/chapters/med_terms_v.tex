VLAP

\medterm{Vitamin D} Vitamin D supports calcium and phosphate balance, bone mineralization, muscle function, and immune processes; deficiency causes rickets in children and osteomalacia or bone loss in adults.

Maximal oxygen consumption

\medterm{Volatile Oils} Plant oils containing compounds that evaporate readily at room temperature.

\medterm{VSD} Ventricular septal defect, a hole in the wall separating the heart’s ventricles.

\synonyms
Ventricular septal defect

\medterm{Vulnerary} A substance traditionally believed to promote wound healing.

\medterm{Vaccination} Administration of a vaccine to stimulate immune response and provide protection against
infectious disease. Types: live attenuated, inactivated, subunit, mRNA, viral vector.
Schedule varies by country. Herd immunity achieved when sufficient proportion immune.

\medterm{Vaccination Schedule} A time-based plan for administering vaccines according to age, previous doses, medical conditions, exposure risks, and public-health guidance. Schedules vary by country and are updated as recommendations change.

\medterm{Vaccine} A biological preparation that stimulates an immune response to a pathogen or its components. Vaccines
are used mainly to prevent infection or reduce disease severity; a limited number are used in therapeutic settings.

\medterm{Vaccine Immunology} The study of how vaccines stimulate innate and adaptive immune responses. Vaccine types
include live attenuated, inactivated, subunit, conjugate, toxoid, viral-vector, and nucleic-acid vaccines; their immune
responses, precautions, and schedules vary.

\medterm{VACTERL Association} A pattern of congenital abnormalities involving vertebral, anal, cardiac, tracheoesophageal, renal, and limb development. The
diagnosis is considered when several component abnormalities occur together and other
syndromes have been excluded.

\medterm{Vacuum Delivery} Assisted vaginal delivery using a vacuum extractor (cup applied to the fetal scalp with
negative pressure) to expedite delivery for prolonged second stage, fetal distress, or
maternal indications.

\medterm{Vacuum-Assisted Delivery} Operative vaginal delivery using a vacuum extractor applied to the fetal scalp. Types:
soft cup (Kiwi OmniCup, Mityvac, for outlet and low deliveries) and rigid cup (metal
Kiwi, for posterior positions and midpelvic deliveries).

\medterm{Vagina} The vagina is a muscular canal connecting the cervix to the external genital tract and serving roles in menstruation, sexual function, and childbirth.
cm long in adult females. Functions include receiving the penis during intercourse,
serving as the birth canal, and channeling menstrual flow.

\medterm{Vaginal Atresia} Congenital absence or complete obstruction of the vaginal canal. It may cause absent menstrual flow, cyclic pelvic pain, or difficulty with sexual activity and requires specialist evaluation.

\medterm{Vaginal Cancer} A rare gynecologic malignancy, most commonly squamous cell carcinoma linked to human
papillomavirus. Treatment depends on stage and includes surgery, radiation, and
chemotherapy.

\medterm{Vaginal Delivery (Spontaneous)} The spontaneous passage of the fetus through the birth canal without the use of
operative instruments (vacuum or forceps). Typically occurs in the second stage of labor
with maternal pushing.

\synonyms
Spontaneous

\medterm{Vaginal Dryness} Reduced vaginal lubrication, commonly associated with menopause, breastfeeding, hormonal
changes, medications, or irritation. It may cause discomfort, itching, painful
intercourse, or minor bleeding.

\medterm{Vaginal Dryness and Atrophy (Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause)} Vaginal dryness and atrophy, collectively termed genitourinary syndrome of menopause,
result from declining estrogen levels that cause thinning, drying, and loss of
elasticity of the vaginal tissues. Symptoms include vaginal dryness, irritation,
burning, dyspareunia, and urinary symptoms such as frequency and recurrent infections.

\synonyms
Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause

\medterm{Vaginal Foreign Body} An object retained in the vagina, most often a tampon or other inserted material. It may cause discharge, odor, bleeding, pain, or infection and should be removed safely with examination when necessary.

\medterm{Vaginal Intercourse} The insertion of the penis into the vagina for sexual pleasure or reproduction. The most
common form of sexual intercourse. Vaginal intercourse can lead to pregnancy if sperm
are deposited in the vagina.

\medterm{Vaginal Itching} Itching or irritation of the vulva or vagina caused by candidiasis, bacterial vaginosis, sexually transmitted infections,
dermatitis, hormonal changes, or other conditions. It may occur with discharge, odor, soreness, or burning;
recurrent or unexplained symptoms require examination and appropriate testing rather than self-diagnosis.

\medterm{Vaginal Microbiome} The community of microorganisms living in the vagina, commonly dominated by Lactobacillus species that help maintain an acidic environment. Changes in this community may contribute to bacterial vaginosis, candidiasis, or other symptoms.

\medterm{Vaginal Stenosis} Narrowing or shortening of the vaginal canal, which may result from surgery, radiation, scarring, inflammation, trauma, or congenital development. It can cause pain, difficult examination, or difficulty with penetration.

\medterm{Vaginal Trauma} Injury to the vaginal tissues caused by childbirth, sexual activity, foreign objects, procedures, or blunt or penetrating trauma. Bleeding, pain, swelling, or urinary symptoms require assessment; severe bleeding or suspected deep injury is urgent.

\medterm{Vaginal Hemorrhage} Heavy or clinically significant bleeding from the vagina. Causes include childbirth, pregnancy complications, trauma, infection, structural disease, hormonal disorders, and gynecologic malignancy; severe or pregnancy-related bleeding requires urgent evaluation.

\medterm{Vaginismus} Involuntary tightening of the muscles around the vaginal opening that can make penetration painful or impossible. It may be associated with pelvic-floor dysfunction, fear, prior pain, or other factors and is treatable with a tailored multidisciplinary approach.

\medterm{Vaginitis} Inflammation of the vaginal mucosa, one of the most common gynecologic complaints.

\medterm{Vaginospasm} Involuntary contraction of the pelvic-floor muscles surrounding the vagina, causing pain
or difficulty with vaginal penetration. It may be associated with fear of pain, prior
trauma, pelvic-floor dysfunction, infection, hormonal factors, or other genitopelvic
pain conditions.

\medterm{Vagus Nerve (Cranial Nerve X)} The tenth cranial nerve (CN X), the longest and most widely distributed cranial nerve in
the body. It contains sensory, motor, and parasympathetic fibers, exiting the skull via
the jugular foramen.

\synonyms
Cranial Nerve X

\medterm{Vagus Nerve Stimulation} A neuromodulation treatment involving electrical stimulation of the left vagus nerve via
an implanted device. It is FDA-approved for treatment-resistant depression and epilepsy.
It requires surgical implantation and delivers intermittent stimulation. Improvement may
take months to develop.

\medterm{Valinemia} A rare inherited disorder of branched-chain amino-acid metabolism causing elevated
plasma valine. It may present with developmental delay, feeding difficulty, hypotonia,
or neurological symptoms, although some individuals are minimally affected.

\medterm{Valley Fever} The common name for coccidioidomycosis, a fungal infection caused by Coccidioides
species and acquired by inhaling airborne spores from soil. It usually causes a mild
respiratory illness but can disseminate to skin, bone, joints, or meninges, especially
in people at higher risk.

\medterm{Valproate (Sodium Valproate)} Valproate is a broad-spectrum antiseizure medicine used for several generalized and focal seizure
disorders and for selected mood disorders. Choice requires consideration of age, pregnancy
potential, liver disease, interactions, and other safety risks.

\synonyms
Sodium Valproate

\medterm{Valsalva's Manoeuvre} Forced expiratory effort against a closed airway (glottis closed or mouth and nose
pinched shut), typically performed by bearing down as if straining during a bowel
movement. The manoeuvre has four phases: Phase I (onset of straining): increased
intrathoracic pressure compresses the aorta, causing a transient rise in blood pressure.

\medterm{Valvular Heart Disease} Valvular heart disease involves dysfunction of one or more heart valves, leading to
stenosis, regurgitation, or a combination of both. The most common causes are
degenerative calcification of aortic and mitral valves, rheumatic heart disease,
infective endocarditis, and ischemic papillary muscle dysfunction.

\medterm{Vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus} Enterococcus bacteria that are resistant to vancomycin, usually because of altered cell-wall precursors. VRE can
cause healthcare-associated urinary, bloodstream, wound, or other infections and may
require susceptibility-guided treatment.

\medterm{Variable Decelerations} Abrupt decreases in fetal heart rate that vary in timing, shape, and duration, commonly caused
by umbilical-cord compression. Their significance depends on frequency, duration, associated
baseline changes, variability, and the clinical setting.

\medterm{Variceal Hemorrhage} Life-threatening complication of portal hypertension involving rupture of dilated
esophageal or gastric varices. The risk is determined by variceal size, red color signs,
liver disease severity, and portal hypertension degree.

\medterm{Varicella} A primary infection with varicella-zoster virus that causes fever and a generalized itchy rash. See Chickenpox.

\medterm{Varicella Vaccine} A live attenuated vaccine that prevents or reduces the severity of chickenpox. The number and timing of doses
depend on age, vaccination history, immune status, pregnancy, and local recommendations.
It is generally avoided during pregnancy and in people with certain immune deficiencies.

\medterm{Varicella-zoster virus} A herpesvirus that causes chickenpox (varicella) and shingles (herpes zoster). VZV
establishes latent infections in dorsal root ganglia and can reactivate decades later.
Chickenpox is highly contagious and typically occurs in childhood. Reactivation as
shingles causes painful dermatomal vesicular rash.

\medterm{Varicocele} A varicocele is enlargement of veins in the scrotum, often on the left, that may cause aching, testicular growth differences, infertility, or no symptoms.

\medterm{Varicose Ulcer} Impaired venous return from the lower extremities due to venous valve incompetence or
obstruction. See chronic venous insufficiency.

\medterm{Varicose Veins} Dilated, tortuous superficial veins, most commonly in the lower extremities. Caused by
venous valve incompetence leading to venous reflux. Risk factors: family history,
prolonged standing, obesity, pregnancy, and older age. Symptoms: leg heaviness,
swelling, aching. Complications: skin changes, ulceration, thrombophlebitis.

\medterm{Variegate Porphyria} An inherited hepatic porphyria caused by reduced protoporphyrinogen oxidase activity. It
can cause acute neurovisceral attacks with abdominal pain and neuropathy and/or
photosensitive blistering skin disease.

\medterm{Variola} A highly contagious and deadly disease caused by variola virus, eradicated globally by
1980 through vaccination. See Smallpox.

\medterm{Vas Deferens} Muscular duct (approximately 45 cm long) transporting sperm from the epididymal tail to
the ejaculatory duct. It ascends through the spermatic cord, passes through the inguinal
canal, enters the pelvis, crosses over the ureter, and joins the seminal vesicle duct to
form the ejaculatory duct (in the prostate).

\medterm{Vasa Previa} Fetal blood vessels traverse the membranes over or near the internal cervical os without
protection from placental tissue or the umbilical cord. Rupture of membranes can cause
rapid fetal exsanguination, so antenatal diagnosis and planned cesarean delivery are
important.

\medterm{Vascular Access} A route into the bloodstream for treatment or monitoring. Hemodialysis access may be an
arteriovenous fistula, arteriovenous graft, or central venous catheter; the choice depends on
vascular anatomy, urgency, expected duration, and individual risks.

\medterm{Vascular Access for Hemodialysis} A surgically created or catheter-based route that permits high-flow blood removal and
return during hemodialysis. Arteriovenous fistulae are generally preferred when
feasible, followed by grafts; central venous catheters have higher infection and
thrombosis risks.

\medterm{Vascular Dementia} Cognitive impairment caused by cerebrovascular disease, such as strokes or chronic small-vessel
injury. Symptoms may develop suddenly or gradually and can include problems with
attention, executive function, memory, or walking. Management addresses the underlying
vascular risks and functional needs.

\medterm{Vascular Diagnostic Yield} The proportion of vascular diagnostic procedures or tests that provide a clinically
useful result.

\medterm{Vascular Graft} Synthetic or biologic conduit for vascular reconstruction. PTFE, Dacron, saphenous vein.

\medterm{Vascular Graft Patency} Continued openness and functional blood flow through a vascular graft or bypass. Patency
is monitored clinically and with duplex or other imaging because thrombosis, stenosis,
infection, intimal hyperplasia, and anastomotic problems can cause failure.

\medterm{Vascular Imaging} Techniques for visualizing blood vessels: duplex ultrasound, CT angiography, MR
angiography, conventional angiography.

\medterm{Vascular Malformation} Abnormal blood vessel development. Arteriovenous malformation, venous malformation,
lymphatic malformation.

\medterm{Vascular Ring} A congenital abnormality of the aortic arch or its branches that encircles and may
compress the trachea, esophagus, or both. Symptoms include stridor, wheezing, recurrent
respiratory infection, dysphagia, feeding difficulty, or respiratory distress.

\medterm{Vascular Trauma} Injury to an artery or vein from blunt, penetrating, iatrogenic, or crush mechanisms.
Assessment prioritizes hemorrhage control, distal perfusion, ischemia, expanding
hematoma, bruit, thrill, and associated nerve or bone injury.

\medterm{Vascularity} The amount and distribution of blood vessels supplying a tissue or lesion.

\medterm{Vasculitis} Inflammation of blood vessels causing tissue ischemia and necrosis. Classification by
vessel size. Large vessel: giant cell arteritis, Takayasu arteritis. Medium vessel:
polyarteritis nodosa (PAN), Kawasaki disease.

\medterm{Vasculitis (Systemic)} Inflammation of blood vessels that may involve large, medium, or small vessels and impair blood flow
or cause tissue injury. Examples include ANCA-associated vasculitis and IgA vasculitis.

\synonyms
Systemic

\medterm{Vasodilation} The widening of blood vessels during inflammation, resulting in increased blood flow to
the affected area. This process is mediated primarily by histamine released from mast
cell granules acting on H1 receptors, nitric oxide produced by endothelial nitric oxide
synthase, and prostaglandins particularly PGE2.

\medterm{Vasomotor Rhinitis} Nonallergic inflammation of the nasal lining causing congestion, runny nose, or sneezing, often
triggered by odors, temperature changes, or irritants.

\medterm{Vasopressin (Antidiuretic Hormone, ADH)} A hormone that increases water reabsorption in the kidney and can constrict
blood vessels. It is released from the posterior pituitary.

\synonyms
Antidiuretic Hormone, ADH

\medterm{Vasopressors in Anesthesia} Medicines that increase vascular tone or cardiac output to support blood pressure during
anesthesia or critical illness. The choice and dose depend on the cause of hypotension,
heart rate, cardiac function, and local protocol; monitoring is required.

\medterm{Vasovagal Syncope} Reflex syncope mediated by increased vagal tone and withdrawal of sympathetic activity,
causing bradycardia and/or vasodilation resulting in transient cerebral hypoperfusion.
Triggers: prolonged standing, heat, pain, emotional stress. Preceded by prodrome
(lightheadedness, nausea, diaphoresis, visual tunneling).

\medterm{Vaughan Williams Classification} The Vaughan Williams classification categorizes antiarrhythmic drugs into four classes
based on their primary mechanism of action.

\medterm{VEAL CHOP Mnemonic} A memory aid for interpreting fetal heart rate patterns. VEAL: Variable decelerations
Cord compression, Early decelerations Head compression, Accelerations OK (fetal
well-being), Late decelerations Placental insufficiency.

\medterm{Vector} A living carrier, commonly an arthropod, that transmits an infectious agent between
hosts. Biological vectors support replication or development of the agent; mechanical
vectors transfer it without such biologic change.

\medterm{Vector Control} Reduction of vector populations. Examples: insecticides, larvicides, environmental
modification, personal protection.

\medterm{Vector-Borne Disease} Disease transmitted by vector (insect, tick). Examples: malaria, dengue, Lyme disease.

\medterm{Vector-borne diseases} Diseases transmitted by arthropod vectors including mosquitoes, ticks, fleas, and
sandflies. Major vector-borne diseases include malaria, dengue, Zika, chikungunya, Lyme
disease, and leishmaniasis. Prevention through vector control, personal protection, and
chemoprophylaxis is essential.

\medterm{Vegetation} A vegetation is a mass composed of platelets, fibrin, and microorganisms that forms on a
heart valve or endocardial surface in the setting of infective endocarditis. The
vegetation typically attaches to the valve leaflet on the low-pressure side and may
progressively destroy the underlying valve tissue, causing valvular regurgitation.

\medterm{Vein} A blood vessel that carries deoxygenated blood toward the heart (except pulmonary veins,
which carry oxygenated blood). Veins have thinner walls and larger lumens than arteries,
with less smooth muscle and elastic tissue.

\medterm{Vein-graft aneurysms} Focal dilation of a venous bypass graft, most commonly a saphenous vein graft used in
coronary artery bypass surgery. Rare but may cause graft thrombosis, distal
embolization, compression of adjacent structures, or rupture. Diagnosis: coronary
angiography or CT angiography. Treatment: re-intervention or medical management.

\medterm{Vena Cava} The two largest systemic veins returning deoxygenated blood to the right atrium.
Superior Vena Cava (SVC): formed by brachiocephalic veins, drains head, neck, upper
limbs, thorax (no valves). Inferior Vena Cava (IVC): formed by common iliac veins at L5,
drains abdomen, pelvis, lower limbs (receives hepatic, renal, gonadal, lumbar veins).

\medterm{Venereal Diseases} Sexually transmitted infections are infections passed from one person to another through
sexual contact, including vaginal, anal, and oral sex. See Sexually Transmitted
Infections (STIs).

\medterm{Venesection (Phlebotomy)} The controlled removal of blood from a vein for therapeutic or diagnostic purposes.

\synonyms
Phlebotomy

\medterm{Venography} Imaging of veins, usually after contrast material is administered, to assess venous anatomy, obstruction, thrombosis, or malformation.

\medterm{Venom} A biological poison produced by a microorganism, plant, or animal that damages host
tissues at low concentrations. See toxin.

\medterm{Venomous Bite} A bite that injects venom from an animal such as a snake, spider, or arthropod. Effects range from local
pain and swelling to bleeding, paralysis, or systemic toxicity.

\medterm{Venous Insufficiency} Impaired venous return from the lower extremities due to venous valve incompetence or
obstruction. See chronic venous insufficiency.

\medterm{Venous Stasis Ulcer} A chronic leg wound caused by venous hypertension and chronic venous insufficiency. It
often occurs near the ankle and may be accompanied by edema, varicose veins, skin
darkening, and lipodermatosclerosis.

\medterm{Venous Thromboembolism in Pregnancy} Pregnancy increases the risk of VTE (DVT and PE) by 5-10 fold due to a hypercoagulable
state (increased fibrinogen, factors VII-XII, vWF, decreased protein S, and venous
stasis from the gravid uterus).

\medterm{Venous Thromboembolism Prophylaxis} Measures used to reduce perioperative deep-vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism,
including early mobilization, mechanical compression, and pharmacologic anticoagulation
when bleeding risk permits.

\medterm{Venous Thrombosis} The formation of a blood clot (thrombus) within a vein, most commonly in the deep veins
of the lower extremities (deep vein thrombosis, DVT).

\medterm{Venous Ulcer} A chronic wound caused by impaired venous drainage, usually occurring near the ankle in a limb with chronic venous insufficiency.

\medterm{Ventilation-Perfusion Scan} Lung imaging assessing ventilation and perfusion. Mismatch indicates pulmonary embolism.
Alternative to CTPA in renal impairment or contrast allergy.

\medterm{Ventilation-Perfusion Mismatch} Unequal matching of air reaching the alveoli and blood reaching the pulmonary
capillaries. Low or high regional ratios can impair oxygen exchange and occur in conditions such as COPD, pneumonia, or
pulmonary embolism.

\medterm{Ventilation-Perfusion Ratio} The relationship between alveolar ventilation and pulmonary capillary perfusion.
Regional mismatch is a major cause of impaired oxygenation; low V/Q produces venous
admixture, while high V/Q reflects wasted ventilation.

\medterm{Ventilation/Perfusion Scan} Pulmonary embolism diagnosis. Ventilation: inhaled Tc-99m DTPA aerosol. Perfusion:
injected Tc-99m MAA. Mismatch (perfusion defect with preserved ventilation) suggests PE.
High single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) sensitivity.

\medterm{Ventilator} Mechanical device providing ventilatory support. Modes: assist-control, SIMV, CPAP,
PRVC. Parameters: tidal volume, respiratory rate, FiO2, PEEP.

\medterm{Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia} Pneumonia developing after at least 48 hours of mechanical ventilation. Diagnosis is
based on clinical findings and respiratory evaluation; cultures may help identify the organism
but are not interpreted in isolation.

\medterm{Ventilator-Induced Lung Injury} Lung injury caused or amplified by mechanical ventilation, including volutrauma,
barotrauma, atelectrauma, and biotrauma. Lung-protective ventilation limits tidal volume
and excessive airway pressures while maintaining appropriate oxygenation and
carbon-dioxide targets.

\medterm{Ventral} Relating to the belly or front side of the body; synonym for anterior in human anatomy.

\medterm{Ventricle} One of the two lower, thicker-walled chambers of the heart that pump blood out of the
heart. The right ventricle pumps deoxygenated blood into the pulmonary artery
(low-pressure system); the left ventricle pumps oxygenated blood into the aorta
(high-pressure system, generating systemic blood pressure).

\medterm{Ventricle (Brain)} The interconnected system of fluid-filled cavities within the brain producing and
containing cerebrospinal fluid: two lateral ventricles (cerebrum), third ventricle
(diencephalon), and fourth ventricle (pons/medulla, continuous with the central canal).

\synonyms
Brain

\medterm{Ventricular Arrhythmia} An abnormal heart rhythm arising from the ventricles. It may cause palpitations, dizziness, fainting,
chest discomfort, or sudden cardiac arrest; fainting or severe symptoms require emergency care.

\medterm{Ventricular Assist Device} A mechanical pump supporting circulation in advanced heart failure. Types include
left-, right-, and biventricular devices. Uses may include temporary support, a bridge to
transplantation, or long-term therapy; complications include bleeding, infection, stroke, and
pump thrombosis.

\medterm{Ventricular Fibrillation} Chaotic electrical activity in ventricles, causing no cardiac output. Immediate
defibrillation is treatment. Causes: coronary artery disease, cardiomyopathy,
electrolyte disturbances, drug toxicity. Post-resuscitation care: targeted temperature
management, coronary angiography, ICU admission.

\medterm{Ventricular Septal Defect} An opening in the wall between the ventricles that can allow blood to flow from the
left ventricle to the right. The effects depend on the size and location of the defect and
may range from no symptoms to heart failure or pulmonary vascular disease.

\medterm{Ventricular Tachycardia} A wide-complex tachycardia (QRS >0.12 s) originating from the ventricles, with a rate of
100-250 bpm. Monomorphic VT has uniform QRS complexes; polymorphic VT has varying QRS
morphology. VT is life-threatening and may degenerate to ventricular fibrillation.

\medterm{Ventriculoatrial} Relating to or connecting a ventricle of the brain or heart with an atrium; often
describing a cerebrospinal-fluid shunt to the right atrium.

\medterm{Ventriculography} Imaging ventricular function. Radionuclide ventriculography (MUGA scan) accurately
measures ejection fraction. Cardiac MRI also accurate.

\medterm{Ventriculoperitoneal Shunt} A surgically implanted system that drains cerebrospinal fluid from a brain ventricle into the abdominal cavity, where the fluid
can be absorbed.

\medterm{Venturi Mask} Precise FiO2 delivery. Color-coded adapters.

\medterm{Venule} A small vessel that drains blood from capillary beds into veins. Postcapillary venules
are important sites for leukocyte migration and inflammatory fluid exchange.

\medterm{Vernal keratoconjunctivitis} A recurrent allergic inflammation of the conjunctiva and cornea, usually affecting children
or adolescents and causing itching, redness, and light sensitivity.

\medterm{Verruciform Xanthoma} A rare benign lesion characterized by a verrucous epithelial surface with lipid-laden
macrophages in the underlying connective tissue. It most often occurs in oral or genital
mucosa and may resemble a wart or malignant lesion clinically.

\medterm{Verrucous carcinoma} A low-grade, well-differentiated variant of squamous cell carcinoma with a warty,
exophytic appearance. It is locally invasive but rarely metastasizes, commonly affecting
the oral cavity, skin, or genitalia.

\medterm{Vertebra} The individual bone of the vertebral column (33 vertebrae: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5
lumbar, 5 sacral fused, 4 coccygeal). Parts: vertebral body, vertebral arch (pedicles,
laminae), spinous and transverse processes, articular processes/facets, and vertebral
foramen (spinal canal).

\medterm{Vertebral Canal} The bony canal formed by the vertebrae that encloses and protects the spinal cord.

\medterm{Vertebral Column} 33 vertebrae: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 fused sacral, 4 fused coccygeal.
Protects the spinal cord, supports the head/trunk, provides muscle/rib attachment. Each
typical vertebra has a body, vertebral arch, and 7 processes. Intervertebral discs
(nucleus pulposus/annulus fibrosus) provide cushioning.

\medterm{Vertebrobasilar Insufficiency} A condition of reduced blood flow through the vertebral arteries or basilar artery to
the posterior circulation of the brain, including the brainstem, cerebellum, occipital
lobes, and thalami.

\medterm{Vertical Talus} A congenital rigid flatfoot deformity in which the talus is positioned vertically and
the midfoot is dorsiflexed. It may be isolated or associated with neuromuscular or
genetic syndromes and usually requires early orthopedic assessment and treatment.

\medterm{Vertigo} A false sensation of rotational or linear movement of the self or the environment, and
it is a symptom rather than a disease, requiring differentiation from dizziness,
disequilibrium, and presyncope.

\medterm{Vesical} Relating to bladder. Vesical carcinoma, vesical diverticulum, vesical neck.

\medterm{Vesicovaginal Fistula} An abnormal connection between the bladder and vagina that causes continuous or frequent leakage of urine through the vagina. It may follow childbirth injury, pelvic surgery, radiation, or advanced infection and usually requires specialist repair.

\medterm{Vesicle} A small (<1 cm) fluid-filled blister within the epidermis, containing clear fluid
(serum) or, if infected, pus. Causes: viral (herpes simplex, herpes zoster, varicella,
hand-foot-mouth disease), dermatitis (contact, dyshidrotic eczema), autoimmune
(pemphigus, bullous pemphigoid early), and friction burns.

\medterm{Vesicoureteral Reflux} Retrograde flow of urine from the bladder into the ureters and potentially the kidneys,
most commonly due to incompetent ureterovesical valve. Predisposes to pyelonephritis and
renal scarring. More common in children, presenting with recurrent febrile UTIs.
Diagnosis: voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG). Grading I-V.

\medterm{Vestibular Migraine} Episodic vestibular symptoms associated with migraine biology, with or without
simultaneous headache. Episodes may include vertigo, motion sensitivity, visual aura, or
sound sensitivity; diagnosis is clinical after exclusion of other vestibular and
neurologic causes.

\medterm{Vestibular Neuritis} Acute vestibular dysfunction due to inflammation of the vestibular nerve, typically
viral in origin. Presents with acute onset of vertigo, nausea, vomiting, and nystagmus
lasting days. Hearing is normal (unlike labyrinthitis). Diagnosis: clinical, HINTS exam
(Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) to differentiate from central causes.

\medterm{Vestibular papillomatosis} A normal anatomical variant of the female vulva characterised by multiple, soft,
finger-like or filiform papules on the labia minora and vestibule, often mistaken for
genital warts (condylomata acuminata).

\medterm{Vestibular Rehabilitation} Exercise-based therapy that improves balance, gaze stability, and adaptation in people with
vestibular disorders or dizziness.

\medterm{Vestibular Schwannoma} A benign, slow-growing tumor of the vestibular portion of the eighth cranial nerve. See Acoustic Neuroma.

\medterm{Vestibule} Central bony labyrinth between cochlea and semicircular canals, containing utricle
(horizontal linear acceleration and head tilt detection) and saccule (vertical linear
acceleration detection). Each has a macula with hair cells covered by gelatinous
membrane embedded with otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals).

\medterm{VEXAS Syndrome} VEXAS (Vacuoles, E1 ubiquitin-activating enzyme, X-linked, Autoinflammatory, Somatic)
syndrome is an adult-onset autoinflammatory disorder caused by somatic UBA1 mutations in
hematopoietic stem cells, affecting men >50.


\medterm{Vibrio cholerae} A gram-negative, curved rod that causes cholera, a severe diarrhoeal illness. The
organism produces cholera toxin, which causes profuse watery diarrhea (rice-water
stools). Transmission occurs through contaminated water and food. The infectious dose
varies depending on stomach acidity.

\medterm{Vibrio vulnificus infections} Infections caused by Vibrio vulnificus, a gram-negative bacterium found in warm seawater and raw
seafood. It can cause wound infection, bloodstream infection, or gastroenteritis, with
particularly severe disease in people with chronic liver disease or other risk factors.

\medterm{Video Laryngoscope} An airway device with a camera near the blade tip that displays the laryngeal view on a
monitor. It may improve visualization during tracheal intubation, particularly when
direct laryngoscopy is difficult, but does not eliminate aspiration or airway-trauma
risk.

\medterm{Video Laryngoscopy} Video laryngoscopy uses a camera-equipped blade to display an indirect view of the
glottis on a screen.

\medterm{Videofluoroscopic Swallow Study} Real-time X-ray imaging of swallowing. Detailed assessment of oropharyngeal phase.

\medterm{Videostroboscopy} A diagnostic technique that uses synchronized stroboscopic light to create a slow-motion view
of vocal-fold vibration. It assesses the mucosal wave, amplitude, symmetry, and glottic
closure.

\medterm{Villous atrophy} Flattening and loss of the normal small-intestinal villi, reducing absorptive surface
area and occurring in conditions such as coeliac disease.

\medterm{Viral assembly} The process of packaging newly synthesised viral genomes and proteins into complete
virions. Assembly typically occurs in the nucleus or cytoplasm, depending on the virus
type. The process involves self-assembly of capsid proteins around the viral genome.

\medterm{Viral attachment} The initial step in viral infection where viral surface proteins bind to specific
receptors on host cells. Attachment determines viral tropism and host range. The viral
attachment protein is called the ligand, and the host cell receptor is typically a
protein or glycan.

\medterm{Viral Conjunctivitis} The most common form of infectious conjunctivitis, caused predominantly by adenoviruses
(serotypes 3, 4, 7, 8, 19, and 37), and it is highly contagious, spreading through
direct contact with infected ocular secretions or contaminated surfaces.

\medterm{Viral culture} Growth of a virus in susceptible cell cultures to identify an infectious agent. It can be useful for
selected viruses, but molecular and antigen-based tests are often faster or more sensitive;
the appropriate method depends on the suspected infection and specimen.

\medterm{Viral cytopathic effects} Observable changes in host cells caused by viral infection. CPE include cell rounding,
detachment, syncytia formation (fusion of infected cells), inclusion bodies, and cell
lysis. CPE are used in viral diagnosis and can be observed by light microscopy.

\medterm{Viral entry} The process by which viruses cross the host cell membrane to deliver their genome into
the cytoplasm. Entry occurs through membrane fusion (enveloped viruses) or endocytosis
(enveloped and non-enveloped viruses). For membrane fusion, viral fusion proteins
mediate merger of the viral envelope with the host cell membrane.

\medterm{Viral gastroenteritis} Inflammation of the stomach or intestines caused by a virus. Common symptoms include watery
diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and fever. Diagnosis is usually clinical; stool
testing is reserved for selected situations. Treatment focuses on fluid replacement and
symptom control.

\medterm{Viral hepatitis} Inflammation of the liver caused by viral infections. The major hepatitis viruses
include A, B, C, D, and E. Hepatitis A and E are transmitted through the fecal-oral
route and cause acute, self-limited infections.

\medterm{Viral immune evasion} Mechanisms by which viruses evade host immune responses. Strategies include antigenic
variation, inhibition of interferon signalling, downregulation of MHC molecules, and
encoding viral homologues of cytokines. Understanding viral immune evasion is essential
for developing effective vaccines and antiviral therapies.

\medterm{Viral Infections} Illnesses caused by viruses, which are intracellular pathogens that hijack host cellular
machinery to replicate. They range from self-limited conditions such as the common cold
and gastroenteritis to serious diseases including influenza, hepatitis, HIV, and
COVID-19.

\medterm{Viral latency} A state in which viral genomes persist in host cells without producing infectious
particles. Latent viruses can reactivate under certain conditions including
immunosuppression. Herpesviruses establish latent infections in specific cell types (HSV
in neurons, EBV in B cells, CMV in myeloid cells).

\medterm{Viral latency mechanisms} The strategies by which viruses establish and maintain latent infections. Herpesviruses
maintain their genomes as episomes in the nucleus. HIV integrates into the host genome
as a provirus. Latent viruses express minimal proteins to avoid immune detection.
Reactivation can be triggered by immunosuppression, stress, or other stimuli.

\medterm{Viral Load (HIV RNA)} The amount of HIV RNA in plasma, measured by a laboratory assay. It is used to assess
viral replication and response to antiretroviral therapy. An undetectable result indicates
that the amount is below the assay's reporting limit; testing intervals and treatment
decisions follow current clinical guidance.

HIV RNA

\medterm{Viral Meningitis} Inflammation of the meninges caused by viruses, usually Enteroviruses. Presents
similarly to bacterial meningitis but generally less severe. CSF shows lymphocytic
pleocytosis, normal glucose, and mildly elevated protein. Self-limited in most cases;
supportive treatment.

\medterm{Viral pathogenesis} The mechanisms by which viruses cause disease. Pathogenesis involves attachment, entry,
replication, and spread within the host. Viral damage can be direct (cytopathic effects)
or indirect (immune-mediated). Direct effects include cell death, altered cell function,
and cell transformation.

\medterm{Viral pneumonia} Viral pneumonia is infection and inflammation of lung tissue caused by respiratory viruses, producing cough, fever, breathlessness, hypoxemia, and variable severity.
or low oxygen in more severe cases.

\medterm{Viral release} The process by which newly assembled virions exit the host cell to infect other cells.
Release occurs through budding (enveloped viruses) or cell lysis (non-enveloped
viruses). Budding allows the virus to acquire an envelope from host cell membranes
without killing the cell.

\medterm{Viral replication} The process of synthesising new viral genomes and proteins within host cells. The
mechanism varies by virus type. DNA viruses typically replicate in the nucleus using
host cell DNA polymerases (except poxviruses). RNA viruses typically replicate in the
cytoplasm using viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase.

\medterm{Viral replication cycle} The series of steps by which viruses reproduce within host cells.

\medterm{Viral structure} The basic components of viral particles (virions). Viruses consist of a nucleic acid
genome (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein coat called a capsid. Some viruses have an
outer lipid envelope derived from host cell membranes. The capsid is composed of
structural units called capsomeres.

\medterm{Viral uncoating} The process by which the viral capsid is disassembled to release the viral genome into
the host cell cytoplasm. Uncoating occurs after entry and before replication. The
mechanism varies by virus type and can involve pH-dependent conformational changes,
proteolytic cleavage, or disassembly of capsid proteins.

\medterm{Virchow's Triad} Virchow's triad describes three broad categories of factors that contribute to
thrombosis: endothelial injury or dysfunction, stasis or turbulent blood flow, and
hypercoagulability. Endothelial injury exposes subendothelial collagen and tissue
factor, initiating platelet adhesion and the coagulation cascade.

\medterm{Viremia} The presence of virus in the bloodstream. Viremia may be transient or persistent and can
enable dissemination to target organs; it may be detected by culture, antigen testing,
or nucleic-acid amplification.

\medterm{Virilisation} The development of male secondary sexual characteristics in females, resulting from
excessive androgen production or exposure.

\medterm{Virilism Foetal} Prenatal or neonatal masculinization of a genetically female fetus or infant due to
excess androgen exposure. Causes include congenital adrenal hyperplasia, maternal
androgen-producing disease, or placental and fetal steroidogenic disorders.

\medterm{Virology} The branch of microbiology concerned with viruses: structure, classification (DNA/RNA,
enveloped/nonenveloped, Baltimore scheme), replication, pathogenesis, epidemiology,
diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. Diagnostic methods: cell culture, antigen
detection, nucleic acid amplification (PCR), and serology (IgM/IgG).

\medterm{Virulence} The relative capacity of a microorganism to cause disease or tissue injury in a host. It
depends on microbial factors such as toxins, adhesins, invasiveness, and immune evasion,
together with host susceptibility.

\medterm{Virulence Factor} A microbial product or characteristic that helps an organism colonize, evade host defenses, injure tissue,
or otherwise cause disease. Examples include adhesins, toxins, capsules, enzymes, and
mechanisms that alter immune recognition.

\medterm{Viscera} The internal organs of the body, particularly those within the thoracic and abdominal
cavities. The thoracic viscera include the heart, lungs, esophagus, trachea, and thymus.

\medterm{Vision} The sensory ability to perceive light and interpret visual information processed by the
eyes and brain, enabling recognition of shapes, colors, depth, and motion. Normal vision
depends on the proper function of the cornea, lens, retina, and optic nerve, as well as
intact neural pathways to the visual cortex.

\medterm{Vision and Hearing} The two primary sensory modalities through which humans perceive and interact with their
environment. Age-related declines in both senses are common, with presbyopia and
cataracts affecting vision and presbycusis affecting hearing, often beginning as early
as the fourth decade of life.

\medterm{Vision Impairment} Reduced visual acuity or visual-field function that affects daily activities. Causes include refractive
error, cataract, glaucoma, retinal disease, optic-nerve injury, and neurologic disease.
Evaluation and treatment depend on the cause; rehabilitation and visual aids may improve
function.

\medterm{Vision Loss} A decrease in the ability to see that cannot be fully corrected with eyeglasses, contact
lenses, or medical treatment. It ranges from partial impairment to complete blindness
and can result from conditions such as macular degeneration, glaucoma, diabetic
retinopathy, cataracts, and traumatic injury.

\medterm{Vision Problems} Changes in visual acuity, field, color, or clarity caused by refractive error, eye
disease, neurologic disease, or systemic illness. Sudden vision loss is an emergency.

\medterm{Vision Screening} Screening for visual problems in children: newborn—red reflex examination (cataract,
retinoblastoma); 3-5 years—visual acuity, strabismus screening (cover test,
photoscreening), and amblyopia risk factors.

\medterm{Visual Acuity} The resolving power of the eye, defined as the ability to distinguish two points as
distinct, and the most common clinical measure of visual function.

\medterm{Visual Agnosia} Inability to recognize or identify visually presented objects, faces, or symbols despite
adequate vision and attention. It results from dysfunction of higher-order visual
association cortex and may follow stroke, trauma, neurodegenerative disease, or other
brain injury.

\medterm{Visual Field Defect} Loss or alteration of a portion of the normally perceived visual field caused by
dysfunction of the retina, optic nerve, chiasm, optic tract, radiations, or visual
cortex. The pattern can help localize the lesion.

\medterm{Visual Field Perimetry} A systematic method of measuring the sensitivity of the retina across the entire visual
field using standardized light stimuli, providing a quantitative map of visual function
that can detect, characterize, and monitor visual field loss caused by diseases of the
retina, optic nerve, and visual pathways.

\medterm{Visual Impairment} Reduced vision that cannot be fully corrected by ordinary glasses or contact lenses. Causes include
refractive error, cataract, glaucoma, retinal disease, optic-nerve injury, and neurologic disease;
new or sudden loss of vision requires urgent assessment.

\medterm{Vital Signs} Basic measurements of physiologic status, commonly including temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, blood
pressure, and oxygen saturation. Values vary with age, activity, illness, and measurement
conditions; trends and clinical context are important.

\medterm{Vitamin A deficiency} A nutritional disorder resulting from inadequate intake or absorption of retinol,
causing xerophthalmia, night blindness, follicular hyperkeratosis, and impaired
immunity. Severe deficiency leads to corneal ulceration and blindness, and is a leading
cause of preventable childhood blindness worldwide.

\medterm{Vitamin A: Retinal} Vitamin A (retinol) is a fat-soluble vitamin essential for vision, immune function, cell

rhodopsin (in rods) and photopsins (in cones).

\medterm{Vitamin B Complex} A group of eight water-soluble vitamins that support energy metabolism, blood-cell production, nervous-system function,
and other biochemical processes. The group includes vitamins B1 through B7 and B9, as
well as vitamin B12.

\medterm{Vitamin B1 (Thiamine)} Water-soluble vitamin, coenzyme in carbohydrate metabolism. Deficiency: beriberi (wet,
dry), Wernicke-Korsakoff. Risk: alcoholism, bariatric surgery, dialysis. Treatment:
supplementation.

\medterm{Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin)} Required for nerve function, red blood cell formation, DNA synthesis. Deficiency:

\medterm{Vitamin B12 Deficiency} Megaloblastic anemia, neurological symptoms. Pernicious anemia, vegan diet. Treated with

\medterm{Vitamin B12 Deficiency in Older Adults} Deficiency caused by inadequate intake, malabsorption, autoimmune intrinsic-factor

\medterm{Vitamin B12: Cobalamin} A water-soluble vitamin needed for red-blood-cell formation, DNA synthesis, and normal nerve function. Deficiency can

\medterm{Vitamin B1: Thiamine} A water-soluble vitamin that acts as a cofactor in carbohydrate and amino-acid metabolism and supports nerve function. Severe

\medterm{Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin)} Precursor for FAD and FMN. Deficiency: cheilosis, glossitis, seborrheic dermatitis.

\medterm{Vitamin B3 (Niacin)} Precursor for NAD+ and NADP+. Deficiency: pellagra (diarrhea, dermatitis, dementia).

\medterm{Vitamin B3: Niacin} A water-soluble vitamin that forms the coenzymes NAD and NADP, which participate in many oxidation-reduction and energy-producing

\medterm{Vitamin B5 (Pantothenic Acid)} Component of coenzyme A. Deficiency rare. Involved in fatty acid metabolism.

\medterm{Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid)} A water-soluble vitamin and a powerful antioxidant, essential for collagen synthesis

\medterm{Vitamin E: Tocopherol} Vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol) is a fat-soluble vitamin and the body's primary

\medterm{Vestibule, vaginal} The space of the vulva between the labia minora that contains the external openings of the urethra and vagina and the ducts of the greater and lesser vestibular glands.

\medterm{Viable} Capable of surviving or developing under suitable conditions. In clinical contexts it may describe a viable pregnancy, tissue, organism, cell, or treatment option.

\medterm{Vincent angina} An acute necrotizing infection of the gums and oropharynx associated with fusobacteria and spirochetes. It causes painful ulceration, bleeding, foul breath, and gingival necrosis and requires dental or medical treatment.

\medterm{Vincent infection} Vincent infection is acute necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis caused by mixed oral bacteria, producing painful bleeding gums, ulcers, and foul breath.

\medterm{Vincent stomatitis} A painful necrotizing infection of the gums caused by mixed anaerobic bacteria, producing foul breath, bleeding, ulceration, and gray necrotic tissue; it is also called acute necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis.

\medterm{Viral infection} A viral infection occurs when a virus enters and replicates in host cells, causing symptoms that vary with the virus, tissue, immune response, and host.

\medterm{Virginal membrane} A historical term usually referring to the hymen, a variable rim or fold of mucosal tissue at the vaginal opening; its appearance cannot reliably establish sexual history.

\medterm{Virilize} To virilize is to develop or cause typically male physical changes from androgen excess or treatment, including facial hair, voice deepening, acne, muscle change, or genital enlargement.

\medterm{Viruses} Viruses are infectious particles containing DNA or RNA within a protein coat, sometimes with a lipid envelope; they replicate only inside host cells and cause disease through direct injury or immune responses.

\medterm{Visceral} Relating to the internal organs, especially those within the thorax or abdomen, rather than the body wall or limbs.

\medterm{Viscus} An internal organ, particularly an organ in the thoracic or abdominal cavity; the plural is viscera.

\medterm{Visual field} The entire area that can be seen while the eyes remain fixed in one position. Visual-field loss may result from retinal, optic-nerve, or brain lesions and is assessed by perimetry.

\medterm{Vital} Essential for life or relating to vital physiologic functions. Vital signs commonly include temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation.

\medterm{Vitamin A} A biological substance, cellular process, or molecular structure whose defining role is indicated by the term; its medical significance depends on function, location, amount, and clinical context.

\medterm{Vitamin B1} Vitamin B1 is a biological, genetic, biochemical, or chemical term used in medicine; its significance depends on its structure, function, concentration, pathway, or clinical context.

\medterm{Vitamin B12} Vitamin B12 is a biological, genetic, biochemical, or chemical term used in medicine; its significance depends on its structure, function, concentration, pathway, or clinical context.

\medterm{Vitamin B2} Vitamin B2 is a biological, genetic, biochemical, or chemical term used in medicine; its significance depends on its structure, function, concentration, pathway, or clinical context.

\medterm{Vitamin B6} Vitamin B6 is a biological, genetic, biochemical, or chemical term used in medicine; its significance depends on its structure, function, concentration, pathway, or clinical context.

\medterm{Vitamin D3} Vitamin D3 is a biological, genetic, biochemical, or chemical term used in medicine; its significance depends on its structure, function, concentration, pathway, or clinical context.

\medterm{Vitamin K} A biological substance, cellular process, or molecular structure whose defining role is indicated by the term; its medical significance depends on function, location, amount, and clinical context.

\medterm{Vitreous} Relating to the transparent gel filling most of the space between the lens and retina, or describing a glass-like appearance. Vitreous disorders can cause floaters, hemorrhage, detachment, or retinal traction.

\medterm{Voice box} The larynx, an organ in the neck containing the vocal folds and forming part of the airway. It produces voice and helps protect the lower respiratory tract during swallowing.

\medterm{Void} To empty the urinary bladder by urinating; in other medical contexts, void may mean an empty space or absence of a substance.

\medterm{Volar} Relating to the palm of the hand or sole of the foot, especially the surface opposite the dorsum.

\medterm{Voluntary} Under conscious control or performed by choice, such as voluntary movement. Voluntary action is distinguished from reflex, autonomic, or involuntary activity.

\medterm{Vomit} To eject stomach or upper gastrointestinal contents through the mouth; the expelled material may also be called vomitus. Persistent vomiting can cause dehydration and electrolyte abnormalities.

\medterm{Vomitus} Material expelled from the stomach or upper gastrointestinal tract during vomiting. Its color and contents can provide clues but do not by themselves establish a diagnosis.

\medterm{Voyeurism} A paraphilic disorder involving recurrent intense sexual arousal from observing an unsuspecting person who is naked, disrobing, or engaging in sexual activity, when it causes distress, impairment, or involves nonconsenting behavior.

\medterm{Vulvar} Relating to the vulva, the external female genital structures including the labia, clitoris, vestibule, and associated glands.

\medterm{Vulvitis, yeast} Inflammation of the vulva associated with vulvovaginal candidiasis, usually caused by Candida species. It may cause itching, burning, redness, swelling, and soreness and should be distinguished from other causes of vulvar symptoms.

\medterm{Venae Cavae} The superior and inferior venae cavae are the major veins returning systemic blood to the right atrium. The superior vena cava drains the head, neck, chest, and upper limbs; the inferior vena cava drains the abdomen, pelvis, and lower limbs.

\synonyms
venae cava; vena cava

\medterm{Vasa Deferentia} The paired muscular ducts that transport mature sperm from the epididymides toward the ejaculatory ducts during ejaculation.

\synonyms
vas deferens; ductus deferentes

\medterm{Vulva} The external female genital structures, including the mons pubis, labia, clitoris, and vestibule. It protects the openings of the urinary and reproductive tracts and contains specialized sensory and glandular tissue.

\synonyms
female external genitalia

\medterm{Vitreous Body} The transparent gel filling the large space between the lens and retina. It helps maintain the shape of the eye and transmits light; age-related liquefaction can cause floaters or contribute to retinal traction.

\synonyms
vitreous humor; vitreous

\medterm{Vitamin deficiency anemia} Anemia caused by inadequate vitamin B-12, folate, or other nutrients needed for normal red blood cell production.

\medterm{Viral hemorrhagic fevers} Severe viral infections that can cause fever, vascular injury, bleeding, shock, and organ failure.

\medterm{Vaginal bleeding after sex} Bleeding after vaginal intercourse; causes include cervical ectropion, infection, polyps, trauma, vaginal dryness, pregnancy-related conditions, or cancer. Persistent or recurrent bleeding needs evaluation.

\medterm{Voice disorders} Conditions affecting voice quality, pitch, loudness, or ease of speaking because of problems involving the vocal folds, airway, nerves, or voice use.

\medterm{Vibrio Infection} Infection caused by Vibrio bacteria, acquired through contaminated seafood or exposure of wounds to warm seawater.
It may cause watery diarrhea, wound infection, or rapidly progressive sepsis, especially in people with liver disease
or impaired immunity. Severe wound pain, blistering, fever, or shock requires emergency treatment.

\synonyms
vibriosis.
\medterm{Volume Overload} Excess total body sodium and water, causing peripheral or pulmonary oedema; common causes include heart failure, kidney failure, liver disease, and excessive intravenous fluid, and treatment depends on the cause and severity.
